# Fork Rights: Freedom Beyond Exit under Conditions of Structural Lock-In

**Working manuscript — not yet formatted for a specific journal**

**Author:** Han Qin  
**Affiliation:** Independent Researcher

## Abstract

Political philosophy has developed rich accounts of freedom as non-interference, autonomy, non-domination, voice, and exit (Berlin 1969). Yet these accounts do not fully capture a recurring form of institutional unfreedom: an agent may be legally free to leave and may even possess an affordable alternative, while remaining unable to carry forward the goods, standing, relationships, and practical capacities accumulated along the existing path. Exit is then formally available only on the condition of discontinuity. This paper develops the concept of **Fork Rights** to identify the missing freedom. To fork is neither to remain and reform an institution nor simply to leave it. It is to preserve a normatively adequate share of a common or accumulated past while developing it in an independently chosen direction. Fork Rights protect this capacity for continuity-preserving divergence. The paper distinguishes forking from exit, voice, and ordinary option freedom; proposes four dimensions for assessing whether a fork is substantive—availability, accessibility, continuity at bearable cost, and intelligibility—and explains why structural lock-in can make formally voluntary participation unfree. It then derives a graduated account of institutional duties, applies it to employment, digital platforms, and education, and answers objections concerning property, excessive institutional burdens, fragmentation, and the alleged reducibility of forking to well-resourced exit. Fork Rights do not require that every departure be painless or that every asset be portable. They require that institutions not convert an agent's accumulated dependence into a veto over that agent's future.

**Keywords:** freedom, exit, autonomy, non-domination, structural lock-in, portability, institutional design

## 1. Introduction

Suppose an employee is legally free to resign. Nothing prevents her from taking another job, and the labor market contains positions for which she is qualified. Yet departure would mean losing health coverage during a medically vulnerable period, abandoning unvested benefits, accepting that years of internally certified work will not be recognized elsewhere, and complying with restrictions that prevent her from using much of what she has learned in the same field. She can leave, but only by surrendering a substantial part of the life she has built.

Or consider a user of a dominant digital platform. She is entitled to close her account and can afford to use a competing service. But her social graph, reputation, creative archive, transaction history, and the practical identity she has accumulated over a decade cannot travel with her. She is free to leave only in the thin sense that no one will stop her from walking away empty-handed.

These cases do not show that exit is valueless. The right to leave associations is indispensable, and recent work has rightly emphasized that exit must be substantive rather than merely formal. Nor do the cases show only that agents need more options. In both, an alternative exists. What is missing is a particular relation between the agent's past and her alternatives: she cannot continue from what she has already made, earned, learned, or become. The institution makes departure conditional on practical discontinuity.

This paper calls the freedom at stake **the freedom to fork** and the claims protecting it **Fork Rights**. An earlier systems-oriented work introduced Fork Rights as the capacity to change direction, path, identity, or affiliation at bearable cost (Qin 2026). That initial formulation did not establish the concept's independent normative basis or distinguish it adequately from the growing literature on substantive and fair exit. The present argument reconstructs it as a standalone account of institutional freedom.

The term is borrowed from open-source software. In that setting, to fork is to copy an existing codebase and develop it in another direction. A fork differs from abandonment because it preserves the work already done; it differs from internal reform because the new branch is not governed by the old project's final authority. The analogy cannot be transferred mechanically from code to human institutions. Persons are not repositories, social goods are not all copyable, and the rights of third parties constrain what anyone may carry away. But the structural distinction is illuminating. Between submission and abandonment lies another possibility: continuity without continued subordination.

The central claim is:

> **Fork Rights protect an agent's substantive capacity to depart from an institutionally structured path while carrying forward a normatively adequate portable stake in the goods and capacities accumulated along that path.**

The adjective “normatively adequate” matters. Fork Rights do not imply that departing employees own their employers' trade secrets, that platform users may export other people's private data, or that students may dictate which credentials another institution must award. Nor do they guarantee success after divergence. They instead protect against an institution's turning accumulated dependence into leverage over a person's future. The fundamental wrong is not that change has costs. It is that the institution can make continued conformity the price of retaining the practical basis from which meaningful change could occur.

The argument proceeds in six stages. Section 2 locates the gap among accounts of exit, autonomy, and non-domination. Section 3 defines a fork and distinguishes it from exit, voice, and fresh entry. Section 4 explains the normative basis of Fork Rights in temporally extended agency. Section 5 offers four dimensions for institutional assessment. Section 6 applies the account to employment, digital platforms, and education. Section 7 answers four objections. Section 8 concludes by presenting Fork Rights as a limited but general requirement of institutional freedom.

## 2. The Gap in Existing Accounts of Freedom

### 2.1 Exit and its costs

Albert Hirschman's classic distinction between exit and voice describes two responses to organizational decline: members may leave, or they may attempt to change the organization from within (Hirschman 1970). The framework remains powerful because the credible possibility of exit can discipline institutional authority and strengthen voice. Robert Taylor develops this insight within republican theory, arguing that suitably resourced exit from abusive relationships can protect freedom as non-domination (Taylor 2017). The mere legal permission to leave is insufficient; agents need information, resources, and accessible alternatives.

The literature on freedom of association likewise recognizes that an exit right can be hollow when leaving exposes a person to deprivation, exclusion, or severe social loss (Shachar 2001). Barbara Fried emphasizes that exit always occurs against a background of social attachments and costs rather than from an abstract starting point (Fried 2003). Élise Rouméas's account of a right to a fair exit explicitly distinguishes excessive from undue costs and argues that a just institutional order must sometimes regulate not only whether exit is possible but how its burdens are distributed (Rouméas 2023). These arguments already rule out a crude equation of formal permission with freedom.

Fork Rights therefore should not be defended by claiming that theories of exit ignore cost. Many do not. The gap lies elsewhere. Exit is conceptually organized around dissociation: the agent ends a relationship, leaves an organization, or moves to an alternative. Even a fair exit asks primarily what the parties may demand, retain, or owe when an association ends. Forking identifies a different object of concern: whether an agent can make a new branch from an accumulated history rather than choose between continued membership and a practical reset.

This difference is clearest where the value at stake is not a detachable asset distributed at the moment of exit. A worker's standing may consist in a record intelligible only inside one firm. A platform user's reputation may exist only through the platform's proprietary architecture. A student's learning may be real although another institution refuses to recognize the credits through which it is documented. In each case, the problem is not adequately captured by asking whether the person may leave or whether the exit settlement is fair. The question is whether institutional design permits accumulated agency to remain usable after divergence.

### 2.2 Autonomy and adequate options

Theories of autonomy appear to offer a broader vocabulary. Joseph Raz argues that autonomy requires an adequate range of valuable options, alongside appropriate mental capacities and independence (Raz 1986). Capability approaches likewise direct attention away from formal liberties toward what people are actually able to do and be (Sen 1999; Nussbaum 2011). Relational accounts further show how oppressive social relations can shape the capacities through which choices are formed and exercised (Mackenzie and Stoljar 2000).

Fork Rights share these substantive commitments, but they isolate a problem that an option-set analysis can miss. Two agents may face the same set of present alternatives while differing radically in their freedom because one may carry her past into a new option and the other must forfeit it. An option set is a snapshot. Lock-in is diachronic: it is produced by the relation between past investment and future movement.

Consider two qualified workers offered the same outside job. One can transfer her pension, use a portable professional credential, retain lawful access to her work portfolio, and continue treatment through portable health insurance. The other loses all four. At the moment of decision, both have the same nominal option—take the new job—but not the same freedom to take it. The difference concerns continuity, not the number of alternatives.

This point does not refute option-based or capability accounts. Fork Rights can be understood as a specification within them. Their contribution is diagnostic: institutions often manufacture unfreedom not by removing every alternative, but by making the agent's accumulated life unusable anywhere else.

### 2.3 Non-domination and structural dependence

Republican theories define freedom not merely as the absence of actual interference but as independence from uncontrolled or arbitrary power (Pettit 1997; Lovett 2010). This captures a central feature of lock-in. An employer or platform need not threaten anyone explicitly. If it controls goods that the agent cannot carry elsewhere, it possesses leverage that can induce compliance even when no command is issued.

Recent work on structural domination in labor markets sharpens the point. Merely having reasonable alternatives may be insufficient when the background organization of production leaves workers dependent in systematic ways (Bryan 2023). Elizabeth Anderson's account of workplace “private government” similarly challenges the thought that the ability to resign by itself makes managerial authority non-dominating (Anderson 2017).

Fork Rights belong naturally beside non-domination, but they are not identical to it. Non-domination supplies a general diagnosis of dependence on uncontrolled power. Fork Rights identify one recurrent institutional remedy: preserve the practical possibility that a governed person can continue from a shared past under a different authority. A person may remain vulnerable to domination despite possessing some fork options, and domination can arise in contexts where forking is not the relevant remedy. Conversely, a restriction on forking may be wrongful even without a single dominating agent—for example, when incompatible credentialing systems collectively force students to repeat completed study. The concept is narrower in its object but potentially wider in its institutional application.

## 3. What It Means to Fork

### 3.1 Four responses to disagreement or constraint

The conceptual space can be clarified by distinguishing four responses to dissatisfaction:

| Response | Relation to the existing institution | Relation to accumulated goods | Authority over the future path |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Remains within it | Preserved within it | Existing authority remains, though it may be contested |
| **Exit** | Leaves it | May be surrendered or settled | Another authority or no continuing project |
| **Fresh entry** | Begins elsewhere | Starts substantially anew | New authority |
| **Fork** | Diverges from it | Carries forward an adequate portable stake | Independent authority over the divergent branch |

Voice seeks to alter the trunk. Exit severs the relationship. Fresh entry begins without institutional continuity. A fork preserves something from the trunk while rejecting its exclusive control over future development.

The “portable stake” in this definition varies by domain. It may include vested benefits, personal contributions, recognized skills, credentials, personal data, a lawful portfolio, an interoperable social identity, access to public infrastructure, or permission to reuse common materials. It need not consist of property owned in the ordinary legal sense. What matters is whether the good is part of the practical basis on which the agent's agency has developed and whether its retention can be justified against competing claims.

### 3.2 A formal definition

Let an agent, *A*, participate in an institutionally structured path, *P*, over time. Through participation, *A* accumulates a set of agency-relevant goods, *G*: skills, relationships, records, entitlements, identity-conferring roles, contributions, and other practical capacities. A fork option exists when *A* can initiate or join a path, *P′*, such that:

1. *P′* is meaningfully independent of the authority governing *P*;
2. *P′* departs from *P* in a direction material to *A*'s purposes;
3. *A* may carry from *G* a normatively adequate portable stake, *G′*; and
4. the transition does not predictably impose costs that destroy *A*'s capacity to function as an agent in the relevant domain.

The fork need not be individual. Some goods—an organization, a community, a technical standard—can be continued only collectively. Fork Rights may therefore protect the opportunity to join others in forming an alternative, not a fantasy that one person can reproduce a social institution alone.

Nor must the new branch remain similar to the old one. The requirement of continuity concerns the usable past, not fidelity to the old institution's purpose. A credential may support a new occupation; exported data may be used in a differently organized network; a worker's accumulated capital may support a cooperative rather than a rival conventional firm.

### 3.3 Forking is not copying everything

The software metaphor has a limit. Code can often be copied without depriving the original project of its copy. Houses, equipment, organizational opportunities, confidential information, and the attention of other persons are rivalrous or protected by independent rights. A general social right to fork cannot imply unrestricted duplication.

The correct claim is instead that an agent is presumptively entitled to an **adequate portable stake**, not to the entire institutional inheritance. Adequacy depends on at least four considerations:

- the agent's contribution to or legitimate reliance upon the good;
- the importance of the good to continued agency;
- the rights and privacy of other parties; and
- the cost and feasibility of making the good portable.

This makes Fork Rights context-sensitive without making them empty. A platform can deny the export of another person's private messages while still permitting export of the user's own posts, contacts subject to consent, transaction history, and reputation in a verifiable form. An employer can protect trade secrets while recognizing external credentials, vested benefits, and non-confidential work. A university can retain control over its degree while providing interoperable transcripts and recognizing demonstrably equivalent study.

## 4. The Normative Basis: Temporally Extended Agency

Why should institutions have duties to preserve the possibility of forking? The answer begins with a familiar but underemphasized feature of agency: persons act across time. They do not choose each morning from a clean slate. Their capacities and options are partly constituted by earlier work, attachment, learning, contribution, and reliance.

This temporal structure produces a vulnerability. The more an institution mediates the goods through which an agent's projects are carried forward, the more it can make the value of the agent's past conditional on continued conformity. Past investment then becomes a mechanism of future control. Economists describe related phenomena through switching costs and lock-in; the normative issue is what such mechanisms do to a person's authorship of her life.

Three premises support Fork Rights.

**First, valuable agency includes revision.** Autonomy is not exhausted by choosing an initial path. People must be able to reconsider commitments, respond to new reasons, and develop purposes not foreseeable when they entered an institution. Consent at time *t1* cannot by itself legitimate arrangements that disable meaningful revision at *t2*.

**Second, revision normally depends on continuity.** Radical reinvention is sometimes valuable, but an institutional order should not require self-erasure as the standard price of change. Skills, relationships, memory, reputation, and material security are not external decorations of agency. They are among its conditions. When a person can revise only by abandoning them, the permission to revise may be formally real yet substantively unusable.

**Third, institutionally created dependence generates responsibility.** Not every obstacle to change is an injustice, and no institution owes everyone a frictionless transition. Duties become stronger, however, when an institution has invited durable reliance, benefited from the agent's investment, designed goods to be non-portable, or acquired gatekeeping control over a socially necessary domain. The party that creates or profits from lock-in has a stronger duty not to use it as leverage.

These premises yield a principle:

> **The Anti-Hostage Principle:** An institution should not intentionally or avoidably make an agent's accumulated basis of agency hostage to continued conformity when a less restrictive, rights-compatible form of portability is reasonably available.

Fork Rights institutionalize this principle. They do not arise because every person owns every product of participation. They arise because the terms on which a person's past remains usable affect whether future participation is genuinely voluntary.

This also explains why forking is more than a welfare interest. Compensation can sometimes repair loss, but payment does not always restore agency. A person deprived of professional standing, a community, a public voice, or years of recognized learning may receive money while still losing the practical continuity needed to choose a direction. The protected interest is not simply the value of what is lost; it is the agent's authority to carry a life forward.

## 5. When Is a Fork Right Substantive?

A formal permission to fork can be as hollow as a formal permission to exit. Four dimensions help assess whether a fork option is substantive.

### 5.1 Availability

A divergent path must be institutionally possible. Laws, licenses, contracts, standards, and organizational rules may either permit alternatives or eliminate them. Availability does not require that the institution itself build every alternative. It requires at minimum that it not unjustifiably prohibit their creation. Antitrust rules, freedom of association, limits on non-compete agreements, open licensing, and the legal possibility of forming cooperatives can all protect availability.

### 5.2 Accessibility

An available alternative must be one the agent can actually enter or initiate. Technical complexity, inaccessible procedures, discriminatory gatekeeping, geographic constraints, or collective-action barriers can render a nominal fork unusable. Accessibility is agent-relative: a process that is feasible for a large firm may be meaningless for an ordinary user.

Institutions need not neutralize every difference in talent or resources. But where a supposed safeguard is designed so that only exceptional actors can use it, its existence provides weak evidence of freedom for everyone else.

### 5.3 Continuity at bearable cost

The agent must be able to carry an adequate portable stake into the new path without costs that defeat the purpose of the freedom. This combines two questions often separated in discussions of exit: what may travel, and what the transition costs.

“Bearable” does not mean painless. A change of job, platform, school, or community inevitably involves effort, risk, and loss. A useful threshold is whether the transition predictably destroys the background capacities required for meaningful agency in the relevant domain. Costs become presumptively prohibitive when they threaten basic security, impose effectively irreversible loss, are grossly disproportionate to legitimate institutional interests, or selectively burden those with the least bargaining power.

Portability is central here. A low administrative fee cannot make a fork substantive if the person must abandon all accumulated standing. Conversely, significant short-term effort may be bearable when credentials, benefits, data, and relationships can continue.

### 5.4 Intelligibility

An alternative must be available as a thinkable and understandable path. This is the most delicate condition because public institutions should not dictate which ways of life people ought to imagine. The duty is not to manufacture desires or guarantee creativity. It is more limited: dominant institutions should not monopolize the language through which alternatives can be described, conceal the rules of transition, or suppress information necessary to understand divergent possibilities.

Transparency, education, exposure to institutional plurality, and protection for dissent can support intelligibility without prescribing an outcome. A fork that exists only in a technical manual no affected person can locate or interpret is not a robust safeguard.

These four dimensions are jointly relevant but not mechanically conjunctive. A minor accessibility defect does not automatically establish a rights violation. They operate as a diagnostic framework for determining whether the institution has preserved a meaningful capacity for divergence.

## 6. Institutional Applications

### 6.1 Employment

Employment illustrates why exit and fork should be distinguished. A worker with another job offer has an exit option. She has a stronger fork option when she can move without forfeiting the portable basis of her professional agency.

Relevant safeguards may include portable benefits; vesting rules that do not function as punitive retention devices; transparent and externally intelligible performance records; limits on non-compete clauses; recognition of general skills acquired at work; the ability to display non-confidential work in a portfolio; and legal room to form or join alternative enterprises. These measures do more than make resignation cheaper. They allow a worker to continue a career under a different authority.

The contrast also clarifies disagreement between market and workplace republicans. A competitive labor market can improve exit by multiplying employers, while workplace democracy can strengthen voice within a firm. Fork Rights ask an additional question: does either arrangement let workers carry accumulated agency across institutional boundaries? Competition among firms may coexist with non-portable benefits and firm-specific credentials. Workplace democracy may coexist with rules that make departure ruinous. Voice, exit, and fork are complementary rather than mutually exhaustive remedies.

The strongest form of workplace fork—forming a cooperative or rival enterprise using an inherited productive base—raises serious property questions. Fork Rights do not imply a unilateral right to seize capital. They support more modest but still significant institutions: access to capital on fair terms, freedom to organize, portable benefits and credentials, lawful use of general knowledge, and in some contexts negotiated employee buyouts. What agents may carry depends on contribution, reliance, and third-party rights. The point is to prevent ownership rules from converting all institutionally mediated learning and standing into the exclusive property of the incumbent.

### 6.2 Digital platforms

Digital platforms make the distinction especially visible because they often permit deletion while obstructing continuation. A user can close an account, yet the platform-specific form of her social and economic life may be impossible to reproduce elsewhere. Network effects deepen the problem: the value is partly constituted by other users, common standards, and accumulated interactions.

Data portability is therefore a fork-enabling institution, but a downloadable archive is not always enough. A static file may preserve memory without enabling functional continuation. Depending on the service, substantive forking may require interoperable formats, verifiable reputation, accessible APIs, contact portability subject to others' consent, and the ability to redirect an audience or transaction history without impersonation or privacy violations.

Open-source governance supplies the originating model. The right to fork code allows participants to preserve an inherited codebase while placing future development under another authority. Nyman and Lindman show how this possibility can restrain project leaders, protect against vendor lock-in, and sustain projects after abandonment (2013). Yet the same literature also shows that formal permission is insufficient: a fork may fail without maintainers, infrastructure, trademarks, or a community. The social theory of Fork Rights generalizes both lessons. Legal permission matters, but freedom depends on the surrounding capacity to make divergence viable.

Platform duties should be proportionate to gatekeeping power and user reliance. A small forum does not owe the same interoperability infrastructure as a dominant platform around which users have organized livelihoods. The more an institution solicits deep investment while retaining unilateral control over portability, the stronger the case for positive fork-enabling duties.

### 6.3 Education and credentials

Education provides a less obvious example. A student may be free to leave a university and enroll elsewhere, yet lose most completed credits because the new institution cannot or will not recognize them. Formally, she has exited and re-entered. Practically, she has been required to restart.

Credit-transfer rules, modular credentials, transparent learning records, and recognition of demonstrated competence can protect educational forking. Such measures need not abolish academic judgment. Institutions may reject work that is genuinely non-equivalent, and they retain legitimate authority over their degrees. The relevant demand is that decisions be connected to educational substance rather than used to manufacture captivity.

Forking also protects changes of direction within education. A student who moves from one field to another should not necessarily retain every credit, but neither should all prior learning become institutionally invisible. The idea of a portable stake focuses the inquiry: which achievements remain relevant to the agent's continued educational agency, and what would it cost the receiving institution to recognize them?

Across these domains, Fork Rights do not specify a single policy. They identify a normative target against which portability, interoperability, transition support, and institutional pluralism can be evaluated.

## 7. Objections

### 7.1 “Fork Rights are just well-resourced exit rights”

The most important objection is reductionist. Once exit rights include low costs, fair settlements, adequate alternatives, information, and transition resources, what remains for Fork Rights to add?

The answer is continuity under independent authority. Exit can be entirely affordable and procedurally fair while requiring the agent to abandon the institutional form of an accumulated good. Imagine that a platform refunds every subscription fee, deletes the account promptly, and supplies enough money to join a competitor, but refuses any usable export of the user's own archive or reputation. The exit may be cheap and fairly administered. It is still not a fork.

One could broaden “exit” until it includes portability and continuity. Nothing prevents that terminological choice. But doing so would obscure a useful distinction between dissociation and divergent continuation. Rights concepts earn their place not by possessing metaphysically unique objects but by making recurring wrongs and remedies visible. Fork Rights direct attention to which parts of an accumulated past remain usable under a new authority—an inquiry not naturally foregrounded by the binary grammar of staying and leaving.

### 7.2 “The right violates property and associational autonomy”

A departing member's claim to continuity may conflict with an institution's property, privacy, expressive association, or organizational autonomy. If every dissenter could copy every asset or compel continued cooperation, forking would destroy the rights it purports to protect.

This objection defeats an unlimited right, not the account defended here. Fork Rights are *pro tanto* claims to a normatively adequate portable stake. They are constrained by others' rights and by the distinction between personal, jointly produced, and genuinely proprietary goods. The strength of the claim rises with the agent's contribution and reliance, the good's importance to continued agency, the institution's role in creating dependence, and the feasibility of portability. It falls with serious privacy, safety, expressive, and property interests on the other side.

The practical question is comparative: could the institution protect its legitimate interests through a less restrictive arrangement? Trade-secret law may be justified; a sweeping non-compete that prevents use of general expertise may not be. Privacy limits on contact export may be justified; refusing an interoperable export to consenting contacts may not be. Academic standards may justify denying credit; opaque blanket non-recognition may not.

### 7.3 “Forkability imposes excessive costs and fragments institutions”

Fork-enabling infrastructure can be expensive. It may duplicate effort, reduce network benefits, complicate administration, and encourage fragmentation. Open-source forks themselves sometimes divide communities and exhaust maintainers. Stable institutions also generate genuine goods that constant divergence can undermine.

Fork Rights do not require maximum forkability. They require safeguards proportionate to lock-in and institutional power. Several limiting principles follow:

1. **Materiality:** the divergence must concern an important exercise of agency, not every preference.
2. **Feasibility:** the demanded safeguard must be technically and institutionally possible at reasonable cost.
3. **Proportionality:** burdens on the institution and third parties must be weighed against the severity of lock-in.
4. **Non-destruction:** a fork claim does not include a right to disable the original branch or compel others to join the new one.
5. **Graduation:** duties range from non-obstruction and disclosure to interoperability and transition support; not every institution bears the strongest duties.

Moreover, the possibility of forking can promote rather than destroy stability. A credible fork option disciplines authority, makes continued participation less captive, and permits experimentation without forcing the entire institution to change at once. The aim is not permanent schism but non-captive cooperation.

### 7.4 “Imaginability is paternalistic and impossible to guarantee”

No institution can guarantee that a person imagines every valuable alternative. Attempts to shape imagination may themselves become manipulative. Why include intelligibility at all?

Because institutions can suppress or monopolize practical knowledge without directly prohibiting action. An exit process hidden behind obscure rules, a career path represented as the only respectable future, or a platform that conceals interoperable alternatives can preserve formal options while weakening an agent's ability to recognize them. The appropriate duty is negative and procedural before it is positive: do not conceal, misrepresent, or punish the articulation of alternatives; provide intelligible information where the institution controls it; protect space for dissent and comparison. This supports reflection without dictating what the agent should choose.

## 8. Conclusion

Political philosophy often describes freedom through a choice between voice and exit: remain and contest, or leave. Contemporary institutions reveal a third possibility that deserves separate recognition. A person may need to depart from an authority without discarding the practical past through which her agency has been formed.

Fork Rights protect this possibility. To fork is to preserve a normatively adequate share of an accumulated history while placing its future development under independent direction. The concept does not replace exit, autonomy, capability, or non-domination. It connects them around a distinctive temporal problem: structural lock-in converts yesterday's investment into today's obedience by making tomorrow possible only on the incumbent's terms.

The resulting right is neither absolute nor costless. It does not license appropriation, eliminate all transition burdens, or require every institution to reproduce itself for every dissenter. It generates graduated duties—non-obstruction, transparency, portability, interoperability, and sometimes transition support—whose strength depends on contribution, reliance, gatekeeping power, and feasibility.

The core demand can be stated simply. Institutions may ask people to contribute to a common path. They may not, without adequate justification, make the value of everything contributed, learned, or built on that path conditional on never choosing another one. Freedom requires more than an open door. Sometimes it requires the ability to leave with enough of one's life intact to begin a branch.
