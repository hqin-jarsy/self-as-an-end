The Layered Composition of Dream of the Red Chamber: Evidence and Theories

Abstract

This is the second topical paper in a series on an interpretive reconstruction of the last thirty chapters of Dream of the Red Chamber. It settles an account left open in §3.4 of the overview: a full register of the evidence for the novel's layered composition, together with a survey of the theories about it. The paper accepts the layered-composition thesis: the surviving first eighty chapters are not a single-layer text written in one pass. It depends on one layer only: the main body of the text of the surviving first eighty chapters went through layering, and the one who integrated it into a book was the Cao Xueqin named in the chain of titles in chapter 1 and in the Zhiyan Zhai commentary. "Cao Xueqin" is taken here as a role in the text; the paper takes no part in the debate over who he was historically. Other layers are recorded separately: the commentators' change of title and their order to delete a passage; the unfinished, damaged and patched end of chapter 22; the supplying of missing chapters; and the changes made in the Cheng–Gao edition. The paper offers no new research on the history of the novel's composition. It does three things. First, it registers by provenance the material in the project's base text that bears on composition: the chain of titles and the general principles (fanli); the commentary's own accounts of deleted drafts, lost drafts and unfinished passages; inconsistencies between earlier and later parts of the text; the form of the manuscripts and the dates recorded in them; and records outside the novel. Second, it reports the three scholarly lines on how the book was composed, lists separately the dispute over whether the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts or the Cheng editions came first, and then reports popular theories. For each it gives only the claim, the reasons offered and what remains unproven, without judging which is right or whether the materials are authentic. Third, it discloses the layering conjectures the project used in its backstage (its internal working notes): a six-stage working chart, whose chain of hand-offs turned out on checking to have been assembled by the project itself; a "composition signature" inferred from two solar-term dates, withdrawn after recalculation failed to match; and the few points that entered the reconstruction, each with its source, use and present status.

Keywords　Dream of the Red Chamber; composition history; textual layering; Zhiyan Zhai commentary; chain of titles; survey of theories; interpretive reconstruction

1 Stance

First, where this paper stands. Each point below follows rulings the author has made over time.

The layered-composition thesis is accepted. Dream of the Red Chamber is not a single-layer text written in one pass. Chapter 1 itself says that the book changed its title several times and passed through the hands of several title-givers; the commentary records drafts that were deleted, lost or left unfinished. Section 3 registers these materials one by one.

The paper depends on one layer only. The main thesis of this paper, and of the whole series, depends on one thing only: the main body of the text of the surviving first eighty chapters went through layering, and the one who integrated it into a book and fixed its centre was the Cao Xueqin named in the chain of titles and in the commentary.

This sentence needs three qualifications.

  • "Cao Xueqin" is taken as a role in the text. It means the one named in the chain of titles and the commentary, who "read it over for ten years and revised it five times," and whom the notes also call Xueqin, Qinxi or Qin. Who this person was historically is a question on which each view has its reasons; the project has no new evidence, and so takes no part in that dispute.
  • "Main body," because not every character of the surviving first eighty chapters is from this one hand. Commentators changed the title and ordered a passage deleted; the end of chapter 22 is unfinished, damaged and patched, and this paper does not rule on who wrote the supplied text or on what it was based; chapters 64 and 67 were missing and were supplied; and the Cheng–Gao edition altered the first eighty chapters. Each of these is recorded as another layer and is not included here (§7).
  • "Fixed its centre" is the project's working judgement, which this paper does not argue. What the centre is belongs to the overview's interpretation; it is for the overview to argue and to test, and it is not brought back here as evidence for this layer.

No new research on the history of composition; the paper reports and does not judge. How many layers there were, and whose hand produced each: scholarly and popular theories alike are reported as they stand, each with its claims, its reasons and what remains unproven. The paper does not rule on who is right and takes no part in the dispute. Quotations, sources and chains of citation are nonetheless checked item by item; reporting is not copying.

The popular theories each have their reasons, and they have helped the project think. Section 6 sets out, item by item, what they helped with, divided into method and material. Wherever the project has used one, the paper says where, and whether it has since been withdrawn.

The commentary is testimony whose provenance must be kept. This is as in paper 1. When two notes disagree, it may be because two drafts differed; the paper records this only as a possibility, and does not use it to judge which note is wrong.

No part in disputes over authenticity. The project chooses to believe that the book is layered and existed in several drafts; a binary of genuine and forged does not help the reconstruction. Where the provenance of a material is disputed, as with the notes of the Jing manuscript, certain manuscripts or certain outside records, this paper registers it by its provenance as a material, together with the layer it claims, notes that it is disputed, and does not rule on its authenticity. To register a layer is not to confirm that the layer historically existed; where an inference about the history of composition is involved, the paper states which source it is conditional on. The paper can stay out of the dispute because it has no need to enter it: the one layer it depends on stands even without the commentary being genuine (§7).

The Guiyou manuscript is left to paper 3.

The table should not be read too strictly.

  • It classifies where materials come from. It is not a sequence of six stages already proven, and its rows are not mutually exclusive.
  • The row on merging says how a layer was formed; the other rows say whose hand it came from and what it went through. They are not on one axis. What was merged might be the author's own draft, as when 1.61 is read as an old work of Xueqin's; or someone else's, as Yurui records.
  • Stages of composition, layers of transmission and later re-creation must be marked separately. The existence of the last kind does not directly prove the particulars of the first. That today's edited texts carry the hands of modern editors proves that the material before us has an editorial layer; it cannot show how many authors the book had in the eighteenth century or before.
  • Paper 1's ground for non-adoption N6 (original lost or deleted by the author) and its entries "filed as a record" (kept without a ruling) mostly record layers of the loss-and-patching kind.

The project's reconstruction is also a layer, and a self-aware one. The overview calls it interpretive reconstruction, with every stroke marked for the layer it stands on. What it proves is the project's own addition, not the prehistory of the original.

3.1 The chain of titles and the general principles

Chapter 1 has a chain of titles:

From then on the Daoist Kongkong, from emptiness seeing form, from form giving rise to feeling, passing feeling into form, and from form awakening to emptiness, changed his name to the Passionate Monk, and changed the title from The Story of the Stone to The Record of the Passionate Monk. Then Wu Yufeng titled it Dream of the Red Chamber. Kong Meixi of Eastern Lu titled it A Mirror for the Romantic. Later, because Cao Xueqin, in the Mourning-the-Red Studio, read it over for ten years and revised it five times, compiled a table of contents and divided it into chapters, it was titled The Twelve Beauties of Jinling.

After an intervening title poem comes one more sentence:

When Zhiyan Zhai copied it and commented on it again in the jiaxu year, he went back to the title The Story of the Stone.

The general principles (fanli) of the Jiaxu manuscript (1.2) say: "This book has very many titles. Dream of the Red Chamber is the name that sums up the whole. It is also called A Mirror for the Romantic, as a warning against giving way rashly to romantic feeling. It is also called The Story of the Stone, being a self-likening: the things the stone recorded (自譬石头所记之事)."

This is the book's most direct account of how it was made. Every school has to explain "revised it five times," and there are broadly three readings.

  • The titles correspond to drafts. Chen Qinghao holds that of the five titles only three, A Mirror for the Romantic, The Story of the Stone and Dream of the Red Chamber, name drafts of different stages (second-hand). Shen Zhijun matches each layer to a title: the first draft was called A Mirror for the Romantic; once the story of the stone was strengthened it became The Story of the Stone; after further revision it may have been renamed The Record of the Passionate Monk; after the Prospect Garden was enlarged and the characters' ages lowered it became The Twelve Beauties of Jinling; and finally it was named Dream of the Red Chamber (second-hand). Li Qingyu reads the five titles as four stages in the development of the author's conception of fiction: a vehicle for admonition; a true record of events; a tale of wonders offered to the world; play and dream as one (reprint).
  • The titles correspond to two books. Du Chungeng, relying on the phrase "the name that sums up the whole" in the general principles, sets out a theory of two books combined (§4.2). Zhou Shaoliang, answering Lu Xun's view that the different titles were a flourish of shifting appearances (摇曳见态), holds that they have a factual basis and reflect a real history of compilation (reprint).
  • The chain of titles is the author's arrangement. The Jiaxu upper-margin note 1.62 says that the prologue (the "wedge," 楔子) was written by the author himself, and that "Xueqin read it over and revised it" is the author's "cunning" (式猾) (§3.2). On this reading, the narrative posture of the chain of titles cannot be taken, without interpretation, as a file recording the book person by person and stage by stage.

The chain of titles presents the book as a text that passed through several titles, through reading over and revising, and through a second commentary. The project takes this as a working understanding: the last title was given by the one who "read it over for ten years and revised it five times," and a commentator then changed the title back. That is the chain's own account, and the reading that it is the author's arrangement still stands alongside it. The sentence in which a commentator changes the title is recorded in this paper as a record of the commentators' layer.

There is one more layer to record. It has been said that the Wu Yufeng sentence and the sentence on the second commentary of the jiaxu year are both peculiar to the Jiaxu manuscript and absent from most others. This paper has not yet seen the collation notes themselves, nor checked manuscript by manuscript; this is to be checked. If it is so, the chain of titles itself has a layer between manuscripts, which can be read in two ways: added in the Jiaxu, or deleted in the others. The various attempts in §5 to match Wu Yufeng to a historical person rest on exactly this sentence.

What follows sets out, group by group, how scholars read these notes.

Old draft and title (1.61, 1.62).

1.61 is the watershed between the second and the third lines. Read as an old work of Xueqin's own, with a preface by his younger brother Tangcun, it is the basis of the line of two books (or two sources) combined. Wu Shih-ch'ang's theory of "Tangcun's short prefaces" comes from it: he held that some of the chapter-head general comments in the surviving Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts were originally short prefaces written by Tangcun for A Mirror for the Romantic, preserved by Zhiyan, in the words "and so I keep it" (according to Liang Yusheng's account of a poem Wu wrote on his own book: "Tangcun's short prefaces are plainly there; the Redology experts, alas, do not know"; chapter and page in Wu's book to be checked). Read as an old book by someone else that Xueqin obtained, it connects with Yurui's "Cao Xueqin obtained it" (§3.5), and belongs to the line of another's old draft. There are also two readings of what was "kept" (故仍因之): the title, or the preface.

1.62 is evidence pointing the other way, and what it points against must be stated precisely. The commentator asks: if Xueqin only read the book over and revised it, who wrote this prologue at the opening? So "Xueqin read it over and revised it" is a device the author played on purpose, and "readers must by no means be taken in by the author." The line that holds Cao Xueqin to be the original author cites this note. What it tells against is "Xueqin was only a reviser," and the reading that takes each link in the chain of titles literally as a different author. It does not tell against "the same author revised the book again and again": the commentator says that the prologue is from the author's hand, not that the book was never revised. Elsewhere in the same manuscript the commentary has 1.140, "I say that in writing this book Xueqin …," and 2.5, "naturally what Xueqin excelled at all his life," which can be read alongside it. As for how the third line answers this note, this paper has seen no specific alternative reading, and says so.

Deleted draft: the Tianxiang Tower (13.3, 13.8, 13.21, 13.51, 13.52, 13.87, 13.89, 13.90).

This is the alteration the commentary records in most detail. The Jiaxu chapter-end general comment 13.89 says that the two matters of the family's future which Qin Keqing entrusts to Xifeng command admiration, and so "I have pardoned her for now, and so ordered Qinxi to delete it"; the Jing 13.8 has the same text, with an added passage on "'the hairpin left behind' and 'changing clothes'" and "that is why this chapter has only ten pages." The Jiaxu upper-margin note 13.87 records the page count separately ("four or five pages are gone"), so that the page count of the deleted draft does not rest on the Jing manuscript alone. The Jiaxu 13.3 says that the Tianxiang Tower episode was suppressed because one "could not bear to set brush to it."

By the commentator's own account, the one who deleted it with the brush was Qinxi, and the one who ordered the deletion was the commentator.

The Jiaxu side note 13.51 stands under Ruizhu's "she too dashed herself against a pillar and died," and says that this "supplies undeleted text of the Tianxiang Tower"; the Jing side note 13.52 adds, "this too is a stroke left undeleted." In the commentators' own words, the deletion was incomplete, and traces of the layer remain in the text. Of what the commentators record about chapter 13, this is the most direct.

13.21 must be read manuscript by manuscript. Within one bracketed passage the base text records three manuscripts. There is a Jiaxu upper-margin note, "nine characters tell the whole Tianxiang Tower affair: this is writing by not writing," with the base-text note "the Jing manuscript adds the signature 'Tangcun'," which refers to this note. There is a Gengchen upper-margin note of only four characters, "this note may be followed." And there is a Jing upper-margin note, which after "this note may be followed" goes on: "That the whole chapter deliberately hides how Keqing died is my great act of compassion. Alas, alas! Late spring, renwu. Hu Sou." In this place, this long form, with "my," "late spring, renwu" and "Hu Sou," is marked as the Jing manuscript only. There is also a Gengchen note, 13.90, which records a shorter text to the same effect: "That the whole chapter deliberately hides how Keqing died is a great act of the compassionate heart. Alas, alas! Spring, renwu." It has no "my," adds the word "heart" (心), is dated "spring, renwu," and is not signed "Hu Sou." The two notes are set side by side as separate entries, and neither the signature nor the date of one is transferred to the other.

The commentator gives the reason for the deletion as "pardon." The surviving text indeed lacks this episode, so Cao Xueqin did not disobey the order. Why Cao Xueqin himself went along with it is another question; the project has a conjecture, disclosed as it stands in §6.3.

Lost and unfinished (20.20, 26.24, 26.120, 26.153, 22.104, 22.106, 75.3).

Several notes record that drafts of the later part existed and were lost: 20.20, "lost by a borrower"; 26.24, a Jiaxu upper-margin note, on the long chapter at the Prison Temple, "lost, no manuscript"; 26.153, at the end of a Jiaxu chapter, on the writing about the archery ground, "lost, no manuscript"; while the Gengchen upper-margin note 26.120 says only "no manuscript." The notes signed with a dinghai date and a Jihu name (Jihu Sou or Hu Sou) are 20.20, 26.120, and the Gengchen version of 26.24; the Jiaxu 26.24 and 26.153 are unsigned. Cai Yijiang uses these notes to explain why what came after chapter 80 did not survive; Mei Jie says that Cao Xueqin had already "delivered the goods" to the small circle headed by Zhiyan, so that in this sense the book was finished, and its later part did not survive because it was lost in lending (both: reprint).

22.104 has two literal readings. The base text reads "from here on damaged and lost; what follows is a later supplement" (此后破失，系再补), meaning that what follows is supplied text; the common reading is "awaiting supplement" (俟再补), meaning that what follows was originally missing and awaits supplementing. Paper 1 filed the note as a record without a ruling; that is a way of handling it, not a third reading. In the base text the chapter continues after this note to its end, and at the end stands 22.106, "this chapter was not finished when Qin [Xueqin] died." These materials concern the unfinished, damaged and patched end of the chapter; this paper does not rule on who wrote the supplied text or on what it was based. This is one of the places outside the "main body" of §1. 75.3, "Mid-Autumn poem missing; awaiting Xueqin," is likewise the manuscript's own record of something unfinished.

Commentators and the brush (22.22 to 22.24, 1.63).

The Gengchen upper-margin note 22.22 speaks of "Xifeng choosing the plays and Zhiyan holding the brush"; 22.23 adds later, "now, in the summer of dinghai, only one decrepit thing is left"; the Jing upper-margin note 22.24 has the same text with one more sentence, "Qinxi, Zhiyan, Xingzhai: these gentlemen have all departed one after another."

Three points about this group.

  • "Zhiyan holding the brush" is not to be translated as co-authorship. Whether holding the brush means writing, copying, or noting down one occasion on which plays were chosen: each interpretation has its proponents, and what this paper has not checked is marked as not checked.
  • 22.23 and 22.24 are later notes following an earlier one. They can be used to discuss the order of the notes, but they are not two independent witnesses.
  • The group provides material on who held the brush and on later notes added to earlier ones. "The earlier note" does not exclude the same commentator adding to his own old note; this group alone does not settle how many commentators there were.

The Jiaxu upper-margin note 1.63, "On the last night of the renwu year, the book not yet finished, Qin [Xueqin] wept his tears dry and died," signed at the end "written in tears, eighth month of the jiawu year," is again a record of date and identity left by the commentators' layer.

Drafts of the later part, and two notes at odds (18.17, 18.19, 20.20).

20.20 contains the statement "I saw it only once, at the time of a fair copy" (余只见有一次誊清时): the commentator says plainly that he saw a draft of the later part. 18.19 says that only "at the end, with Jinghuan's Roster of Feeling, will one know" the names of the registers; it points to the design of the later part, without saying explicitly that the commentator had seen it himself. The material on the number of chapters has been tabulated in the overview, §3.5, and is not repeated here.

18.17 and 6.48 are an instance of what §1 calls two notes at odds, registered only as a possibility. The Gengchen double-line interlinear note 18.17 conjecturally assigns Ping'er to the Tertiary Register ("the rest are not much touched on; presumably they are … Ping'er and others, without doubt"), while the Jiaxu double-line interlinear note 6.48 says of Ping'er, "presumably she too is in the Secondary Register." Paper 1 takes the Secondary Register layer of 6.48: Rec. ch. 110 has been supplemented according to Addendum 003, so that Ping'er's page falls within the Secondary Register. In paper 1's words, it "records only which side was taken, and does not judge the other side wrong."

Division into chapters and collation (17.1, 75.2).

17.1, "this chapter would properly be divided into two," corresponds to the form of the Jimao and Gengchen manuscripts, in which chapters 17 and 18 are not divided, and is a record that the division into chapters was still being worked out. 75.2, "collated on the seventh day of the fifth month of the twenty-first year of Qianlong," is a record of the collation of a manuscript.

The Western Hall (28.82, 28.83).

The Gengchen upper-margin note 28.82: "Drinking from a great bowl: the day the Western Hall brought forth nine-stalk lingzhi. Commenting on the book up to here, how can I not grieve? The Double Ninth of renwu." The Jiaxu side note 28.83: "Who has lived through this? Alas, alas! The old affairs of the Western Hall." The "Western Hall" (西堂) is usually read as Cao Yin's study; this paper has not yet found who first proposed this, or where. Among the popular theories, some take the "Western Hall" to be You Tong, and some, on the strength of a note in a disputed manuscript, propose a "late elder brother of the Western Hall" (西堂故兄); the latter involves the Guiyou manuscript and is left to paper 3.

Two general rules.

  • The entries of paper 1 given above are the project's records of how it handles each note, not a historical authentication of the materials. They are set side by side here so that the reader can see how the same note is used in each of the two papers; the audit's labels are not to be taken as supporting evidence for the history of composition.
  • Several notes in the table are found only in the Jing manuscript (13.8; the "Tangcun" signature of 13.21 and the Jing upper-margin note of 13.21; 13.52; 22.24; for a shorter text with the same purport as the Jing note on 13.21, see the Gengchen 13.90), and in 13.3 missing characters are supplied from the Jing. The authenticity of the Jing manuscript is disputed: Gao Shuwei's A Refutation of the Jing Manuscript of Honglou meng (Zhonghua shuju, 2024) holds that it is a forgery; Mei Jie's "Another Look at the Jing Manuscript" (Honglou meng xuekan 2002, no. 1) holds that it is genuine. Following §1, this paper takes no part in the dispute and registers these notes only by provenance. Paper 1 gives the Jing manuscript reference weight (参级), which is "the weight the project gives to each manuscript's notes; it is not a judgement of the truth of any note or of the worth of any manuscript."

3.3 Inconsistencies within the text

Within the first eighty chapters there is a set of places where earlier and later passages do not agree, which scholars use to argue for traces of revision or of merged drafts. In 1979 Dai Bufan listed four kinds of internal evidence: Beijing speech mixed with the Wu dialect; the Jia mansion moved from south to north, and its garden from west to east; disorder in the story's chronology; and two Baoyus of different ages (from a summary in a history of scholarship; second-hand). Later discussions of layering cite these kinds again and again. For each kind this paper gives representative examples, first checked against the base text, then with their sources and the counter-arguments.

A few of these examples need more comment.

Caiyun and Caixia. The maid who is on good terms with Jia Huan is Caixia in chapter 25 of the base text ("only Caixia still got on with him"), Caiyun in chapters 30, 60 to 62 and 70 (ch. 70: "because Caiyun had lately fallen out with Jia Huan"), and Caixia again in chapter 72 ("though in her heart she had an old attachment to Jia Huan"). These names have been checked against the base text. Why Caiyun and Caixia should be taken as earlier and later names of one character, rather than as two persons existing side by side, depends on each scholar's particular argument; this paper has not obtained that argument, and lists the case provisionally as a textual phenomenon. Whether variant readings in the manuscripts have regularized some of the instances remains to be checked manuscript by manuscript.

The clan list in chapter 13. After Qin Keqing's death, "Jia Dairu led Jia Chi, Jia Xiao, Jia Dun, Jia She, Jia Zheng, Jia Cong … Jia Lan, Jia Jun, Jia Zhi and others, who all came." The list includes even Jia She and Jia Zheng, and Jia Cong and Jia Lan are in it; the plainest reading is that these are the same characters appearing. Liu Shide holds that in the first draft Jia Cong was just an ordinary clansman, and became Jia Lian's younger brother only in the revised draft (second-hand). This paper has not yet read, in the original studies, why proponents take these to be two different persons; the case is recorded provisionally as "a doubt raised by a particular theory," and the list itself is not taken as established evidence of layering.

Dong'an and Dongping. In chapter 3, the inscription under the ebony couplet in the Hall of Glorious Felicity reads "respectfully written by your fellow townsman and junior in family friendship, Mu Shi, hereditary Prince of Dong'an" (同乡世教弟勋袭东安郡王穆莳拜手书). "Dong'an" appears only here in the whole book; among the four princes of chapters 11 and 14 it is the Prince of Dongping, and Mu Shi's name is not given. That the two titles coexist is registered here only as a configuration of princely titles awaiting comparison; whether the manuscripts agree remains to be collated manuscript by manuscript, and the paper does not assume in advance that one man's title was changed. The project's backstage (its internal working notes) once read this as a "trace of renaming" left by the Cao Xueqin layer; this paper has not found who in scholarship first raised the point (§6, Appendix 3).

Thirteen years. In chapter 25 the monk says, "since we parted at Greensickness Peak, in the blink of an eye thirteen years have passed," which scholars take to disagree with Baoyu's age elsewhere. Eileen Chang's account of Baoyu's age being lowered from revision to revision is the most often cited explanation of such inconsistencies (reprint). This paper has checked only the chapter 25 sentence; why it counts as a disagreement depends on the chronological links each scholar adopts, and it is listed provisionally as a textual phenomenon.

Of the above, those checked against the base text are Caiyun and Caixia, the chapter 13 list, the "thirteen years" of chapter 25, and the examples from chapter 3 and chapters 11 and 14. The others will be checked against the base text case by case before the next version. Where the earliest proponent cannot be found, the paper says "not found"; finding the earliest proponent is not a condition for an inconsistency to hold.

3.4 The form of the manuscripts, and the dates recorded in them

On the form of the manuscripts, this paper goes only as far as kinds, and does not examine each case:

  • Division into chapters: in the Jimao and Gengchen manuscripts chapters 17 and 18 are not divided; note 17.1 says, "this chapter would properly be divided into two."
  • Missing and supplied chapters: the Gengchen lacks chapters 64 and 67, which later manuscripts supply (as commonly recorded). Scholars differ on the presence and source of these two chapters in each manuscript; the paper does not judge.
  • Unfinished: the end of chapter 22 (22.104, 22.106).
  • Variant chapter titles: the chapter titles differ among the manuscripts; to be listed manuscript by manuscript.
  • Dates in the notes: the winter of jimao, renwu, yiyou, dinghai, jiawu. Yiyou appears only once, in the Gengchen upper-margin note 25.61: "Winter of yiyou, by the snowy window. Jihu Laoren."
  • Record of collation: 75.2, "collated on the seventh day of the fifth month of the twenty-first year of Qianlong," a bingzi year (1756).

Dates must be distinguished by what they date. "Renwu," "dinghai," "copied and commented on again in the jiaxu year," and the collation of the twenty-first year of Qianlong are first of all dates and acts recorded in the materials. To read them as the years in which the surviving manuscripts were copied, or in which some original draft was finalized, needs a further layer of evidence. This paper does not date the manuscripts here.

3.5 Records outside the book

Several records outside the book bear on its making. For each, the paper states from whose writing it actually quotes.

Mingyi, "Twenty Poems on Dream of the Red Chamber" (题红楼梦), with a short preface. The preface begins: "Master Cao Xueqin brought out a copy of the Dream of the Red Chamber he had written, recording in full the splendour of romance and luxury …" (as quoted in Eileen Chang, Nightmare in the Red Chamber, in the essay "Er xiang Honglou meng"; online text at Nunu Shufang, checked; to be checked again against Honglou meng juan, compiled by Yi Su). The copy Mingyi saw does not fully agree with the present text. On this basis Wu Shih-ch'ang proposed his "first draft" theory: the first draft of Cao Xueqin's Dream of the Red Chamber that Mingyi saw had "the story entirely within the Prospect Garden," and Wu concludes that "Xueqin was the author of Dream of the Red Chamber, and also the one who worked it over after the materials recorded by the stone had been combined with Dream of the Red Chamber" ("On the First Draft of Honglou meng Seen by Mingyi," Honglou meng xuekan 1980, no. 1; reprint).

Yurui, in Zaochuang xianbi (枣窗闲笔), records: "There was an old book, A Mirror for the Romantic, also called The Story of the Stone, by whose hand no one knows. Cao Xueqin obtained it, and because what it recounted was broadly the same as the history of his own family, he took it as a vehicle for his own purposes, and deleted and altered it as many as five times." He also records that the manuscripts he saw "every one had the notes of his uncle Zhiyan." This paper follows the quotation by Zhou Jianyu from the 1957 facsimile published by Wenxue guji kanxingshe (pp. 9 and 21); a second-hand quotation elsewhere reads "his uncle Zhiyan Zhai"; the two versions stand side by side, to be checked against the facsimile. This is the earliest documentary basis for the theory that the book was another's old draft, reworked by Cao. Liu Mengxi holds that Yurui's records rest mostly on hearsay and are not necessarily reliable (second-hand). The authenticity of Zaochuang xianbi itself is the subject of separate discussion: Gao Shuwei's book of 2024, for example, has a chapter on the authenticity of Yurui's Zaochuang xianbi; this paper has seen only the book's table of contents, and its conclusion is to be checked against the book.

The Chengjia edition (1791), preface by Cheng Weiyuan: "Accounts of the author differ, and in the end no one knows from whose hand it came; only, the book itself records that Mr. Cao Xueqin deleted and altered it several times." (Common transcription; this paper has not seen a transcription it can name, and the page of the Chengjia edition itself is to be checked.) The preface also describes where the last forty chapters came from: "Over several years I had gathered only some twenty-odd juan. One day, by chance, I obtained ten-odd juan from a street pedlar's load (鼓担)." (As quoted in Hu Shi, "A Textual Study of Honglou meng," reprint at Aisixiang; checked.) The foreword to the Chengyi edition (1792): this paper has not seen the page; to be checked. These sentences of Cheng's preface are the Cheng lineage's record of the "reading over and revising," corresponding to the chain of titles.

Duncheng, in "Sent to Cao Xueqin, Thinking of Him" (寄怀曹雪芹), writes "better to write books in the Village of Yellow Leaves" (不如著书黄叶村), a line often cited as evidence that Cao Xueqin wrote a book, though it names none; in "Lament for Cao Xueqin" (挽曹雪芹), the phrase "the 'ox-demon' writings left behind" (牛鬼遗文) has been read by some as evidence of drafts left behind (source to be checked; not yet seen by this paper). In the poems of Dunmin and Zhang Yiquan, this paper has found no line that directly mentions the writing of a book. The pages in the original collections are all to be checked.

The above reports only the records and how scholars have read them. Where the provenance of a historical source is disputed, the dispute is noted, and no ruling is made on authenticity.

4 Scholarly Theories of Composition

This paper presents the scholarly theories of composition in three lines, and lists separately the dispute over whether the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts or the Cheng editions came first. Scholars are grouped below by the main claims of the works cited. The whole of a scholar's work is not assigned to a single camp, and different stages or different questions in one person's work may be discussed in different places. Each scholar's claims, evidence, representative works, opponents and reliability are given in Appendix 1; the main text gives only the gist. The anchor for the history of scholarship is Du Zhijun's "Thirty Years of Redology in the New Period, 1978–2008" (Honglou meng xuekan 2009, no. 2).

4.1 One book, revised many times

This line holds that the book was produced by revising one and the same draft many times, and that traces of the old drafts are layered into the present text.

Hu Shi is the baseline. The first version of his "A Textual Study of Honglou meng" was published in May 1921 as the preface to the Yadong Library's punctuated edition of the novel, and was revised in November of the same year. He wrote that "the author of Honglou meng is Cao Xueqin" and that "the forty chapters after chapter 80 were supplied by Gao E" (reprint). He posits no layer of old drafts: the whole book has only two layers, Cao's eighty chapters and Gao's supplement of forty.

Zhou Ruchang, Feng Qiyong and Cai Yijiang, building on this, accept that "revised it five times" refers to Cao Xueqin's own repeated revisions, and that A Mirror for the Romantic was an earlier work of his. Feng Qiyong, comparing the format of the copying, the chapter titles and the commentary of the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts, argues that the Gengchen is "the most complete of the early manuscripts of The Story of the Stone made in Cao Xueqin's lifetime" (reprint). Cai Yijiang says that "the possibility is very great" that A Mirror for the Romantic "was the basis on which The Story of the Stone was later worked over and revised"; he opposes the theory of two books combined, on the ground that "a novel is not a theoretical essay; with their different characters, [two novels] are very hard, or one should say impossible, to combine" (reprint).

Eileen Chang reconstructed the layers of rewriting through close reading of the variants. She says that Cao Xueqin "was all along the one in charge of rewriting this book," that the first eighty chapters were rewritten over more than twenty years, that early versions from different periods had already circulated, and that the variants are the strata left by the rewriting; Baoyu's age was lowered from one revision to the next (reprint). She also held that The Story of the Stone and A Mirror for the Romantic were originally two books, and that the Land of Illusion entered the book only after A Mirror for the Romantic was taken in. She thus straddles the first two lines; she is placed here, under one book revised many times, according to where her main effort lay.

Those who divide stages by the titles. Chen Qinghao, Shen Zhijun and Li Qingyu read the chain of titles as stages of drafting or of the conception of fiction (§3.1). Liu Shide, from collating the manuscripts, discerns "two components, the first draft and the revised draft," and takes inconsistencies such as the two Jia Lans, the two Jia Congs, and Caiyun and Caixia as traces of two drafts lying one on the other (§3.3; second-hand).

Xia Wei takes A Mirror for the Romantic to be Cao Xueqin's first draft, which the author "sublimated and renewed on the basis of the first draft, A Mirror for the Romantic," and from the opposite journeys of the two Baoyus, Zhen and Jia, reads a dualist political outlook of taking office and retiring from the world ("From A Mirror for the Romantic to Dream of the Red Chamber: The Evolution of the Book's Composition and Creative Thought," Wenxue pinglun 2022, no. 6; reprint).

Zhu Danwen is placed in this line provisionally. Xia Wei's article opens by quoting p. 197 of Zhu Danwen's Honglou meng lunyuan (Jiangsu guji chubanshe, 1992): the various titles "name drafts of one and the same novel at different stages of composition"; there were not five drafts before jiaxu, and "the first two revisions were made on the first draft, because the drafts we can at present be sure really existed are only two" (Xia Wei's article read in a reprint; her quotation checked). This is a statement of the one-book view. Both sentences are thus known only second-hand, through Xia Wei; pp. 197 and 200 of the book itself have not been checked, and whether the book argues elsewhere for two books combined is to be checked. She also argues that the genealogy in the old draft differed from the present text, the original draft having made Grandmother Jia the wife of the Duke of Rongguo (cited by Hu Lianhao).

Counter-arguments. The line of two books combined holds that the one-book view cannot explain the difference in the writing of the Ning and Rong mansions (§4.2). Du Zhijun judges both the one-draft-many-revisions view and the two-books-merged view to be "still immature in some respects, while objective, systematic, detailed and in-depth micro-level demonstration is still lacking" (reprint).

4.2 Two books (two sources) combined

This line holds that two drafts, such as A Mirror for the Romantic and The Story of the Stone, were combined into one book by Cao Xueqin.

Zhou Shaoliang proposed early on that two drafts were joined. He holds that A Mirror for the Romantic was an old draft of Cao Xueqin's that really existed, and that the present text joins two first drafts, the original Story of the Stone and A Mirror for the Romantic. From the latter come a series of "romantic" stories, such as Xifeng and Jia Rui, Qin Keqing and the Tianxiang Tower, Jia Lian with You Erjie and with the Duo girl, Qin Zhong, and Xue Pan, whose theme turns on "a warning against giving way rashly to romantic feeling" ("'Xueqin formerly had a book called A Mirror for the Romantic'," Honglou meng xuekan 1979, no. 1; reprint).

Wu Shih-ch'ang has two theories. His English monograph of 1961 proposed the theory of "Tangcun's short prefaces" (§3.2). An article of 1980 proposed two sources combined. One source is the first draft of Cao Xueqin's Dream of the Red Chamber that Mingyi saw; the other is "the story of the Consort's visit home, what the stone recorded: The Story of the Stone, which Zhiyan commented on. The commentator of The Story of the Stone was also the author of The Story of the Stone. That author was not Cao Xueqin." Cao Xueqin was the one who combined the two and worked them over (reprint). In Honglou meng tanyuan waibian, p. 483, he has the sentence "it is not by Xueqin" (cited by Zhou Jianyu); what "it" refers to must be checked in the book.

Du Chungeng is the clearest proponent of two books combined. He holds that the present text was combined from two original drafts: one centred on the Rongguo mansion, which was The Story of the Stone, and one centred on the Ningguo mansion and Jia She's branch, which was A Mirror for the Romantic. Cao Xueqin made a "second creation," merging the two books and altering the relations between characters, "making the Dukes of Rong and Ning into full brothers." The evidence adduced includes the different standards for choosing wives in the two mansions; the difference in scale between Jia Jing's and Jia Zheng's birthdays; the scale of Qin Keqing's funeral; the wording "the Rongguo branch" and "the two mansions of Ning and Rong" in Leng Zixing's account of the family; and the phrase "the name that sums up the whole" in the general principles, together with 1.61 ("The Rong and Ning Mansions, Two Books," Honglou meng xuekan 1998, no. 3, reprint; also "On Cao Xueqin's 'Read It Over for Ten Years and Revised It Five Times'," same journal, 2007, no. 3, catalogue, second-hand).

Tan Dejing sorts earlier research into three kinds: many revisions, combination, and cut-and-paste. He holds that Dream of the Red Chamber is a selective integration of A Mirror for the Romantic, its theme shifting from a warning against lust to the expression of women's beauty and of a sense of sorrow (reprint).

Counter-arguments. Cai Yijiang holds that two novels with different characters "cannot be combined." He also criticized the theory of two books combined as "suspecting the neighbour of stealing the axe" (a proverb, from the story of the man who, having lost his axe, saw a thief in everything his neighbour's son did); the phrase is known from Du Chungeng's account, and Du replied that the same logic applies equally to the view of one draft revised many times (the original source of Cai's phrase has not been found).

4.3 Another's old draft, reworked by Cao

This line holds that the original author was someone else, and that Cao Xueqin was the one who revised and reworked the book.

Yurui's record is the earliest documentary basis for this line (§3.5).

Dai Bufan published "Unveiling the Mystery of the Author of Honglou meng" in Beifang luncong 1979, no. 1, subtitled "On how Cao Xueqin skilfully recast Brother Stone's old draft, A Mirror for the Romantic, and reworked it into the book" (second-hand); it was followed by "Brother Stone and Cao Xueqin" (same journal, no. 3) and "On Qin Keqing's Late Death" (first publication to be checked). He holds that the "Brother Stone" of the book was another person, whose old draft was A Mirror for the Romantic, and that Cao Xueqin was not the author in the sense of originator, only a reviser. His internal evidence is given in §3.3. On dialect he says: "its old draft was written by someone who could hardly shake off a Wu accent … while the revised (new) draft is the work of someone thoroughly versed in the Beijing dialect" (reprint).

Dai's theory drew nearly thirty rebuttals, and the articles of the debate were later collected as Collected Papers of the Debate on the Authorship of Honglou meng (compiled by the editorial office of Beifang luncong; Taiyuan: Shanxi renmin chubanshe, 1985). The rebuttals had three main points.

  • Chen Xizhong and Hou Zhongyi pointed out that Dai "confused dialect with dialect vocabulary" (as reported by Zhan Dan); Wu Shih-ch'ang and Zhang Jinchi also held that the method of using dialect to prove more than one author was unscientific.
  • Liu Mengxi wrote "Qin Keqing's Death and Cao Xueqin's Authorship: After Reading Comrade Dai Bufan's 'On Qin Keqing's Late Death'" (Wenyi yanjiu 1979, no. 4; catalogue, second-hand). He also argued that the festivals and the weather are "on the whole consistent throughout, following on from chapter to chapter," so that no conclusion of time running backwards can be drawn (reprint).
  • Zhang Bibo and Zou Jinxian rebutted Dai's inference that Brother Stone was Cao Zhucun.

The titles and issue numbers of the individual articles follow the table of contents of the Collected Papers, and remain to be checked one by one.

Pan Chonggui held that Dream of the Red Chamber "had another, anonymous original author; Cao Xueqin was only the one who held the brush in revising, supplementing and correcting it" (cited by Zhou Jianyu). His view comes from the nationalist strand of allegorical decoding (suoyin); this paper takes only his claim about composition.

Pi Shumin notes of his own work: "on the inference that The Story of the Stone had two authors, for details see my Honglou meng kaolun ji, pp. 37–39, 111–115" (reprint; Honglou meng kaolun ji, Taipei: Linking, 1984). His view is commonly summarized as: Zhiyan Zhai was Li Ding, and Li Ding took part in the early drafts of The Story of the Stone (second-hand). Whether he claims that Li Ding had a complete old draft and that Cao Xueqin was only a reviser, this paper cannot say, not having seen the book, and does not say so.

4.4 The dispute over whether the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts or the Cheng editions came first

Ouyang Jian argues that the Cheng editions came first and the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts later: the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts are only later copies, "Zhiyan Zhai" is "the collective name of a succession of forgers," and the Chengjia edition is the true text (second-hand). His series of articles began with "Doubts about the Theory of 'Two Great Textual Systems' of Honglou meng, with a Discussion of Zhiyan Zhai as a Forgery by Liu Quanfu" (Fudan xuebao 1991, no. 5); his monographs include Honglou xinbian (Huacheng chubanshe, 1994), Hongxue bianwei lun (Guizhou renmin chubanshe, 1996) and Huanyuan Zhiyan Zhai (Heilongjiang jiaoyu chubanshe, 2003).

Rebuttals came from Ying Bicheng, "On the Textual Systems of Honglou meng: A Discussion with Comrade Ouyang Jian" (Fudan xuebao 1992, no. 1), and from Chen Zhao, Cai Yijiang, Song Mouyang, Yang Guanghan, Feng Qiyong, Xu Naiwei and others (according to the survey by Lin Yi and Cai Zhengxing; second-hand). Du Zhijun's assessment is that this "exposure of forgery" "appears at present … hard to sustain," but that "the very rise of the doubts shows, from the other side, … the complexity of the relation between the Zhiyan Zhai and Cheng texts" (reprint).

This dispute does not lie on the same axis as the three lines above. The three lines dispute how the book was written; this dispute concerns the order of the materials. What it challenges is that part of the argument on composition which presupposes that the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts and their commentary are earlier than the Cheng editions. The three lines also cite other materials, such as the text of the chain of titles in the Cheng lineage, Cheng Weiyuan's preface, and the inconsistencies in the text, and so are not wholly affected by it. This paper records the theory and its rebuttals as they stand, and takes no part in the dispute over authenticity.

4.5 Scholarship in English

On studies of composition in English this paper adds only a sentence. Wu Shih-ch'ang's monograph of 1961, On The Red Chamber Dream: A Critical Study of Two Annotated Manuscripts of the XVIIIth Century (Oxford: Clarendon Press), is an early monograph dealing directly with composition; it was reviewed in the same year by P. D. Hanan in BSOAS and by C. T. Hsia in JAS. David Hawkes's introduction to the first volume of the Penguin The Story of the Stone (1973) discusses A Mirror for the Romantic and the rewriting; page numbers to be checked. Andrew H. Plaks's work is mainly on structure and allegory, and is not assigned to any line.

4.6 Where the three lines agree

The three lines dispute whether there was one book or two, and whether it was one and the same author throughout or an old draft by someone else. This paper does not decide between them.

Among the three kinds of claim set out in this paper that explicitly discuss the part played by Cao Xueqin's rewriting, the common ground is that they acknowledge his role in writing or reworking the first eighty chapters of the present text: the one-book view says he revised his own draft; the two-book view says he combined two drafts; the view of another's old draft says he reworked someone else's. Hu Shi is the baseline and posits no layer of old drafts, so he is not counted here. This summarizes only the intersection of the claims described, and does not use it to prove any one particular process of composition.

The one layer this paper depends on has three segments. The first two are that the main body of the text of the first eighty chapters went through layering, and that the one who integrated it into a book was the Cao Xueqin named in the text. They correspond to this common ground, and can also point to the text's own account in the chain of titles ("compiled a table of contents and divided it into chapters"); the project takes them as a working understanding, and only in the sense of a role in the text. The third segment, "fixed its centre," is the project's working judgement, which this paper does not argue, and it is not what the three lines agree on (§1, §7). The dispute over whether the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts or the Cheng editions came first challenges the order of the materials; the one layer this paper depends on also has something to go on in the Cheng lineage (§7).

5.2 The theories in brief

Each theory is given with its claim, its reasons and what remains unproven. The tone is neutral, and proponents are referred to only by the names under which they publish.

Xu Xinggong (Liao Yihu). The claim is that the author of The Story of the Stone was the late-Ming Fuzhou book collector Xu Bo (courtesy name Xinggong, 1570–1642). "Cao Xueqin" is a pen name whose three characters stand for Confucian, Buddhist and Daoist: Cao for Cao Xuequan, Xue for Xuefeng Temple in Fuzhou, Qin for Mount Qin in Fuzhou. The prototypes of the characters are mostly late-Ming literati of Fujian, such as Xie Zhaozhe for Baochai, Ye Xianggao for Yuanchun, Shao Jiechun for Tanchun, Zhang Xie for Xiangyun, and Lin Chong for Daiyu. There are three reasons: Xu Xinggong "died before the last night of the renwu year," which fits the "last night of renwu" of 1.63; the author must meet several "hard criteria," namely book collecting, fame as a poet, social circle, standing as a man of letters, and experience with fiction; and item-by-item identifications of names, sobriquets and lines of verse. Unproven: this paper has found no outside document before Liao that attaches The Story of the Stone to Xu Xinggong; Liao also attributes Jin Ping Mei, Qingshi and other works to Xu Xinggong; and on the last forty chapters he has two views, supplementation by Xu Ling and composition "advancing side by side." "Kong Meixi, Wu Yufeng" are in his account a split of the characters 东吴 ("Eastern Wu") indicating that the author was from Fujian; they are not real title-givers. (From the sample chapters on Douban Read and the Bilibili column; of the Zhihu pieces only the titles have been seen.)

Fang Yizhi (Chen Xianfu). According to a China News report of 31 October 2011, Chen Xianfu claims that Fang Yizhi was the author, "and even Cao Xueqin was invented by him," the purpose of the book being "to mourn the Ming and oppose the Qing." His reasons: the three author-clues of chapter 1, the stone, the Passionate Monk and Cao Xueqin, relate to Fang Yizhi's alias "Wu Shigong" (吴石公) and to his taking the tonsure in Wuzhou; "Xueqin" is a homophone of xueqin 学勤, "diligent in study"; "red" hints at the Zhu (朱, "vermilion") house of the Ming, and "moon" (月) hints at "Ming" (明). The report says that his research had not been formally published and that he planned a book; this paper has so far found no such book. It has been pointed out that Fang Yizhi died in 1671, which is rather early (as reported by Ouyang Jian). A theory of collaboration, with Fang Yizhi writing and Mao Pijiang revising, also circulates; its original proponent is to be checked.

Hong Sheng (Tumore). The claim is that Hong Sheng wrote the first version, first called A Mirror for the Romantic among other titles and later changed to The Story of the Stone, and that Cao Xueqin was the one who read it over and revised it. The prototypes of the "four great families" are the four Hangzhou families of Hong, Gu, Huang and Qian; the women of the Banana Garden Poetry Society are the prototypes of the Twelve Beauties; Xixi is the prototype of the Prospect Garden; and Zhiyan Zhai is a pseudonym of Hong Sheng's wife Huang Hui (as summarized in Pang Guanqing's account of Tumore's "ten theses"). Unproven: this paper has found no direct record of Hong Sheng writing a novel.

Wu Meicun. Fu Bo and Zhong Changshan claim that Wu Meicun was the original author, and that Yan Shengsun revised the book under the name Cao Xueqin. Their reasons are that "Wu Yufeng," "Kong Meixi" and "Tangcun" together conceal "Wu Mei(cun)," and that the renwu of 1.63 should be the renwu of the Kangxi reign (1702), the very year of Yan Shengsun's death (from a reprint; original publication to be checked). Wu Chengmou claims that Wu Meicun wrote the original, with Cao Xueqin, Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E as its editors, and calls this "one scholar's view." Unproven: the splitting and joining of characters and the rereading of dates are both interpretations; this paper has found no outside evidence. A note in a manuscript on which this theory relies involves the Guiyou manuscript and is left to paper 3.

Mao Pijiang. Mao Lianquan wrote An Unsolved Case Solved: Seventy-Three Proofs that Mao Pijiang Wrote Dream of the Red Chamber; Gu Hao holds that the book was completed in the jiaxu year of Kangxi (1694), for reasons including the correspondence of the Shuihui Garden with the Prospect Garden, the Rugao dialect, and the theme of mourning the Ming; Huang Weimin takes Dong Xiaowan as the prototype of Lin Daiyu. Against this, Yan Zhong points out that chapter 54 mentions Sequel to the Lute (续琵琶), which Cao Yin wrote in 1703, whereas Mao Pijiang died in 1693.

Cao Fu (Chen Lin). The claim is that the author of the 120-chapter Story of the Stone was Cao Fu, that Cao Xueqin is a character invented by Cao Fu, and that the Jimao, Gengchen and other Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts are later forgeries (as reported). The dispute involves the authenticity of the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts; this paper records it and does not judge.

Zhu Xiangyu (Huo Guoling and others). The claim is that the book has a "double nature," with a history hidden behind the novel, and that the prototype of Lin Daiyu was Zhu Xiangyu; the eighty-chapter text with the Zhiyan Zhai commentary is Cao's complete work, and the core of the commentary is from Cao Xueqin's own hand. This theory denies that the book is unfinished, and so has no need of a layer of continuation.

The Ming royal house and other figures. These theories take the author to be Zhu Cizhao, a son of the Chongzhen emperor ("Honglou yinshi die lian hua"), or Zhu Cilang, the Chongzhen emperor himself, Yinreng, Zheng Keshuang and others (listed by Ouyang Jian in 2015; the proponents of each to be checked one by one). "The Third Prince Zhu" and the like are the persons identified, not proponents. Theories of this kind rest mainly on homophones, as when Jia Wang Xue Shi 贾王薛史, the four great families, is read as jia wang xue shi 家亡血史, "a family ruined, a history of blood."

Qin studies (Liu Xinwu). Liu Xinwu holds that the prototype of Qin Keqing was a daughter of Yinreng, the deposed crown prince of the Kangxi emperor, and that Cao Xueqin "had to delete and alter chapters already written in order to escape the 'literary inquisition'" (reprint). The reconstruction makes Qin Keqing come from the line of the deposed crown prince, in agreement with this view. The project did not rely on Liu's work; Liu's view came first (2003). Similarities and differences are left to paper 8; they are noted here in advance.

General assessments. Yan Zhong said in 2015 that 63 "authors" had been proposed (China News, 2015-11-30; this was his count for that year). Peng Guangqian says that "there is only one true answer to the riddle; the remaining sixty-odd are beyond doubt all false," and argues that Dunmin, Duncheng, Zhang Yiquan, Mingyi, Yurui, Yongzhong and the Zhiyan Zhai commentary should be the standard (The Paper, 2022-03-01). Ouyang Jian argues rather that "divergence is to be valued" and that one should "not rush to conclusions" (lecture of 2015, published by Aisixiang).

5.3 How they bear on the layer this paper depends on

Following §1, "Cao Xueqin" is taken as a role in the text. Seen in this way, the popular theories fall broadly into three kinds.

  • Those that take Cao Xueqin as the reviser, such as the Hong Sheng and Wu Meicun theories, are compatible with the layer this paper depends on.
  • Those that take "Cao Xueqin" to be a pen name or an invention, such as the Xu Xinggong, Fang Yizhi, Mao Pijiang and Cao Fu theories. They diverge from the common view over "who this person was," a dispute in which this paper takes no part; they do not directly conflict with the layer this paper depends on, and can simply be reported.
  • Those that take the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts to be forgeries, such as Chen Lin's, dispute the authenticity of the materials; as in §4.4, they are recorded and not judged.

6 How the Project Has Used These Conjectures

This section is a disclosure. In its backstage notes (the project's general account, the backstage volume and the volume on political background), the project put together a working chart of layers, used it to think through a good many things, and part of this entered the reconstruction. Following the author's ruling, the section gives the structure of the working chart, the source of each layer and the result of checking; every item that influenced the plot or wording of the reconstruction is set out one by one; inferences that have been withdrawn are reported as withdrawn. Prototype identifications are listed only in a table of sources in an appendix, without argument, and not as evidence.

6.1 The backstage working chart of layers

The backstage working chart has six stages: Xu Xinggong's Story of the Stone; Fang Yizhi's Record of the Passionate Monk; Kong Shangren's A Mirror for the Romantic and the "Wu Yufeng group's" Dream of the Red Chamber; the three-part Mirror for the Romantic of the "late elder brother of the Western Hall"; and finally Cao Xueqin. There is also a theory of a relay of signatures across three generations of Zhiyan Zhai, and more than forty prototype identifications drawn from the literary circle of Fuzhou.

To write this section, the paper traced the chart to its sources, stage by stage. The results are as follows.

First: in the materials of the various writers checked in this round, no one was found to propose this complete chain of hand-offs; the links between the stages were assembled by the project itself. The stages belong to theories that are incompatible with one another. In Liao Yihu's system, for example, there is only one authorial layer, Xu Xinggong; "Kong Meixi, Wu Yufeng" are for him a split of the characters for "Eastern Wu," which leaves no room for intermediate layers such as Fang Yizhi or Kong Shangren. Some individual nodes have sources; that does not give a source to the links between the nodes.

Second: the parts that have sources.

  • The theory of Xu Xinggong as author comes from Liao Yihu. Among the prototype identifications used in the backstage, Lin Chong for Daiyu, Ma Xu for Jia Lian and others are found in Liao's readable writings, which contain more than twenty such identifications. Several other identifications are recorded in the backstage as Liao Yihu's, such as Lin Gudu for Xiren, Ni Fan for Ping'er, Hong Shiying for Xiaohong and She Xiang for Jia She, but were not found in Liao's own writings in this round; Appendix 3 lists them as "source not found (once recorded in the backstage as Liao Yihu)," and does not put them under his name. Author's statement: the prototypes contemporary with Xu Xinggong came basically all from Liao Yihu; apart from the Xiangyun item, these prototypes had no influence on the writing of the current draft of the reconstruction. Lines written in earlier drafts on the basis of such prototypes and later withdrawn, such as the Fuzhou thread, in which Lingguan returns to Fuzhou to comment on the book, and the Bao Yong thread, are listed under "withdrawn" in §6.3. This is the author's account of the project's own writing history, not a judgement on Liao's theory.
  • The theory of Fang Yizhi as author comes from Chen Xianfu.
  • Kong Meixi as Kong Shangren comes from Zhang Zhijian (Sina blog, 2011).
  • The phrase "late elder brother of the Western Hall" comes from a note in a disputed manuscript; the problems of that manuscript are left to paper 3.

Third: the parts the project inferred itself: the "Wu Yufeng group"; Fang Yizhi's addition of the Roster of Feeling and of the monk and the Daoist; and the reading of two solar-term dates as Fang Yizhi's "signature" on his final draft (§6.2).

Fourth: the backstage is not consistent with itself, and more than ten points remain unreconciled. For example: whether "mourning the Ming" belongs to Xu Xinggong or to the Wu Yufeng group; whether Jihu Sou was Lin Chong, or the supplementer of some continuation; whether the cyclical dates should be moved back by one sixty-year cycle; and the total number of chapters, first 108 and later changed to 110.

Fifth: wording. In the backstage notes there are about thirty places where a conjecture is worded as a settled conclusion, such as "iron-clad proof," "final ruling," and "solving a riddle unsolved in 200 years of Redology." This paper does not use such wording, and where it quotes it, it gives the source. Where the backstage claims something as "the first" or as "never said by anyone," a literature search is made before publication.

6.2 Withdrawing the solar-term signature

The backstage once read "the thirtieth day of the eleventh month, the winter solstice" in chapter 11, and "the twenty-sixth day of the fourth month, the beginning of Grain in Ear" in chapter 27, as Fang Yizhi's signature on a final draft of 1665–1666, and on that basis, together with the objects in the first eighty chapters and other matters, drew up a timetable for the whole book covering 1655–1674.

The recalculation does not match, and the inference is withdrawn. Recalculated with modern true solar terms and true new moons (sxtwl 2.0.7, UTC+8), the winter solstice of 1665 falls on the fifteenth day of the eleventh month, and that eleventh month is a short month, with no thirtieth day; Grain in Ear of 1666 falls on the third day of the fifth month, seven days later than "the twenty-sixth day of the fourth month." The dates obtained do not support the original inference, and the author has withdrawn it on that basis. With differences of half a month and of seven days, the project does not make the pages of the calendar of the time a precondition of the withdrawal; the pages of the Shixian almanac for those years are wanted only for the record.

Even if a year in which both dates fit were one day found, that would show only that the story could be set in that year. It could not reach "someone finalized the book in that year," still less "this person was the author."

The signature is withdrawn, but the timetable left one thing behind: the sequence of years, the festivals and seasons, and the characters' ages (counted in sui) in each chapter of the reconstruction were arranged by it; the backstage records that Daiyu dies in Rec. ch. 95 on the third day of the third month, the Shangsi festival, with the note "according to the timetable." The text of the reconstruction gives no reign titles, and does not name Fang Yizhi. So the timetable has entered the text in the form of a schedule of festivals and seasons; it now serves only as a working coordinate for the writing, and has no independent reason of its own (Appendix 4, item 2). An attentive reader might work back from the festivals and seasons of the chapters to the years set in the backstage; the project does not make this a claim about the years of the story or of the book's composition. The other thing that entered the text is the Kangxi-era ground of the object layer; its source and its reason are given in §6.3.

6.3 What entered the reconstruction

The backstage materials list twenty-four items, each marked as in the text, withdrawn, or not in the final text; Appendix 4 gives an account of each. The main text selects, grouping the items by the part that the thought of layering played.

Mostly left in the backstage. The great majority of the prototype identifications; Lin Xue commenting on the book, and the theory of a relay across three generations of Zhiyan Zhai; Fang Yizhi's "four avatars"; and the "trace of renaming" in the Prince of Dong'an.

By the author's statement (§6.1), the prototypes of the Liao Yihu line, apart from the Xiangyun item, had no influence on the writing of the current draft of the reconstruction. The backstage once linked the apricot tree outside Ping'er's room in Rec. ch. 109 to Ni Fan's "Old Apricot Studio" (古杏轩), and linked Tanchun's surviving her distant marriage in Rec. ch. 85 to Shao Jiechun's "poetic compensation"; by the author's statement, the writing of these two passages does not rest on the prototypes, and they too are recorded in this group.

Those that reached the plot or the wording.

  • In Rec. ch. 109 the Sanhe troupe stages The Peach Blossom Fan. This echoes the theory that Kong Meixi was Kong Shangren, but the final proofreading required that Kong Shangren's name not appear in the text.
  • In Rec. ch. 110 Xiangyun's "Rhapsody on My Humble Lodging" (敝舍赋) has the line "I once read the Dongxiyang kao" (尝览《东西洋考》, Zhang Xie's study of the maritime world), which draws on Zhang Xie as a prototype. As far as the prototype identifications of the Liao Yihu line go, this is the only item that influenced the writing of the current draft.

These prototypes suggested material. Their entry into the novel does not, in return, prove that the proposed prototypes are the persons behind the original characters.

Wording changed; the layering account left in the backstage. In Rec. ch. 83 Grandmother Jia admonishes Jia Zheng. An earlier draft of the reconstruction had her speak of "an empty frame" (虚架子); this is now changed to a warning not to care only for appearances in outside affairs, so that Grandmother Jia does not foresee the confiscation of the family's property. The backstage recorded this change as "what is kept is acknowledged": what a former author kept, a later one acknowledges. This paper cannot state which place in the first eighty chapters the former author kept, so the account is left in the backstage, and is not counted as a case in which the thought of layering directly influenced a choice.

Layering conjecture used where it is not load-bearing. The rereading of Qin Keqing. The Roster of Feeling in Rec. ch. 110 says of her: "The world says she ruined the family by her beauty, and names her lewd; yet her sin was not beauty but politics." In Rec. chs. 104 and 105 her background is supplied through Baozhu's account and through Qin Keqing appearing in a dream. This rereading rests on a conjecture: that Cao Xueqin did not himself accept the "lewdness" with which the Mirror for the Romantic layer had cast Qin Keqing (overview §3.5). This is the project's own judgement on the number of layers and on whose each was. The overview (§1.4) has already listed it, together with "the reason for the excision of the Tianxiang Tower episode," as not load-bearing: if it falls, only a part is damaged. The reconstruction makes her come from the line of the deposed crown prince, in agreement with Liu Xinwu's view, as noted in §5.2.

Grounds established independently; the thought of layering only a side aid.

  • The Tianxiang Tower is not supplied. The ground is the inviolable: what the first eighty chapters narrate is neither changed nor supplemented (overview §3.5). The layering thought that "a deletion is a blank to be left" serves only as a side aid.
  • The object layer takes a Kangxi-era ground, with the dynasty left unnamed. The source is the backstage, where it was originally linked to the Fang Yizhi layer: the backstage used it as "the yardstick for judging whether a given description in the first 80 chapters belongs to Fang Yizhi's original layer." The Fang Yizhi layer and the solar-term signature have both been withdrawn. The present reason is the author's: the glass, the Western medicines, the full-length mirrors and so on described in the first eighty chapters are, within the book, rarities that only the nobility could enjoy, and the project reads this as closer to conditions in the Kangxi reign than in the Qianlong. This is the project's own reading and a basis for its writing; it is not textual research, and this paper does not argue from it to any date of composition.

Withdrawn. The Fuzhou thread, in which Lingguan returns to Fuzhou to comment on the book; the mapping of Yuanchun's and Yuanyang's deaths onto the years 1627 and 1628; the double reading of Lady Wang "blocking the scissors"; the Bao Yong thread; and Zhen Baoyu becoming a monk and delivering the Yushi xinbian (玉石新编).

7 The Minimal Working Judgement Adopted in This Paper, and Its Limits

The paper depends only on this. The main body of the text of the surviving first eighty chapters went through layering, and the one who integrated it into a book and fixed its centre was the Cao Xueqin named in the chain of titles and in the commentary (a role in the text).

Known exceptions, each recorded as another layer, and not part of the layer this paper depends on:

  1. the unfinished, damaged and patched end of chapter 22: 22.104, "from here on damaged and lost; what follows is a later supplement," and 22.106, "this chapter was not finished when Qin [Xueqin] died"; this paper does not rule on who wrote the supplied text or on what it was based;
  2. the title: the commentator "copied it and commented on it again in the jiaxu year, and went back to the title The Story of the Stone";
  3. the Tianxiang Tower: the commentator ordered Qinxi to delete it (13.89);
  4. the missing and supplied chapters 64 and 67;
  5. the Cheng–Gao edition's alterations to the first eighty chapters.

These are listed in the order that holds if the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts came first. If the view that the Zhiyan Zhai manuscripts came later were to hold, items 1 to 3 would be recorded as layers of the Zhiyan Zhai lineage, and item 5 would run in the opposite direction; the one layer this paper depends on would not be affected, but the list of exceptions would have to be rearranged.

"Fixed its centre" is the project's working judgement, which this paper does not argue. What the centre is belongs to the overview's interpretation; it is for the overview to argue and to test, and it is not brought back as evidence for this layer.

What stands even if the commentary is not genuine. The Cheng lineage also has, in the chain of titles, the sentence "Cao Xueqin, in the Mourning-the-Red Studio, read it over for ten years and revised it five times" (from the common text; the page of the Chengjia edition to be checked); Cheng Weiyuan's preface says, "only, the book itself records that Mr. Cao Xueqin deleted and altered it several times" (common transcription; no transcription that can be named has been seen); and the inconsistencies are all in the text itself. The Cheng editions keep the book's own account, and Cheng Weiyuan's preface cites it, so the two do not count as independent pieces of supporting evidence. On this basis the paper keeps the working understanding of the segment "the one who integrated it into a book was the Cao Xueqin named in the text," whose historical attribution remains open to testing; the segment "fixed its centre" is not argued in this paper and is not included here. So the paper takes no part in the dispute over authenticity because it has no need to.

Conditions for overturning.

  • Reliable evidence that the one who integrated the main body of the text of the surviving first eighty chapters was not the one named in the chain of titles and the commentary, the known exceptions listed above not counting.
  • Or evidence that the present text has no layering on the side of composition (revision by the author, merging, or reworking), and was written in one pass.

Since "Cao Xueqin" is taken as a role in the text, the first condition concerns the one named in the text, and has nothing to do with who this person was historically. The second conflicts with the materials of §3 and is unlikely, but it is stated all the same.

Limits. If the judgement on composition changes, what must change are the corresponding attributions, layers and citations; whether the textual interpretation "people have qing" then fails would need a separate argument. The overview (§1.4) has already said that even if the working hypotheses in its not-load-bearing column were overturned, "people have qing" and the method of interpretive reconstruction could still stand.

The paper does not depend on:

  • any determination of the number of layers or of whose each was. The main thesis does not; one not-load-bearing place used such a determination once, namely the rereading of Qin Keqing (§6.3);
  • any prototype identification;
  • any ruling on the authenticity of any material;
  • the provenance of the Guiyou manuscript (paper 3).

8 Conclusion

The conclusion of this paper can be stated briefly. The book is layered. The materials are in §3 and the theories in §§4 and 5, all registered by provenance, without judgement on which is right. The paper depends on one layer only, and has stated its exceptions, the conditions under which it would be overturned, and what it does not depend on. The layering conjectures the project used in its backstage are disclosed as they stand in §6: which were only thought about, which entered the reconstruction, and which were withdrawn. Where there is a source, the source is given; what was not found in this round is marked as not found; and only what the backstage explicitly records as set up by the project itself is marked as proposed by the project.

The reader should be able to see three things clearly: what the original materials say, which step is some scholar's interpretation, and which step is the project's own association. The reader should also be able to see, once an association has been withdrawn, which choices in the novel are still kept for other reasons. If these three are kept apart, the paper has no need to win a dispute over authorship.

Appendix 6 Differences from the Overview v0.8

In the following places this paper is authoritative, and the next version of the overview will be revised accordingly (Chinese and English together):

  1. §3.4, "Its main thesis depends on one point only: that the book is layered, and that the one who finally fixed its centre was Cao Xueqin": to be revised following §1 and §7 of this paper, to read "the main body of the text of the surviving first eighty chapters went through layering, and the one who integrated it into a book and fixed its centre was the Cao Xueqin named in the chain of titles and in the commentary"; with a note on the known exceptions (the end of chapter 22 described as "unfinished, damaged and patched," not as "a supplement after Qin [Xueqin]'s death"), and a note that "fixed its centre" is a working judgement, not argued in this paper.

1 立场

先把本文站在哪里说清。以下各条，都照作者历次的裁定。

认可层累说。《红楼梦》不是一次写成的单层文本。第一回自己就说，书名换过几次，题者换过几人；脂批又记下了删去的、迷失的、未完的稿子。这些材料，本文第 3 节逐条登记。

只依赖一层。本文的主论点，以及整个系列，只依赖一件事：今存前八十回正文的主体经过层累，把它整合成书、按下中心的，是题名串与脂批所称的曹雪芹。

这一句要限定三处。

  • “曹雪芹”取文本角色，指题名串与脂批所称、“披阅十载，增删五次”的那一位，批语里又称雪芹、芹溪、芹。此人历史上是谁，诸说各有道理，本项目没有新证据，所以不参与这一争论。
  • “主体”二字，是因为今存前八十回并非每一个字都出于这一位之手。批者改过题名，命删过一节；第二十二回末有未完、破失与补缀，补入的文字由谁、据何而来，本文不裁；第六十四、六十七回有缺有补；程高本又改动过前八十回。这些各记另一层，不在此列（第 7 节）。
  • “按下中心”是本项目的工作判断，本篇不证。中心是什么，是总篇的诠释，由总篇去论证与检验，不回头作这一层的证据。

不提出新的成书史考证，只述不判。具体分几层、各层出于谁手，学界诸说与民间诸说一律照述：各写主张、理由与未证之处。本文不裁对错，也不参与争论。但引文、来源与转引关系，仍逐项核对；照述不等于照抄。

民间诸说各有缘由，对本项目的思考有帮助。帮了什么，第 6 节逐条写明，分方法与取材两类。凡被本项目用过的，交代用在哪里，是否已撤。

批语是需保留来路的证词。这一条与专题一相同。两条批语不合，可能是两稿不同；本文只作可能登记，不据以判谁错。

不参与真伪之争。本项目选择相信书有层累、稿有多版；真与伪的二元对立，对复原没有帮助。凡来历有争议的材料，如靖本批语、某些抄本、某些书外记载，本文照其来路登记为一项材料及其所声称的层次，注明有争议，不判真伪。登记不等于确认该层历史上确曾存在；涉及成书史推断时，注明以何种来源为条件。本文所以可以不参与，是因为不必参与：本文只依赖的那一层，不靠脂批之真也站得住（第 7 节）。

3.1 题名串与凡例

第一回正文有一串题名：

从此空空道人因空见色，由色生情，传情入色，自色悟空，遂易名为情僧，改《石头记》为《情僧录》。至吴玉峰题曰《红楼梦》。东鲁孔梅溪则题曰《风月宝鉴》。后因曹雪芹于悼红轩中披阅十载，增删五次，纂成目录，分出章回，则题曰《金陵十二钗》。

隔着一首标题诗，又有一句：

至脂砚斋甲戌抄阅再评，仍用《石头记》。

甲戌本凡例（1.2）则说：“是书题名极多，《红楼梦》是总其全部之名也。又曰《风月宝鉴》，是戒妄动风月之情。又曰《石头记》，是自譬石头所记之事也。”

这是全书关于成书最直接的自述，各派都要解释“增删五次”，读法大体有三种。

  • 题名对应稿次。陈庆浩认为，五个题名中只有《风月宝鉴》《石头记》《红楼梦》三个是不同阶段稿本的命名（转引）。沈治钧把各层与题名一一对应：初稿名《风月宝鉴》，强化石头叙事后名《石头记》，再经增删可能易名《情僧录》，扩建大观园、降低人物年龄后成《金陵十二钗》，最后定名《红楼梦》（转引）。李清宇把五个题名读作四个创作观念阶段：寓劝戒，实录其事，问世传奇，戏与梦同（转载）。
  • 题名对应两部书。杜春耕据凡例“总其全部之名”，立二书合成之说（第 4.2 节）。周绍良回应鲁迅把异名看作“摇曳见态”的说法，认为异名有事实根据，反映真实的编纂历史（转载）。
  • 题名串是作者的布置。1.62 甲戌眉批说楔子即作者所撰，“雪芹披阅增删”是作者的“式猾”（第 3.2 节）。照这一读，题名串的叙事姿态，不能不经解释就当作一份逐人、逐阶段的档案。

题名串把书呈现为经过若干题名、披阅增删与再评的文本。本项目依此作工作理解：最后一次题名，出于“披阅十载，增删五次”的那一位；评者又把书名改了回去。这是题名串自己的说法，作者布置的一读仍然并列。评者改名一句，本文记作批者一层的记录。

还有一层要记。有说吴玉峰一句与甲戌再评一句都是甲戌本独有，他本多无；本文尚未见到校记原文，也未逐本核对，待核。若确是如此，题名串本身就有本与本之层，又有两读：甲戌本所增，或他本所删。第 5 节吴玉峰的诸种比附，据的正是这一句。

旧稿与题名（1.61、1.62）。

1.61 是第二路与第三路的分水岭。读作雪芹自己的旧作、其弟棠村为之作序，是二书或二源合成一路的依据；吴世昌“棠村小序”之说即由此出，他认为今存脂本若干回的回前总评原是棠村为《风月宝鉴》作的小序，经脂砚“仍因之”保留下来（据梁羽生转述吴氏自题诗“棠村小序分明在，红学专家苦未知”；原书章节页码待核）。读作雪芹旧得他人之书，便与第 3.5 节裕瑞“曹雪芹得之”相接，是他人旧稿一路。“故仍因之”因的是题名还是序，也有两读。

1.62 是一条反向的证据，要说清它反的是什么。批者问：若说雪芹只是披阅增删，开卷这篇楔子又是谁写的？可见“雪芹披阅增删”是作者故弄的笔法，“观者万不可被作者瞒蔽了去”。主张曹雪芹即原作者的一路引它。它反的是“雪芹只是改作者”，以及把题名串逐环实指为不同作者的读法。它不反“同一作者反复修改”：批者说楔子出于作者之手，并没有说书没改过。同本批者别处有 1.140“余谓雪芹撰此书”、2.5“自是雪芹平生所长”，可以并看。第三路怎样回应这一条，本文未见具体另说，照写。

删稿：天香楼（13.3、13.8、13.21、13.51、13.52、13.87、13.89、13.90）。

这是批语里记得最细的一处删改。13.89 甲戌回末总评说秦可卿托凤姐的两件后事令人感服，“姑赦之，因命芹溪删去”；13.8 靖本同文，多出“‘遗簪’、‘更衣’诸文”与“是以此回只十页”一段。13.87 甲戌眉批另记页数（“少去四五页”），所以删稿的页数不只系于靖本。13.3 甲戌说天香楼一节“不忍下笔”。

照批者自述，执笔删的是芹溪，命删的是批者。

13.51 甲戌侧批在瑞珠“他也触柱而亡”一句下，说这是“补天香楼未删之文”；13.52 靖侧批又说“是亦未删之笔”。照批者自己的话，删而未尽，层痕留在正文里。这是第十三回批者所记最直接的一处。

13.21 要分本读。底本在一处【】之内著录了三本：甲戌眉批“九个字写尽天香楼事，是不写之写”，底本注“靖本多署名‘棠村’”，指的是这一批；庚辰眉批只有“可从此批”四字；靖眉批“可从此批”之后，才有“通回将可卿如何死故隐去，是余大发慈悲也。叹叹！壬午季春。笏叟”。带“余”“壬午季春”“笏叟”的这一段长款，在这一处只标为靖本。另有 13.90 庚辰批，记同旨而较短的文字：“通回将可卿如何死故隐去，是大发慈悲心也，叹叹！壬午春。”没有“余”，多一“心”字，署“壬午春”，不署“笏叟”。两条分条并列，不把一条的署名与署年移给另一条。

删的缘由，批者自述是“赦之”。今本确不存此节，可见曹雪芹没有违背此命。至于曹雪芹自己为什么从之，是另一个问题，本项目有一个推测，第 6.3 节照实披露。

迷失与未完（20.20、26.24、26.120、26.153、22.104、22.106、75.3）。

几条批语记下后部有稿而迷失：20.20“被借阅者迷失”；26.24 甲戌眉批，狱神庙一大回“迷失无稿”；26.153 甲戌回末，射圃文字“迷失无稿”；26.120 庚辰眉批则只说“无稿”。署丁亥、畸笏的，是 20.20、26.120，以及 26.24 的庚辰本；26.24 甲戌本与 26.153 无署。蔡义江用这些批语解释八十回以后何以不传；梅节说，曹雪芹已向以脂砚为首的小圈子“交了货”，这个意义上书是完成了，后部因借阅迷失而不传（均转载）。

22.104 有两种字面读法。底本作“此后破失，系再补”，意思是此后是再补之文；通行本作“俟再补”，意思是此后原缺，待补。专题一把它报存不裁，那是处理方式，不是第三种读法。底本在这一批之后一直写到回末，回末是 22.106“此回未成而芹逝矣”。这些材料涉及回末的未完、破失与补缀；补入的文字由谁、据何而来，本文不裁。这是第 1 节所说“主体”之外的一处。75.3“缺中秋诗俟雪芹”，也是未完的自记。

批者与执笔（22.22 至 22.24，1.63）。

22.22 庚辰眉批说“凤姐点戏，脂砚执笔事”；22.23 追记“今丁亥夏只剩朽物一枚”；22.24 靖眉批同文，多出“芹溪、脂砚、杏斋诸子皆相继别去”一句。

这一组有三点要说。

  • “脂砚执笔”不译作合著。执笔是写作、是抄录，还是记下某一次点戏之事，诸解各有提出者；本文未核的，照写未核。
  • 22.23、22.24 是后批承前批，能用来讨论批语之间的先后，但不算两个独立的见证。
  • 这一组提供了执笔关系与前后批追记的材料。“前批”未排除同一批者追记自己的旧批，单凭这一组，不定批者人数。

1.63 甲戌眉批“壬午除夕，书未成，芹为泪尽而逝”，末署“甲午八月泪笔”，又是批者一层留下的时间与身份记录。

后部之稿与两批互异（18.17、18.19、20.20）。

20.20 有“余只见有一次誊清时”的自述，是批者见过后部之稿的明说。18.19 说“至回末警幻情榜方知”诸册芳讳，指向后部的设计，没有明说亲见。回数的材料，总篇 3.5 节已列表，这里不重列。

18.17 与 6.48 是第 1 节所说两批互异、只作可能登记的一个实例。18.17 庚辰双行夹批把平儿推在又副册（“余未多及，想为……平儿等人无疑矣”），6.48 甲戌双行夹批说平儿“想亦在副册内者也”。专题一取 6.48 的副册一层：第 110 回照补记条〇〇三补写，平儿一页在副册之内。专题一的说法是“只记取了哪一边，不判另一边错”。

分回与对清（17.1、75.2）。

17.1“此回宜分二回方妥”，与己卯、庚辰两本第十七、十八回未分回的形态相应，是章回尚在整理中的记录。75.2“乾隆二十一年五月初七日对清”是抄本的对清记录。

西堂（28.82、28.83）。

28.82 庚辰眉批：“大海饮酒，西堂产九台灵芝日也，批书至此，宁不悲乎？壬午重阳日。”28.83 甲戌侧批：“谁曾经过？叹叹！西堂故事。”“西堂”通行读作曹寅的书斋，首倡者与出处本文尚未查到。民间另有以“西堂”为尤侗的，也有据一种争议抄本的批语立“西堂故兄”之说的；后者涉及癸酉本，留给专题三。

两条通则。

  • 以上专题一的登记，是项目的处理记录，不是替材料完成的历史鉴真。本文把它们并列在这里，是为了让读者看到同一条批语在两篇里各怎样用，不把审计标签当作成书史的旁证。
  • 表中只见于靖本的几条（13.8，13.21 的“棠村”署名与靖眉批，13.52，22.24；13.21 靖眉批的同旨短文，另见 13.90 庚辰），以及 13.3 据靖本补的缺字，靖本有真伪之争：高树伟《红楼梦靖藏本辨伪》（中华书局，2024）主伪；梅节《也谈靖本》（《红楼梦学刊》2002 年第 1 辑）主真。本文照第 1 节不参与，只照来路登记。专题一定靖本为参级，那是“本项目给各本批语定的采信分量，不是对批语真伪或各本价值的判断”。

彩云与彩霞。与贾环相好的丫鬟，底本第二十五回作彩霞（“只有彩霞还和他合的来”），第三十、六十至六十二、七十回作彩云（第七十回“彩云因近日和贾环分崩”），第七十二回又作彩霞（“心中虽是与贾环有旧”）。这几处人名，本文已对底本核到。何以把彩云、彩霞视为同一人物的前后异名，而不是两人并存，依各家的具体论证；本文未取得那一层论证，暂列为文本现象。诸本异文是否把一部分统一过，待逐本核。

第十三回的族人名单。秦氏死后，“贾代儒带领贾敕、贾效、贾敦、贾赦、贾政、贾琮……贾兰、贾菌、贾芝等都来了”。这份名单连贾赦、贾政都在内，贾琮与贾兰在列，最平的一读是同一人物出场。刘世德认为，初稿里的贾琮只是一般族人，到了改稿才成了贾琏之弟（转引）。持论者为什么认作两人，本文还没有读到原研究的论证，这一例暂记为“某说提出的疑点”，不把名单本身当作已经确立的层累证据。

东安与东平。第三回荣禧堂乌木联牌对联下的落款是“同乡世教弟勋袭东安郡王穆莳拜手书”，“东安”全书只此一见；第十一、十四回的四王之中作东平郡王，没有写穆莳之名。两个王号并存，本文只登记为王号配置待比较，诸本是否一致待逐本校，不先认定是同一人的封号被改。本项目后台曾把这一处读作曹雪芹一层的“改名遗痕”，学界的最早提出者本文未查到（第 6 节、附录三）。

十三载。第二十五回和尚说“青埂峰一别，展眼已过十三载矣”，学者以为与他处宝玉的年齿不合。张爱玲论宝玉年龄在改写中逐次下调，是这类参差最常被引的解释（转载）。本文只核到这一端；何以视为不合，依各家所采的时间连接，暂列为文本现象。

  • 缺回与补回：庚辰本缺第六十四、六十七回，后来诸本补入（通行著录）。两回在各本中的存缺与来源，学者看法不同，本文不判。

  • 回目异文：各本回目有出入，待逐本列。
  • 批语署年：己卯冬、壬午、乙酉、丁亥、甲午。乙酉只一见，25.61 庚辰眉批“乙酉冬，雪窗。畸笏老人。”

3.5 书外的记载

书外有几种记载与成书有关。每条写明本文实际从谁的文字转引。

明义《题红楼梦》二十首并小序。小序开头说：“曹子雪芹出所撰《红楼梦》一部，备记风月繁华之盛……”（据张爱玲《红楼梦魇·二详红楼梦》所引，努努书坊网络本，已核；拟再核一粟编《红楼梦卷》）。明义所见本与今本不尽相合，吴世昌据此立“初稿”说：明义所见的曹雪芹《红楼梦》初稿“故事全在大观园内”，结论是“雪芹是《红楼梦》的作者，又是把石头所记的材料与《红楼梦》合在一起以后的加工者”（《论明义所见〈红楼梦〉初稿》，《红楼梦学刊》1980 年第 1 辑；转载）。

裕瑞《枣窗闲笔》记：“旧有《风月宝鉴》一书，又名《石头记》，不知为何人之笔，曹雪芹得之，以是书所传述者，与其家之事迹略同，因借题发挥，将此部删改至五次。”又记所见抄本“本本有其叔脂砚之批语”。本文据周建渝所引文学古籍刊行社 1957 年影印本（第 9、21 页）；另有转引作“其叔脂砚斋”，两种引文并存，待核影印本。这是“他人旧稿、曹为改作”一说最早的文献依据。刘梦溪认为裕瑞所记多据传闻，未必可信（转引）。《枣窗闲笔》本身的真伪另有专门讨论，如高树伟 2024 年专书设《裕瑞〈枣窗闲笔〉真伪考》一章；本文只见该书目录，其结论待核原书。

程甲本（1791）程伟元序：“作者相传不一，究未知出自何人，惟书内记雪芹曹先生删改数过。”（通行转录；本文未见可指名的转录本，程甲原页待核。）又述后四十回的来路：“数年以来，仅积有二十余卷。一日，偶于鼓担上得十余卷。”（据胡适《红楼梦考证》所引，爱思想转载，已核。）程乙本（1792）的引言，本文未见原页，待核。程序这几句，是程本一系对“披阅增删”的记录，与题名串相应。

敦诚《寄怀曹雪芹》“不如著书黄叶村”，常被引作曹雪芹著书之证，诗句未点书名；《挽曹雪芹》“牛鬼遗文”一句，有人读作遗稿之证（出处待核，本文尚未见到）。敦敏、张宜泉的诗中，本文未查到直接提到写书的句子。以上原集页均待核。

4 学界成书诸说

学界的成书之说，本文分三路述，另列脂本与程本先后之争。以下按所引著作的主要主张归组，不把学者的全部著述归入唯一阵营；同一人的不同阶段、不同问题，可在各处分别说明。每家的主张、所依证据、代表著作、反方与可靠度，详见附录一；正文只写要点。学术史的锚点，用杜志军《新时期红学三十年：1978—2008》（《红楼梦学刊》2009 年第 2 辑）。

4.1 一书多改

这一路认为，书是同一部稿子多次改出来的，旧稿的痕迹叠在今本里。

胡适是基线。《红楼梦考证》初稿 1921 年 5 月作为亚东图书馆标点本《红楼梦》的序文刊行，同年 11 月改定。他说“《红楼梦》的著者是曹雪芹”，“八十回以后的四十回，是高鹗补的”（转载）。他不设旧稿之层，全书只分曹作八十回与高补四十回两层。

周汝昌、冯其庸、蔡义江在此基础上承认，“增删五次”是曹雪芹本人的多次修改，《风月宝鉴》是他的旧作。冯其庸从脂本的抄写款式、回目与批语比对，论庚辰本是“曹雪芹生前《石头记》早期钞本中最完整的一个本子”（转载）。蔡义江说，《风月宝鉴》“是后来《石头记》加工修改的基础，这种可能性是很大的”；他反对二书合成，理由是“小说不是理论性文章，有各不相同的人物形象，是很难或者应该说是不可能合成的”（转载）。

张爱玲用异文细读重建改写层次。她说曹雪芹“一直是此书改写的主持人”，前八十回改写了二十多年，不同时期的早本已经传出，异文就是改写留下的地层；宝玉的年龄在改写中逐次下调（转载）。她同时认为《石头记》与《风月宝鉴》原为二书，《风月宝鉴》收入之后，书中才有太虚幻境。所以她跨在一二两路之间，这里按其主要用力之处，归在一书多改。

按题名分期的几家。陈庆浩、沈治钧、李清宇把题名串读作稿次或创作观念的阶段（第 3.1 节）。刘世德从版本比勘中辨出“初稿和改稿两种成分”，把两个贾兰、两个贾琮、彩云与彩霞等参差看作两稿叠压的痕迹（第 3.3 节；转引）。

夏薇以《风月宝鉴》为曹雪芹的初稿，作者“以初稿《风月宝鉴》为基础进行升华与鼎新”，并从甄、贾二宝玉的相反游历，读出出仕与归隐的二元政治观（《从〈风月宝鉴〉到〈红楼梦〉——成书与创作思想的嬗变》，《文学评论》2022 年第 6 期；转载）。

朱淡文的归类暂定在这一路。夏薇文开头所引朱淡文《红楼梦论源》（江苏古籍出版社，1992）第 197 页说，诸题名“所题的是同一部小说在不同创作阶段的稿本”，甲戌以前并非有五个稿本，“前两次增删即在初稿之上进行，因为目前我们能够肯定确实存在过的稿本只有两本”（转载，已核夏薇引文）。这是一书多改的说法。这两句都是转引，原书第 197、200 页本文未核；书中别处是否另有二书合成之论，待核。她另论旧稿的世系与今本不同，原稿把贾母设计为荣国公之妻（胡联浩引）。

反方：二书合成一路认为，一书多改说解释不了宁荣两府的笔墨差异（第 4.2 节）。杜志军评一稿多改与二书合并两说，“都还有不成熟的一面，而客观、系统、翔实、深入的微观论证仍嫌不足”（转载）。

4.2 二书（二源）合成

这一路认为，《风月宝鉴》与《石头记》等两部稿子，由曹雪芹合成一书。

周绍良较早提出两稿汇合。他认为《风月宝鉴》是曹雪芹真实存在过的旧稿，今本是原《石头记》与《风月宝鉴》两部初稿的汇合；出自《风月宝鉴》的，有凤姐与贾瑞、秦可卿与天香楼、贾琏与尤二姐及多姑娘、秦钟、薛蟠等一系列“风月”故事，主题都围绕“戒妄动风月之情”（《“雪芹旧有〈风月宝鉴〉之书”》，《红楼梦学刊》1979 年第 1 辑；转载）。

吴世昌有两说。1961 年的英文专著提出“棠村小序”之说（第 3.2 节）。1980 年的文章提出二源合成：明义所见的曹雪芹《红楼梦》初稿是一源；“省亲故事，石头所记，《石头记》者，脂砚所评。《石头记》的评者亦即《石头记》的作者。这个作者不是曹雪芹”，是另一源；曹雪芹是把两者合在一起的加工者（转载）。他在《红楼梦探源外编》第 483 页有“它不是雪芹所作”一句（周建渝转引），“它”指什么，须核原书。

杜春耕是二书合成说最明确的一家。他认为今本由两部原稿合成：一部以荣国府为中心，是《石头记》；一部以宁国府及贾赦一支为中心，是《风月宝鉴》。曹雪芹做了“二次创作”，合并二书，改动人物关系，“让荣、宁两公成了亲弟兄”。所举证据有两府择偶标准不同，贾敬与贾政生日的规格差异，秦可卿丧礼的规格，冷子兴演说中“荣国一支”与“宁荣两宅”的措辞，以及凡例“总其全部之名”与 1.61（《荣宁两府两本书》，《红楼梦学刊》1998 年第 3 辑，转载；另有《曹雪芹“披阅十载、增删五次”考》，同刊 2007 年第 3 辑，书目转引）。

谭德晶把既有研究归纳为多改说、组合说、剪贴说三类，认为《红楼梦》对《风月宝鉴》是有选择的整合，主题由戒淫转向对女性之美与悲感的表现（转载）。

反方：蔡义江认为人物形象不同的两部小说“不可能合成”；他还以“疑人偷斧”批评二书合成说，这一语见杜春耕转述，杜氏反驳说同样的逻辑也适用于一稿多改说（原出处未查到）。

4.3 他人旧稿，曹为改作

这一路认为，原作另有其人，曹雪芹是增删改作者。

裕瑞的记载是这一路最早的文献依据（第 3.5 节）。

戴不凡1979 年在《北方论丛》第 1 期发表《揭开〈红楼梦〉作者之谜》，副题是“论曹雪芹是在石兄《风月宝鉴》旧稿基础上巧手新裁改作成书的”（转引），此后又有《石兄和曹雪芹》（同刊第 3 期）与《秦可卿晚死考》（初刊待核）。他认为书中的“石兄”另有其人，石兄的旧稿就是《风月宝鉴》，曹雪芹不是创始意义上的作者，只是增删者。所举内证见第 3.3 节，其中方言一条说：“它的旧稿原是个难改吴侬口音的人写的……而改（新）稿则是一位精通北京方言的人的作品”（转载）。

戴说引来近三十篇反驳，论争文章后来结集为《〈红楼梦〉著作权论争集》（《北方论丛》编辑部编，太原：山西人民出版社，1985）。反驳的要点有三：

  • 陈熙中、侯忠义指出，戴氏“把方言和方言词汇混为一谈”（詹丹转述）；吴世昌、张锦池也认为用方言证明作者不止一人的方法不科学。
  • 刘梦溪有《秦可卿之死与曹雪芹的著作权——读戴不凡同志〈秦可卿晚死考〉书后》（《文艺研究》1979 年第 4 期，书目转引）；又论节令、气候“基本上是前后贯通、回次相继的”，得不出时间倒流的结论（转载）。
  • 张碧波、邹进先驳戴氏推定的石兄即曹竹村。

各篇的篇名与期次，本文以《论争集》目录为准，尚待逐篇核对。

潘重规认为《红楼梦》“另有隐名的原作者；曹雪芹只是增删补订的执笔人”（周建渝转引）。其说出自民族主义索隐一脉，本文只取其成书主张。

皮述民自注：“关于《石头记》有两个作者的推考，详情请参阅拙著《红楼梦考论集》，第 37—39、111—115 页”（转载；《红楼梦考论集》，台北：联经出版事业公司，1984）。一般概括其说为脂砚斋即李鼎，李鼎早期参与了《石头记》的稿子（转引）。他是否主张李鼎有一部完整的旧稿、曹雪芹只是改作者，本文未见原书，不作此说。

4.4 脂本与程本先后之争

欧阳健主张“程前脂后”：脂本只是后出的抄本，“脂砚斋”是“一群先后相承的作伪者的共名”，程甲本才是真本（转引）。系列论文始于《〈红楼梦〉“两大版本系统”说辨疑——兼论脂砚斋出于刘铨福之伪托》（《复旦学报》1991 年第 5 期），专著有《红楼新辨》（花城出版社，1994）、《红学辨伪论》（贵州人民出版社，1996）、《还原脂砚斋》（黑龙江教育出版社，2003）。

批驳者有应必诚《关于〈红楼梦〉的版本系统——兼与欧阳健同志商榷》（《复旦学报》1992 年第 1 期），以及陈诏、蔡义江、宋谋玚、杨光汉、冯其庸、徐乃为等（据林沂、蔡政星综述，转引）。杜志军的评价是，这一“辨伪”“目前看来……是难以成立的”，但“质疑的发生也从反面证明了……脂程关系的复杂性”（转载）。

这一争与上三路不在一根轴上。上三路争的是书怎样写成，这一争争的是材料的先后。它挑战的，是以脂本、脂批早于程本为前提的那部分成书论证；前三路还援引别的材料，如程本一系的题名串文字、程伟元序、正文的前后参差，不全受它影响。本文照录其说与批驳，不参与真伪之争。

4.5 英文学界

英文学界的成书研究，本文只带一句。吴世昌 1961 年的专著 On The Red Chamber Dream: A Critical Study of Two Annotated Manuscripts of the XVIIIth Century（Oxford: Clarendon Press），是直接处理成书的早期专著，同年有韩南（P. D. Hanan）在 BSOAS、夏志清（C. T. Hsia）在 JAS 的书评。霍克思（David Hawkes）为企鹅本 The Story of the Stone 第一卷（1973）所写导言，述及《风月宝鉴》与改写，页码待核。浦安迪（Andrew H. Plaks）的研究以结构与寓意为主，不归入任何一路。

4.6 三路相合之处

三路所争，是一书还是二书，是同一作者还是他人旧稿。本文不判。

就本篇所述、明确讨论曹雪芹改写作用的三类主张而言，共同点是承认他在今本前八十回形成中的写作或改作作用：一书多改说他改的是自己的稿子；二书合成说他合的是两部稿子；他人旧稿说他改的是别人的稿子。胡适是基线，不设旧稿之层，不在此列。这里只概括所述主张的交集，不据此证明其中任何一种具体的成书过程。

本文只依赖的那一层有三段。前两段，即前八十回正文的主体经过层累、整合成书的是文本所称的曹雪芹，与此相合之处相应，又有题名串的书内自述可据（“纂成目录，分出章回”）；本项目依此作工作理解，只取文本角色一义。第三段“按下中心”，是本项目的工作判断，本篇不证，也不是三路之所合（第 1 节、第 7 节）。脂本与程本先后之争挑战的是材料的先后，本文只依赖的那一层，在程本一系也有所凭（第 7 节）。

5.2 各说要点

每说写主张、理由、未证之处。语气中立，只称提出者署名。

徐兴公说（廖一壶）。主张《石头记》的作者是明末福州藏书家徐𤊹（字兴公，1570—1642）。“曹雪芹”是笔名，三字拆作儒、释、道：曹指曹学佺，雪指福州雪峰寺，芹指福州芹山。人物原型多是明末福建文人，如谢肇淛之于宝钗，叶向高之于元春，邵捷春之于探春，张燮之于湘云，林宠之于黛玉。理由有三：徐兴公“逝世于壬午年除夕之前”，合于 1.63“壬午除夕”；作者须满足藏书、诗名、交游、文人身份、小说经历几条“硬标准”；名、号、诗句的逐一比附。未证之处：本文未查到廖氏之前有外部文献把《石头记》系于徐兴公；廖氏同时把《金瓶梅》《情史》等也归于徐兴公；关于后四十回，他有徐陵补写与“齐头并进”两说。“孔梅溪、吴玉峰”在他那里是“东吴”二字的拆字，指作者是福建人，不是实有的题者。（据豆瓣阅读试读段与 B 站专栏；知乎各篇仅见标题。）

方以智说（陈贤富）。据中新网 2011 年 10 月 31 日报道，陈贤富主张方以智是作者，“甚至曹雪芹都是他杜撰出来的”，书的用意是“吊明反清”。理由是：第一回的石头、情僧、曹雪芹三条作者线索，与方以智化名“吴石公”、梧州出家相关；“雪芹”谐音“学勤”；“红”暗示朱明，“月”暗示“明”。报道说他的研究尚未正式发表，拟写书；本文至今未查到成书。有人指出方以智卒于 1671 年，时代偏早（欧阳健转述）。方以智写、冒辟疆增删的合作之说另有流传，原提出人待核。

洪昇说（土默热）。主张洪昇初创，初名《风月宝鉴》等，后改《石头记》，曹雪芹是披阅增删者；“四大家族”原型为杭州洪、顾、黄、钱四家，蕉园诗社诸女为十二钗原型，西溪为大观园原型，脂砚斋是洪昇之妻黄蕙的化名（逄冠卿概括其“十论”）。未证之处：本文未查到洪昇撰写小说的直接记载。

吴梅村说。傅波、钟长山主张吴梅村原创，严绳孙化名曹雪芹增删；理由是“吴玉峰”“孔梅溪”“棠村”合起来隐含“吴梅（村）”，1.63 的壬午应是康熙壬午（1702），正是严绳孙卒年（据转载本，原刊待核）。吴称谋主张吴梅村原作，曹雪芹、程伟元、高鹗为编修者，自称“一家之言”。未证之处：拆合与改读都是解读，本文未查到外证。此说所据的一种抄本批语涉及癸酉本，留给专题三。

冒辟疆说。冒廉泉著《悬案不悬：冒辟疆著〈红楼梦〉七十三证》；顾浩认为成书于康熙甲戌（1694），理由有水绘园与大观园相合、如皋方言、悼明主题；黄伟民以林黛玉原型为董小宛。反方：严中指出书中第五十四回提及曹寅 1703 年所作《续琵琶》，冒辟疆 1693 年已卒。

曹頫说（陈林）。主张一百二十回《石头记》的作者是曹頫，曹雪芹是曹頫虚构的人物，己卯、庚辰等脂本是后人伪造（据报道转述）。所争涉及脂本真伪，本文照录，不判。

竺香玉说（霍国玲等）。主张书有“双重性质”，小说背后隐写历史，林黛玉原型是竺香玉；带脂批的八十回本即曹著之全璧，脂批核心部分出自曹雪芹之手。此说否认书是未竟之作，也就不需要续补之层。

明室及其他人物说。以作者为崇祯之子朱慈炤（“红楼隐史蝶恋花”），或朱慈烺、崇祯、胤礽、郑克塽等（欧阳健 2015 年列举，各说提出者待逐一核）。朱三太子等是被指认的对象，不是提出者。这一类以谐音为主要论据，如“贾王薛史”读作“家亡血史”。

秦学（刘心武）。刘心武认为秦可卿原型是康熙废太子胤礽之女，曹雪芹“不得不为躲避‘文字狱’而删改已写就的篇什”（转载）。复原本写秦可卿出身废太子一支，与此说相合。本项目未以刘著为据；刘说先出（2003）。同异留给专题八，这里先注明。

总评。严中 2015 年说，已出现 63 位“作者”（中新网 2015-11-30，这是他在那一年的统计）。彭光谦说，“真正的谜底只有一个，余下60多个无疑全都是假的”，主张以敦敏、敦诚、张宜泉、明义、裕瑞、永忠及脂批为准（澎湃 2022-03-01）。欧阳健则主张“多歧为贵”，“不要忙于作结论”（2015 年演讲，爱思想刊）。

5.3 与本文所依赖一层的关系

照第 1 节，“曹雪芹”取文本角色。以此看民间诸说，大体三类：

  • 以曹雪芹为增删者的，如洪昇说、吴梅村说，与本文所依赖的一层相容。
  • 以“曹雪芹”为笔名或虚构的，如徐兴公说、方以智说、冒辟疆说、曹頫说。它们与通行说的分歧在“此人是谁”，本文不参与这一争论，与本文所依赖的那一层不直接相冲，照述即可。
  • 以脂本为伪的，如陈林，所争的是材料的真伪；与第 4.4 节同样照录，不判。

这一节是披露。本项目在后台（项目总论、后台卷、政治背景卷）拼过一张层累工作图，用它想过不少事，其中一部分进了复原本。照作者的裁定，这里写工作图的结构、各层来源与复核结果；凡影响了复原本情节或字句的，逐条写；撤回的推定照写撤回。原型对应只在附录列来源表，不论证，不作证据。

6.1 后台的层累工作图

后台的工作图有六个阶段：徐兴公《石头记》，方以智《情僧录》，孔尚任《风月宝鉴》与吴玉峰群《红楼梦》，“西堂故兄”的《风月宝鉴》三段本，最后是曹雪芹。另有脂砚斋三代接力署名之说，以及四十余条福州文人圈的原型对应。

本文为写这一节，把这张图逐段溯源。结果如下。

第一，本轮所核各家材料中，未见有人提出这条完整的接力链；其连接关系为项目自组。各段分属互不相容的诸说。例如廖一壶的体系里只有徐兴公一个作者层，“孔梅溪、吴玉峰”在他那里是“东吴”二字的拆字，容不下方以智、孔尚任等中间层。个别节点有出处，不替节点之间的承接关系取得来源。

第二，有出处的部分。

  • 徐兴公作者说出自廖一壶。后台所用的原型对应中，林宠之于黛玉、马歘之于贾琏等，见于廖氏可读的文字；廖氏可读文字中这类对应有二十余组。后台另有若干对应记作廖一壶，如林古度之于袭人、倪范之于平儿、洪士英之于小红、佘翔之于贾赦，本轮在廖氏本人的文字中未见；附录三写作“来源未查到（后台曾记作廖一壶）”，不挂在他名下。作者说明：与徐兴公同时期的原型，基本都来自廖一壶；除湘云一项外，这些原型对复原本现行稿的写作没有产生影响。早先稿里据这类原型写过、后来撤去的，如龄官回福州批书的福州线、包勇一线，见第 6.3 节“已撤的”一组。这是作者对本项目写作经历的说明，不是对廖氏之说的判断。
  • 方以智作者说出自陈贤富。
  • 孔梅溪即孔尚任，出自张志坚（新浪博客，2011）。
  • “西堂故兄”一语，出自一种争议抄本的批语；这一抄本的问题留给专题三。

第三，项目自推的部分：“吴玉峰群”；方以智添加情榜与僧道；两个节气日期是方以智定稿的“签名”（第 6.2 节）。

第四，后台内部口径不一，有十余处未统一。例如：“悼明”归徐兴公还是吴玉峰群；畸笏叟是林宠，还是某种续书的续补者；干支纪年是否前移一甲子；总回数先作一〇八，后改一一〇。

第五，措辞。后台笔记里，以推测为定论的措辞约有三十处，如“铁证”“拍板”“解 200 年红学未解之谜”。本文不作这类措辞；引述时注明出处。后台自称“首次”“无人说过”的，公开前先做文献检索。

6.2 节气签名的撤回

后台曾把第十一回“十一月三十日冬至”、第二十七回“四月二十六日交芒种”，读作方以智于 1665—1666 年定稿的签名，并据此与前八十回的物件等，排出一张 1655—1674 年的全书时间表。

复算不合，撤回。本次以现代定气定朔复算（sxtwl 2.0.7，东八区），1665 年冬至在十一月十五日，而且这一年十一月是小月，没有三十日；1666 年芒种在五月初三日，比“四月二十六日”晚七天。所得日期不支持原推定，作者据此撤回。两处之差一在半月、一在七日，本项目不以历书原页为撤回的前提；当年《时宪书》的原页，只为存档。

即使将来找出某一年两个日期都吻合，也只说明故事可以安排在那一年，不能跨到“某人在那一年定稿”，更不能跨到“此人是作者”。

签名撤了，那张时间表还留下一样东西：复原本各回的年序、节令与虚岁，是照它排的；后台记第 95 回黛玉死于三月初三上巳，即注“依时间表”。前台不出年号，也不出方以智之名。所以时间表是以节令排期的形式进了前台；它现在只作写作坐标，没有另立的理由（附录四第 2 项）。细心的读者据各回节令，或可推回后台所设的年代；本项目不以此为故事年代或成书年代的主张。进了前台的另一样，是物件层的康熙底色，来路与理由见第 6.3 节。

6.3 哪些进了复原本

后台材料整理列了二十四项，每项标明已落、已撤或未进定稿，附录四逐条有账。正文择要，按层累之想起了什么作用分组。

大体只留后台的。绝大多数原型对应；林雪批书与脂砚斋三代接力之说；方以智的“四分身”；东安郡王的“改名遗痕”。

照作者的说明（第 6.1 节），廖一壶一系的原型，除湘云一项外，对复原本现行稿的写作没有产生影响。后台曾把第 109 回平儿屋前的杏树连到倪范“古杏轩”，把第 85 回探春远嫁得活连到邵捷春的“诗意补偿”；照作者的说明，这两处的写法不以原型为据，也记在这一组。

落到情节或字句的。

  • 第 109 回三和班排《桃花扇》。与孔梅溪即孔尚任之说相呼应，但终校要求正文不出孔尚任之名。
  • 第 110 回湘云的《敝舍赋》有“尝览《东西洋考》”一句，取的是张燮这一原型。就廖一壶一系的原型对应而言，这是现行稿里唯一影响了写作的一项。

这些原型启发了取材。它们进入小说，不反过来证明所拟原型就是原书人物。

字句已改、层累说法只留后台的。第 83 回贾母叮嘱贾政，复原本旧稿作“虚架子”，今改作外头的事不要只讲面子，免得贾母预知抄家。后台把这一改记作“保处即承认”：前作者保留的，后来者承认它。前作者保的是前八十回哪一处，本文未能写实，所以这一说法只留后台，不算层累之想直接影响取舍的一例。

层累推测用在非承重处的。秦可卿的重读。复原本第 110 回情榜秦可卿一条写：“世谓其以色败家，目之为淫；然其孽非色，乃政。”第 104、105 回又由宝珠口述、秦氏托梦补出她的背景。这一重读所据，是一个推测：曹雪芹本不认可《风月宝鉴》一层给秦可卿的“淫”的设定（总篇 3.5 节）。这是本项目自推的一个层数与层主之判。总篇 1.4 节已把它与“天香楼删节之由”一并列为非承重：倒了只伤局部。复原本写她出身废太子一支，与刘心武之说相合，已在第 5.2 节注明。

根据另立、层累之想只作旁助的。

  • 天香楼不补。根据是不可违：前八十回所写之事不改不补（总篇 3.5 节）。“删处即留白”的层累之想只作旁助。
  • 物件层取康熙底色、朝代架空。来路是后台，原与方以智一层相连：后台把它当作“判断前 80 回某个描写是不是方以智原稿层的标尺”。方以智一层与节气签名都已撤。现在的理由由作者给出：前八十回所写的玻璃、西洋药品、穿衣镜等，在书中是贵族才得享用的稀罕之物，本项目读作更近康熙年间的情形，不像乾隆时。这是本项目自己的读法与写作的依据，不作考证，也不在本篇作成书年代的论证。

已撤的。龄官回福州批书的福州线；以 1627、1628 两年映射元春与鸳鸯之死；王夫人“挡剪子”的双层读法；包勇一线；甄宝玉出家、送《玉石新编》。

不靠脂批之真也站得住。程本一系也有题名串中“曹雪芹于悼红轩中披阅十载，增删五次”一句（凭通行本，程甲原页待核）；程伟元序说“惟书内记雪芹曹先生删改数过”（通行转录，未见可指名的转录本）；前后参差都在正文。程本保留了书内自述，程伟元序又援引它，两者不算彼此独立的旁证。本文据此保留“整合成书的，是文本所称的曹雪芹”这一段工作理解，其历史归属仍可受检验；“按下中心”一段，本篇不证，不在此列。所以本文不参与真伪之争，是因为不必参与。

推翻条件。

  • 若有可靠证据表明，今存前八十回正文主体的整合者，不是题名串与脂批所称的那一位；上列已知的例外不计。

  • 任何一家对层数与层主的判定。主论点如此；非承重处用过一次，即秦可卿的重读（第 6.3 节）。

  • 任何材料的真伪之判。

8 结语

本文的结论可以说得很短。书有层累，材料在第 3 节，诸说在第 4、5 节，都照来路登记，不判对错。本文只依赖其中一层，并写明了它的例外、它的推翻条件，以及它不依赖什么。本项目在后台用过的层累猜测，第 6 节照实披露：哪些只是想过，哪些进了复原本，哪些撤了；有出处的写出处；本轮未查到的，照写未查到；只有后台明记为项目自立的，才写项目自拟。

读者应当看得清三件事：哪一句是原材料说的，哪一步是某位研究者的解释，哪一步是本项目自己的联想。一个联想撤回之后，小说里的哪些选择，仍由别的理由保留。守住这三步，本文就不必去赢一场作者身份之争。
