Exit and Switching
退出与改接

Han Qin (秦汉) · Independent Researcher · 2026
DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23815789 · Full PDF on Zenodo · CC BY 4.0
Abstract

When a member wants to exit an external arrangement, how is the exit reviewed, and by whom? This paper takes up the three questions handed over by the Theory of the State: how such an exit is to be reviewed, and by whom; whether a rule can also hand to the outside the decision to change that rule itself; and whether a state that has locked its exit in the hands of the outside is still at the state's end. The answer: exit is a change of structure in this state, questioned inside this state, and not decided alone by the position being questioned; the system can review and record, and may require a notice period and a transition period in the covenant, but cannot veto, and the notice period may not be made into a lock. Any arrangement that in fact acquires force over members is a superior, and locking exit uses the same criterion; where a member has set an external consent on its own exit, changing that consent is still its own affair, and the outside cannot stop it except by force. Four steps follow: exit does not write off the account, in three stages; a member is free to leave, but coming back needs consent, and re-accession neither washes the account clean nor lets a state come back bringing a colonization under way; colonizing after first exiting does not count as coming from the system, but there is no exiting, colonizing and then rejoining; the system may expel a member under strict conditions, expulsion does not write off the account and does not change where the colonizing comes from, and its cost is that, for those reached by that state's acts, the road of petition against its acts thereafter is closed within the system. When the system dissolves, it does its best to make follow-up arrangements, and what cannot be arranged is registered as a remainder. The paper opens with the DRP's exit from Article 14 of the Compact while it was being questioned, and draws on Stonegorge's exit and its application to rejoin, a hypothetical case in which the DRP sets an external consent on exit in its own law, a hypothetical case in which Stonegorge enters another state after its exit, and the proposal to expel the UCSE. It interfaces in the text with Grotius, and at the end converses with the "continuously free association" of Appendix II of Kant's Perpetual Peace. The paper has seven propositions (XZ45 to XZ51), each stated at the end of its section and listed together at the end.

Introduction

In the winter of Year 13, a court of the Democratic Republic of Purposes (DRP) sentenced two people, one to twelve years and the other to nine, on a charge of "collaboration with the enemy." They had been members of the Wheat Plain Provisional Self-Government Committee. That committee had been selected when the Office of Defense of the United City-States of Ends (UCSE) set up an administration office in the Wheat Plain buffer zone in the winter of Year 9; in the spring of Year 11 the two states signed the Withdrawal Agreement, and after the UCSE withdrew, nine of the committee's eleven members left with the UCSE forces and were granted asylum in the UCSE, while the two who stayed were prosecuted by the DRP (Theory of the State Paper 6, §4, has told this story). The trial was not public, and defense counsel was appointed by the court. In the summer of Year 15, the DRP's Supreme Court dismissed the appeal.

By then the DRP was already a member of the Three-Borders Cooperation System. The system was formed by the Three-Borders Compact (the Compact), which the three states, the UCSE, the Nation Union of Telos (NUT) and the DRP, signed at Three Borders in the spring of Year 13. The Stonegorge Commonwealth (Stonegorge) joined in the autumn of Year 14, as the fourth member. Article 14 of the Compact establishes the Three-Borders Petitions Committee: where a member's acts touch on the matters listed in Article 4 of the Compact and in Annex II, such as life, the person, and detention without trial, those affected may petition after exhausting the remedies within that member. What the Committee gives are opinions, not rulings; the member petitioned against answers in writing within six months, and the answer is made public. A member may declare its exit from this article; the declaration takes effect twelve months after it is made; petitions already submitted before it takes effect are heard and concluded as usual, and those submitted after it takes effect are not taken up. The articles of the Compact, the organs of the system, and the laws of the various states written of below are examples set up by this series; this series does not claim that they are the best design; they are here so that judgment has facts to rest on.

When the DRP's People's Assembly ratified the Compact in the autumn of Year 13, it took a separate vote to accept Article 14: 318 votes to 247. Article 14 took effect for the DRP from the spring of Year 14. In the spring of Year 16, through a lawyer engaged by their families and by one of the nine, the two petitioned the Committee against the DRP; the member of the Committee holding DRP citizenship did not take part in the deliberations. The ruling came before Article 14 took effect for the DRP; the dismissal of the appeal and the detention came after. The Committee took up the petition under clause 6 of Article 14, which allows it to take up acts from before entry into force whose effects continue after it. In the summer of Year 17, the DRP's Foreign Ministry answered in writing with a single sentence: "The trial was conducted in accordance with the laws of the Republic." That autumn, the Committee asked the DRP to submit the trial record and to allow a member of the Committee to visit the two in prison.

In the spring of Year 18, the DRP's Foreign Ministry, with the approval of the DRP President, declared its exit from Article 14 under clause 5 of that article. The declaration read: "Article 14 has been used to interfere in the Republic's administration of justice. The Republic will make no further answer on this petition." The DRP's National Purposes Commission issued an opinion: "Exit is consistent with the national purposes." The People's Assembly took no vote on the exit. Sixty-one members of the Assembly jointly demanded a vote, saying that "accepting Article 14 was voted on by the People's Assembly, and exit should also be voted on by the People's Assembly"; the Presiding Board of the People's Assembly did not put it on the agenda.

In the summer of Year 18, the Conference of Members passed a record by 3 to 0, the DRP not taking part in the vote: "While the Petitions Committee was putting questions to it on a petition, the DRP declared its exit from Article 14. Under clause 5 of Article 14, the declaration takes effect twelve months after it is made; petitions already submitted before it takes effect are heard and concluded as usual." The statement of the DRP's permanent representative was entered in the record: "Exiting an article is a member's right." The Committee went on hearing this petition and three other petitions already submitted against the DRP, and the DRP gave no further answer. In the winter of Year 18, the Committee gave its opinion on the case of the two: it asked the DRP to hold a public retrial and to allow visits. The DRP did not answer within six months; in the summer of Year 19, the Conference of Members recorded that no answer had come. After the exit took effect in the spring of Year 19, the Committee returned seven newly received petitions against the DRP, on the ground of clause 5 of Article 14: "Those submitted after it takes effect are not taken up." The UCSE's Office of External Affairs, through the liaison office at Three Borders, twice raised the situation of the two with the DRP's Foreign Ministry. As of Year 20, the two were still in detention, and one of them had been moved to the prison hospital.

This matter falls on several groups of people: the two from Wheat Plain; the petitioners of the seven petitions returned after the spring of Year 19; and everyone reached thereafter by the DRP's acts who could otherwise have petitioned against the DRP on the matters listed in Annex II. What must be looked at is the fifth of the five oughts: a road within reach by which the DRP can be questioned. Article 14 was exactly such a road, and it is exactly what the DRP exited. Before this, the DRP had accepted Article 14 for four years; the Committee had taken up the petition of the two under it; and the rule of exit was written in clause 5 of Article 14.

In this matter several things lie on top of one another. What the DRP exited was precisely the item under which it was being questioned. What decided the exit was the Foreign Ministry, the very body answering the questions, with the President's approval; the People's Assembly, which had voted to accept the article, did not vote this time. The system made a record and did not stand in the way. The two remain in detention, and the seven later petitions were returned.

The Theory of the State said in Paper 1, §5: "Exiting a treaty, ending or narrowing an arrangement admitting individual petitions, switching to another external body: all are decisions of this state's positions." It went on to ask: "How such decisions are to be reviewed, and by whom, whether by handing them to the outside to judge by rules set in advance, or by having one place at this level judge; whether a rule can also hand to the outside the decision to change that rule itself; whether a state that has locked its exit in the hands of the outside is still at this end of the definition: these are questions of how cooperation between states is arranged; this series acknowledges them as a remainder and hands them to the Theory of Inter-State Cooperation." In the same place it established only one sentence, that external questioning cannot be cancelled: this state "can close one channel, but it cannot cancel the questioning of it by other states, by the people of other states, and by those affected" (Theory of the State Paper 1, §5).

This paper takes up these three questions and goes further. §1 asks how exit is reviewed and by whom, and what authority the system has. §2 asks what a system is if it can in fact block exit, and what follows when a rule hands to the outside the authority to review its own repeal. §3 asks where the earlier account lies after exit. §4 asks how a member that has left comes back, and §5 what of one that first exits and then enters another state. §6 asks whether the system can expel a member, and §7 what the system leaves behind when it dissolves itself. §8 is a conversation with Kant.

Where judgment is required, this series speaks in three layers: the normative layer, the rule layer and the judgment layer; how to read them is set out in the Prequel, §11. In each section this paper states which sentence is a norm, which item is a suggested rule or an example, and which step is left to the judgment of those in positions. In judging a matter, this paper reads the facts by the norm, writes only as far as the side the facts point to, and states who made the judgment in that matter; how a particular act is judged under the rules is left to those positions.

§1 Exit Is Questioned Inside; the System Can Review It but Cannot Veto It

When a member wants to leave, who reviews it? The two easiest answers are both wrong. One says that the system reviews it, and that a member which does not pass the review may not leave; then the system holds a lock. The other says that this is the member's own affair, and that the position there which makes the decision to exit has the say; then a position that is being questioned can shut the questioning by exiting. In the DRP's case, both dangers were in plain view.

Exit is an act of this state. The Theory of the State says: "Suspending or exiting a treaty is itself an act of this state" (Theory of the State N24). The original sentence speaks of treaties between two states; a member's exiting an article of a multi-state system is the same kind of act, only the other side has changed from one state to several (own undertaking). Being an act of this state, it is a change of structure in this state, and is questioned inside this state. The road set out in Paper 1 of the Theory of the State applies here as it stands: where a decision concerns the position being questioned, that position does not decide it on its own.

Look at the DRP's exit in this light. The declaration came from the Foreign Ministry, which is precisely the position answering the Committee's questions; it was approved by the President. Acceptance of Article 14 was voted on by the People's Assembly; at the exit, the People's Assembly took no vote. Sixty-one members demanded a vote, and the Presiding Board did not put it on the agenda. Through which positions the decision to exit ought to pass under the DRP's law is not recorded, and this paper does not read the DRP's own law on the DRP's behalf. The facts point to only one thing: the decision to exit was made by the position being questioned; the road that would have had it questioned inside, the joint demand of the sixty-one members, stopped at the Presiding Board. This road is the DRP's internal affair. The system does not answer on behalf of the People's Assembly, nor does it close the road on behalf of the Presiding Board. Three positions made judgments in this matter: the Foreign Ministry and the President made the exit; the Presiding Board decided not to put the demand on the agenda; the Conference of Members recorded, 3 to 0, that the exit took place during questioning, the DRP not taking part in the vote and its statement being entered in the record.

The reason given in the DRP's declaration is that Article 14 "has been used to interfere in the Republic's administration of justice." This says that the covenant has been bent to a wrong use, and that the DRP may therefore cease to be bound by it. Grotius, writing of treaties, set down the same kind of reasoning: "If one of the parties violates a treaty, such a violation releases the other from its engagements. For every clause has the binding force of a condition." (The Rights of War and Peace, bk. II, ch. 15, §12, trans. A. C. Campbell, New York & London: M. Walter Dunne, 1901, Project Gutenberg EBook #46564; that edition is an abridged translation, and the Latin original has not been checked; tier B) He at once leaves a limit: where the parties have agreed otherwise, a treaty is not made void by small grievances and lapses (ibid., same section, paraphrased).

Grotius and this series ask about the same thing: a covenant is held between the parties; when one party says that the other has broken it, is it still bound? On this point the two go in the same direction. A covenant is held between the parties, and there is no court above to decide for them which side broke it first; the party bound may cease to be bound. They part in two places. Grotius says that a violation releases at once; this series says that, whatever the reason, exit is itself an act of this state, questioned inside this state, and that the system may require a notice period and a transition period in the covenant. Further, what is released is only the binding going forward, and procedures already begun are still concluded; that is the business of §3. Whether the "interference" the DRP speaks of took place: the Committee's opinions and the DRP's statements are both in the record, and readers can compare them; this paper does not make that judgment for anyone.

What the system can do. Stonegorge's exit was not entangled with any questioning under way, and so it shows the system's authority clearly.

In the autumn of Year 17, Stonegorge's Commonwealth Chamber was re-elected, and the new majority elected a new Chief of the Commonwealth. On taking office she said: "The system gave us a record, and Lamp Island is still in the Plateau Federation's hands." The sentence speaks of what had happened that spring: the Plateau Federation's fleet entered the Stonegorge Strait, landed on Lamp Island and set up a "sea-lane administration post"; the Conference of Members recorded: "There is a colonizing entry"; the UCSE sent forces to escort shipping and withdrew them after two periods of ninety days; the garrison on Lamp Island stayed (see Paper 5). In the winter of Year 17, the Commonwealth Chamber agreed, by 97 votes to 83, to give notice of exit, and the Chief of the Commonwealth notified the Secretary-General in writing under Article 25 of the Compact.

Article 25 of the Compact provides: written notice takes effect twelve months after it is given, and the six months that follow are a transition period; the accounts for sharing are settled; petitions already submitted are heard and concluded as usual. Clause 3: "Exit does not affect obligations arising from acts before the exit, nor procedures already begun." Clause 4: the Conference of Members may review a notice of exit and make a record, and may not veto it.

The opposition asked the Supreme Tribunal to interpret whether giving notice of exit also required two-thirds. In the spring of Year 18 the Supreme Tribunal replied: "The Fundamental Law prescribes two-thirds only for ratification." In the same season, the Conference of Members made a record by 3 to 0 under clause 4 of Article 25, Stonegorge not taking part in the vote. The record listed the matters under way at the time of notice: two petitions against Stonegorge taken up by the Petitions Committee, one concerning the rules on going out of the Eastfield camp, the other concerning an editor sentenced under the Anti-Division Act; Common Measures No. 1 and No. 2; and the settlement of sharing funds. The UCSE's permanent representative said: "The system cannot keep Stonegorge, nor does it intend to." The DRP's permanent representative said: "Exit is a member's right." The NUT's permanent representative asked whether the Stonegorge Pass Transit Agreement would continue as before after the exit, and Stonegorge answered that it would. The exit took effect in the winter of Year 18; in the autumn of Year 20, Stonegorge applied to rejoin, which is the business of §4.

This matter falls on the people of Stonegorge, including those represented by the eighty-three seats in the Commonwealth Chamber that voted against; on the petitioners in the two petitions; and also on the roughly two hundred ten thousand people in the Eastfield camp, whose sharing and transfer depended on the system's common procedures. The line to look at has two sides. On the system's side, it is the first of the five oughts, that the system's acts cross no line: the system does not act as a superior to its members, and so does not veto exit; and also the fifth: petitions already submitted must have someone to hear and conclude them as usual. On Stonegorge's side, it is that exit is questioned inside the state itself. Before this, there was Article 25 of the Compact, and there was the ratification at Stonegorge's accession: in the autumn of Year 14, the Commonwealth Chamber ratified accession by 121 votes to 59, where the Fundamental Law required one hundred twenty votes.

The direction the facts point to is clear. Inside Stonegorge, the exit went through a vote of the Commonwealth Chamber; the opposing side asked the Supreme Tribunal to interpret the threshold, and the Tribunal replied; the questioning ran its course inside, and the answer rested on Stonegorge's own law. In the system, the Conference of Members reviewed the notice, recorded the matters under way one by one, and did not stand in the way. The questioning inside and the system's review each stayed in its own place, and neither acted on the other's behalf.

A sample constitution in Appendix B of How Is Institution Possible (Foundational Paper 6; cited as F6) wrote another road for group exit: "The Federal Assembly may not unreasonably refuse an application for exit, but it has the right to negotiate the terms of exit. If negotiation fails, the Constitutional Court determines the terms of exit." (F6, Appendix B, Article 6(c); a working demonstration; used only as an example) There, the terms of exit end up in the hands of a court above. The Theory of the State did not take this road when it spoke of secession within a state; what it required is that "Neither side is final on its own" (Theory of the State N16). Between members and the system there is not even such a court above, and here too the road is not taken.

Conditions may not become a lock. A covenant may set conditions on exit: a notice period, the settling of accounts, a transition period. What if the conditions are not met? On secession within a state, the Theory of the State said: "unsettled matters do not keep exit from taking effect indefinitely" (Theory of the State N16). This sentence is extended to a member's exit from the system, the subject term changed from the seceding unit to the member (own undertaking). If the conditions are not met, the account remains, still recorded and still questioned; the exit still takes effect when its time comes. Where a covenant has no exit clause, the system cannot use "non-recognition" in place of a veto either: to refuse to recognize that a member has left, and then to require it to go on implementing common decisions as before, is the same thing as not letting it leave. Toward a member in the course of exiting, a freeze serves only the account and is not made a condition of exit; this was said earlier (Paper 4, §2; XZ22).

Conditions must also be checked from the opposite direction. The Theory of Government has said that the cost of exit can be high enough to amount to a lock: "'Possible in principle' does not mean 'affordable': exit costs can be high enough to be practically equivalent to lock-in." So "what must be checked is whether the party being left can cancel it on its own, by pricing or by approval" (Theory of Government G39). The original sentence speaks of a person's exiting an arrangement of a level of government; here it is extended to a member's exit from the system (own undertaking). The twelve months of Article 25 and of clause 5 of Article 14 of the Compact both have a fixed end; when the time comes, the exit takes effect, and the Conference of Members cannot extend the notice period indefinitely by approving or not approving. This rules out the kind of lock in which the party being left extends the period on its own; the actual burdens during the notice period, the existing conditions, and whether the matter can pass into a reviewable procedure must still be checked separately, and are not all judged settled because there is an end.

What the notice period did in the DRP's case is also written as it was. Within the twelve months, the Committee heard and concluded the case of the two as usual and gave its opinion; this case did not come to nothing because of the exit. But the notice period could not make the DRP answer: it did not answer within six months, and what the Conference of Members could do was record that. The notice period lets questioning under way run its course; it cannot make the party questioned speak. This is a cost of this system, which Paper 8 writes of (Paper 8, XZ54).

The Theory of the State has a requirement: "For every channel it must be stated who initiates it, which object it affects, whether the party questioned can close it, and how the account is booked when it fails" (Theory of the State N36). At the place of exit it is like this. Who initiates it: positions and persons inside this state; the system reviews and records. The object: exit, as a change of structure. The party questioned can close one channel, but cannot cancel the questioning (Paper 2, XZ11). When it fails, the account falls on the positions and persons that made the exit; if a notice period, a freeze, a condition or "non-recognition" in fact locks exit, it is read by the criterion of the next section.

It may be said that, if the system cannot veto, a member can use exit to evade questioning while it is being questioned, which is exactly what the DRP did; or that members can be pulled away one by one under pressure from outside the system, which is exactly how Stonegorge left. The weight of this objection lies in the facts: the DRP no longer answers, and seven petitions were returned; before Stonegorge left, Lamp Island was in someone else's hands, and after it left, it made a covenant with the Plateau Federation. But a veto could have saved neither. "Exit does not stand in for questioning." (Theory of Government G27) The DRP has left, the petitions already begun are still heard and concluded, and the questioning remains (§3). A veto needs force: if a member is not allowed to leave and there is no force at hand, the veto is only a sentence; with force, the system becomes the member's superior (§2). Members drifting away one by one under pressure is how a system fails in ordinary times; the War Treatise, speaking of preparation for war in ordinary times, asks that the channels of exchange stay open and that mutual recognition not be broken off (War Treatise §10). This failure must be recorded as it is and counted as a cost; it is no reason to give the system a lock.

Conversely, it may be asked whether the notice period itself might become an artificial cost of exit that holds the leaving party back. A notice period is not in itself a lock; nor does a fixed term by itself prove that exit can actually be carried through. Checked by the sentence of the Theory of Government above, these two places state that exit takes effect when the time comes and cannot be extended by the party being left on its own; the other conditions that could decide whether exit can actually be carried through are not set out item by item in the example, and no judgment on them is supplied here.

Some will also infer from this that, since members can exit, the system can be thin. This inference does not hold. Law Series P4 writes of the top level where exit is feasible, where what remains is consensus plus exit (Law Series P4 §2); here it is different: members can walk away from an arrangement, but not from one another. Of a treaty between two states, the Theory of the State says: "the agreement can be exited, the river cannot." (Theory of the State Paper 5, §1) Stonegorge has exited the system; the Stonegorge Strait and Lamp Island are where they were, and the people of the Eastfield camp are still there.

By the three layers of the Prequel, §11, this section divides as follows. No veto; the notice period may not be made into a lock; conditions do not keep exit from taking effect indefinitely: these belong to the normative layer. The twelve months and six months of Article 25 of the Compact, and the twelve months of clause 5 of Article 14, are examples at the rule layer, and this series does not claim that they are the best. How long the notice period and the transition period are to be in a particular covenant is left to the judgment of those in positions.

One thing is left here. Who inside the DRP ought to make the decision to exit for it to accord with the DRP's own law, and what became of the demand of the sixty-one members, is not recorded; the system cannot reach to answer this on the DRP's behalf, and this paper registers it as a remainder.

XZ45 Exit is questioned inside; the system can review it but cannot veto it. Exiting or switching an external arrangement is a change of structure in this state (Theory of the State N24, extended from two states to many; own undertaking); it is questioned inside this state, and is not decided alone by the position being questioned. The system may review and record, and may require a notice period and a transition period in the covenant, but cannot veto; the notice period keeps exit from being used to evade questioning under way, it may not be made into a lock, and a fixed end shows only that it cannot be extended by the party being left on its own. External review of an exit is one kind of external questioning, which this state cannot cancel, and so the system can review (Paper 2, XZ11); only force can block exit, and so the system cannot veto (XZ46). §2 Whatever Acquires Force over Members Is a Superior

§1 said that the system cannot veto exit. But what if a system can in fact block exit, or a member has itself handed the veto to the system? To answer this, it must first be made clear what force is.

How force is recognized. Force is substituting one's own judgment for the other party's judgment on its own affairs. Between the system and its members, the party substituted for is the member; the judgment substituted is whether, at this moment, the member implements a decision, and how. A decision made by majority under a covenant agreed beforehand lands through the member's own law, and whether it lands still rests with the member; what the system can do is question, record and withdraw cooperation, and it is not a superior (Paper 1, XZ1). Only what can force a decision to land over the member's own law is one. Withdrawing cooperation substitutes for no one's judgment: the member still decides for itself, only others no longer cooperate with it (Paper 4, XZ22). The same distinction holds in finance. Withholding part of the funds the system was to disburse to a member is withdrawing cooperation, which is a check; taking the member's own capital is disposing of the member's own things on its behalf, which is force. If an arrangement in fact does this, it is already a superior.

An arrangement that is already a superior answers to the requirements the Theory of Government places on a level of government: the multi-branch separation of powers, channels within reach of its internal subjects, and its own openness to questioning. It cannot evade these requirements with "it is only cooperation." This series does not forbid several states from combining into a larger state (the Prequel, §3); what it forbids is carrying force without acknowledging it.

Locking exit. Locking exit uses the same criterion. A lock needs force: when a member wants to leave and the system will not allow it, the only thing that can make it stay is a power that makes this one judgment in its place. An arrangement that locks by force is a superior, and has already reached the level the Theory of Government speaks of. The Law Series says: "Whoever controls exit controls the boundary of law." (Law Series P3 §6) The original sentence speaks of the general relation between law and exit; read between the system and its members, the party that controls a member's exit is deciding the boundary of that member's law, and is already above the member (own undertaking). Power Theory says of cultivating power: "The A of cultivating power likewise invests in maintenance, but does not cancel B's interrupt-right: B can leave" (Power Theory P6 §I). Moral Law P9 says that "institutional features that cannot compel retention establish exit as a right" (Moral Law P9 §4.2). These two sentences originally speak of power and of institutions toward persons; here they are extended to the system toward its members: the system may invest in maintaining cooperation, but cannot cancel a member's leaving (own undertaking).

What, then, is a "lock" that does not rest on force? A system says "we do not agree" to a member that wants to leave, but has no means to make it stay; this "do not agree" is only a question and a record. The member can leave, but it cannot cancel this question. The state is still at the state's end.

A rule that hands to the outside the authority to review its own repeal. Of the three questions handed over by the Theory of the State, the second is the hardest. If a rule can also hand to the outside the authority to review its own repeal, it is as if a layer had been added above this state: whether this rule is changed is, in the end, for the outside to say. The definition of the state's end speaks of having no superior with force overhead; then is a state that hands its own exit to external review still at this end? The answer here comes in two steps.

Step one: external review is one kind of external questioning. The system can review an exit: record it, record the matters under way, and ask its reasons. The system cannot veto. If a member hands to the system a veto over its own exit, the system does not accept it either. A system of cooperation that relies only on questioning, recording and withdrawing cooperation would, in accepting this veto, accept an authority it cannot make good; to really make it good would be force.

Step two: if a member, in its own law, has set an external consent on its own exit, that is its own law. To change it is a change of structure, questioned inside this state (Theory of the State N24). The outside cannot stop it except by force; and an arrangement that carries force is no longer within cooperation as this series speaks of it, but is a level of the Theory of Government. This step extends that sentence of the Theory of the State to an external consent a member sets on itself (own undertaking). Put the two steps together, and that question no longer needs to go outside to find a place whose word is final: questioning runs inside, and also outside; only force can block, and force is not here.

Take a hypothetical case for step two. What follows did not happen; it is only supposed. Suppose that, in the autumn of Year 13, the resolution by which the DRP's People's Assembly accepted Article 14 contained a further sentence: "The Republic's exit from this article requires the consent of the Conference of Members." This sentence is part of the DRP's own resolution, not an article of the Compact; clause 5 of Article 14 of the Compact stays as it is, and says nothing of the consent of the Conference of Members.

The supposed course of events is this. In the spring of Year 18, the People's Assembly amended that resolution by 322 votes to 251, with 27 absent, deleting the sentence. In the same month, the Foreign Ministry, with the President's approval, declared exit under clause 5 of Article 14; the People's Assembly still took no vote on the exit itself. In the summer of Year 18, the UCSE's permanent representative proposed in the Conference of Members a vote on "consent or not," under the sentence the Republic itself had written. He said: "The Republic itself once wrote that the consent of the Conference of Members was required." The DRP's Foreign Minister replied: "That sentence was written by the People's Assembly, and the People's Assembly has deleted it; the exit is under clause 5 of Article 14 of the Compact." The NUT's permanent representative said: "The system cannot use a state's own law to lock that state." The Secretary-General, who held DRP citizenship, was recused; the Deputy Secretary-General acting in that place gave a written opinion: "Clause 5 of Article 14 of the Compact says nothing of the consent of the Conference of Members." Whether it was put to a vote, and what the result was, this paper does not posit.

This hypothetical case falls on the DRP as a construct and on several positions inside it: the People's Assembly, the Foreign Ministry, the President; it also falls on the petitioners reached by the exit from Article 14, who are the people in the DRP's actual case. The line to look at is the one criterion used both for locking exit and for being a superior. Before this, that sentence was written in the DRP's own resolution, and the Compact did not have it; the People's Assembly deleted it by the Republic's own procedure; on the exit itself, the People's Assembly still took no vote.

Read by step two, the facts point this way. That sentence was in the DRP's law; deleting it was a change of structure in the DRP, which the People's Assembly made by 322 votes to 251, and this change was questioned inside the DRP. The exit itself did not pass through the People's Assembly; this point, as in §1, remains a remainder inside the DRP. If the Conference of Members votes on "consent or not," the vote itself is a question, recording what the DRP once wrote and what it later deleted, and this is within the system's authority. If the system used this sentence to block the exit, refusing to recognize that the DRP had left and still requiring it to be bound by Article 14 and to implement the decisions under it, it would be substituting its own judgment for the DRP's judgment on its own affairs: whether the DRP stays in Article 14 is precisely its own affair. If the system had no means to make the DRP comply, this "blocking" would leave only a record; if it did, the system would have acquired force over a member and become the superior it says it is not. Three positions make judgments in the hypothetical case: the UCSE's permanent representative who made the proposal, the Deputy Secretary-General who gave the written opinion, and the members who would vote. This paper posits no vote, and does not make the final judgment on their behalf.

Whether there is an exit clause is not the criterion. The same criterion also makes clear the difference between the Compact and a federal charter. Clause 2 of Article 2 of the Compact reads: "This Compact establishes no organ above the members. No organ of the system may exercise force against a member or against persons within a member's territory." Article 25 permits exit. When the UCSE ratified the Compact in the summer of Year 13, a member of the Chamber of Member Units chosen by the South Bay State said in the debate: "A country can exit the Three-Borders system after twelve months, yet on a State's exiting the UCSE the Charter has not a word." What he said was fact: Article 66 of the UCSE's Charter provides only for how new member units join, and has no provision on exit.

This remark is easily read as saying that one has an exit clause and the other does not, and that this is where the difference lies. But whether there is an exit clause is not the criterion; as §1 said, where a covenant has no exit clause, the system cannot use "non-recognition" in place of a veto either. The criterion is whether an arrangement has, or needs, force to hold members in. The UCSE is the level that carries force over its member units: Article 52 of the Charter establishes the Defense Force, and Article 53 permits the Defense Force to carry out tasks within member units' territory in three circumstances. A State's exiting the UCSE is secession, which takes the road of Paper 3 of the Theory of the State (Theory of the State N16), and is not within this series. The South Bay State's referendum in the spring of Year 11 and the joint committee that followed have been judged in Theory of the State Paper 3, §6, and serve here only as a contrast. Where the system parts from the UCSE is clause 2 of Article 2 of the Compact, not whether an article mentions exit.

It may be said that a system that cannot even block exit binds no one, and that to bind there must be a lock. The weight of this objection is that an arrangement anyone can leave seems to guarantee nothing. But binding does not rest on a lock. It rests on questioning, which the party questioned cannot close; on the account, which exit does not write off; on withdrawing cooperation; and also on coming back needing others' consent. The account and coming back are written in §§3 and 4 below. To want a lock is to want a superior; whoever wants a superior answers under the Theory of Government, and acknowledges it as a superior.

One thing is left here. When an arrangement has "in fact" acquired force, there will be cases at the boundary that are hard to judge. The method of checking follows the Prequel, §2: whether it makes the other party unable to live unless it does as it is told, or in fact takes away the other party's room to bear the cost and not comply; and in the same place it is stated which party, and which judgment, is substituted. How a particular matter is to be judged is left to the judgment of those in positions.

XZ46 Any arrangement that in fact acquires force over members is a superior; locking exit is one case. Force is substituting one's own judgment for the other party's judgment on its own affairs; here the party substituted for is the member, and the judgment is whether, at this moment, the member implements a decision, and how. A decision made by majority under a covenant agreed beforehand lands through the member's own law; the system can only question, record and withdraw cooperation, and is not a superior (Paper 1, XZ1); only what can force a decision to land over the member's own law is one. Withdrawing cooperation does not substitute for judgment (Paper 4, XZ22); withholding from disbursements is a check, and taking a member's own capital is force. An arrangement that has acquired force answers to the requirements the Theory of Government places on a level of government (the multi-branch separation of powers, channels for internal subjects, openness to questioning), and cannot evade them with "it is only cooperation." Locking exit uses the same criterion: a lock needs force, and an arrangement that locks by force is a superior (Law Series P3 §6, Power Theory P6 §I and Moral Law P9 §4.2 extended to the system toward its members; own undertaking); a "lock" that does not rest on force is only an external questioning that the member can walk away from but cannot cancel, and the state is still at the state's end. To a rule that hands to the outside the authority to review its own repeal, the answer comes in two steps: external review is one kind of external questioning; the system can review but cannot veto, and if a member hands the veto to the system, the system does not accept it; if a member, in its own law, has set an external consent on its own exit, that is its own law, and changing it is a change of structure, questioned inside this state (Theory of the State N24); the outside cannot stop it except by force, and what carries force is no longer within this series' domain (own undertaking). Whether there is an exit clause is not the criterion; the criterion is whether force is had, or needed, to hold members in. When an arrangement has in fact acquired force is, at the boundary, left to the judgment of those in positions, and in the same place it is stated which party, and which judgment, is substituted.

§3 Exit Does Not Write Off the Account

The DRP's permanent representative said: "Exiting an article is a member's right." This is not wrong, and the DRP did indeed leave: after the spring of Year 19, the Committee no longer took up new petitions against it. But the two are still in detention, and the Committee's opinion of the winter of Year 18 is still waiting for an answer. After exit, where is the earlier account?

Leaving and the account must be kept apart. What goes away is binding force going forward; the account is earlier acts. Institution Theory says: "past residue is not written off by closing off questioning" (Institution Theory Z35a). F6, speaking of continuity when a member exits, says: "Exit terminates the current answering load that the position supports; it does not by itself clear facts of prior performance, nor cancel other handling positions that have independent grounds." (F6 §3.3) The Conflict Treatise, speaking of two parties after they part, says: "Where shared resources, third-party responsibility or procedural obligation remain, departure does not close those accounts and can only pass to arrangement or arbitration." (Conflict Treatise §25) These three sentences originally speak of institutions, of member positions, and of two first-person laws; extended to a member's exit from the system, what changes is the subject term, and the step of extension is from "closing off questioning, leaving a position" to "exiting a multi-state arrangement" (own undertaking). The sample charter in Appendix A of the same F6 wrote: "After exit, the civilization is no longer bound by this Charter" (F6, Appendix A, Article 6(b); a working demonstration; used only as an example). Read together with the three sentences above, it speaks only of binding force going forward. Binding force stops going forward; the earlier account is not written off.

That exit does not write off the account is seen in three stages, by time.

Stage one: acts during membership are still recorded. The detention of the two took place in the years when the DRP was a member and Article 14 was in force for it; it is booked under the DRP's name, and exit does not strike it out. Stonegorge was still a member during its notice period, and its acts in that year are still recorded too. In the spring of Year 18, under Article 7(c) of the Compact, it explained publicly that it would no longer take part in Common Measure No. 1, giving as its reason that "Stonegorge is negotiating with the Plateau Federation over Lamp Island"; the Federal Clearing Bureau's assets frozen in Stonegorge's banks were released accordingly, and port services for the Mirror Sea Fleet at the harbor at Gorgemouth were restored. Article 7(c) permits a member not to take part in a given common measure, provided that it explains its reasons publicly to the Conference of Members; Stonegorge took this road, and its reasons were entered in the record. In the same period, the Petitions Committee put questions to it twice on the Eastfield camp petition, and it answered neither time; this was recorded too. The account is booked under Stonegorge's name, and is also booked to the positions that made these decisions.

Stage two: procedures already begun are still concluded. Before the DRP's exit took effect, the Committee went on hearing the case of the two and the three others; in the winter of Year 18 it gave its opinion on the case of the two, asking the DRP to hold a public retrial and to allow visits. The DRP did not answer, and in the summer of Year 19 the Conference of Members recorded that no answer had come. Stonegorge's two petitions were heard and concluded under Article 25, opinions were given in Year 19, and Stonegorge did not answer. The procedures ran their course; the answers did not come. That procedures are still concluded does not guarantee that the party questioned will speak.

Stage three: whether new procedures can be opened after exit on acts during membership follows the acknowledged covenant. Where the covenant permits, the covenant is followed; where it does not, that road within the system is closed, but questions from peers and other channels go on as before, and the account is not written off. Clause 5 of Article 14 of the Compact, "those submitted after it takes effect are not taken up," is a plain case of the covenant not permitting. After the spring of Year 19, seven new petitions were returned, and that road within the system was closed. But the UCSE's Office of External Affairs, through the liaison office, twice raised the two with the DRP's Foreign Ministry, and questions from peers went on as before.

Clause 5 must be read together with clause 6. At the entrance, clause 6 allows the Committee to take up acts from before entry into force whose effects continue after it, and that is how the petition of the two was taken up. At the exit, clause 5 takes up nothing submitted after exit takes effect, no matter when the act took place. The entrance is wide, the exit narrow. This asymmetry is how this example is written, and this series does not claim that it is the best; the stretch it closes is registered as a remainder as it stands, and is not written as though a road remained.

Stage three can also land at the door. Paper 6, §3, wrote a hypothetical case: in the spring of Year 18, a commander of the Stability Squads of the Republic of the Sandbars (the Sandbars), with two of his men, crossed the Grey River, surrendered his weapons to Stonegorge's Coastal Guard and applied for asylum; people in the camp recognized him and said that it was he who had led the burning of villages in the spring of Year 16; the Sandbars' Whitewall government and the Provisional Assembly in the north both demanded him. Suppose that when Stonegorge's exit took effect in the winter of Year 18, his asylum application was still undecided. Read by this section, clause 3 of Article 25 of the Compact says that exit does not affect obligations arising from acts before the exit, nor procedures already begun; the commitment of not pushing back in Article 4(2) of the Compact no longer binds Stonegorge by way of the Compact thereafter. But not pushing back at the door never rested on the Compact; it rests on the unified sentence: those whom this state's refusals of entry and returns reach, and who have no way to avoid them by exiting, are, as regards those acts, in this state's interior (Theory of the State N15). Direction one, which Paper 6, §3, set up on this basis, is that those who have crossed the line are not pushed back when they reach the door either (Paper 6, XZ38). Exit does not change this. How Stonegorge deals with him thereafter, this paper does not posit.

What stops going forward. What stops going forward is the rights newly acquired or exercised as a member; rights and procedures that have already arisen and continue under the covenant follow the original covenant. After exit, a state can no longer initiate new procedures under the covenant as a party to it. Stonegorge's transition period is an instance. The Conference of Members paid in full, as the covenant required, the sharing funds for the Eastfield camp for the first half of Year 19, 240 million accounting units; transfers under Sharing Decision No. 4 were carried out until the summer of Year 19. These continued under the covenant and ran their course under the original covenant. From the winter of Year 18, Stonegorge could no longer, as a member, request that questions be put or open common procedures.

It may be said that exit means no longer being bound by the covenant, and that still requiring a member to answer for earlier matters amounts to not letting it leave. This is just what the DRP's statement meant. The weight of this objection is that, if exit carries endless old accounts with it, leaving remains a name only. But leaving and the account are two things. The DRP's exit took effect on schedule, and the system did not stop it. The case of the two is still concluded; it was submitted before the exit took effect, and under clauses 4 and 5 of Article 14 the DRP must still answer it in writing within six months. If it does not answer, the system records that. The system cannot compel it to answer; this is a cost of this system, not a writing off of the account (Paper 8, XZ54).

It may also be said that, when the covenant does not permit new procedures, this amounts to writing off the account. It does not. What is closed is that one road within the system; the account is still in the record, in the questions of peers, and also in the time when the member wants to come back (§4).

One thing is left here. By what road do the two put their questions from now on? The same goes for the petitioners of the seven returned petitions. In the system, that road is closed; what remains are the channels inside the DRP and the questions of peers. This paper registers this as a remainder as it stands, and does not write it as though a road already existed.

XZ47 Exit does not write off the account. Acts before exit are still recorded, procedures already begun are still concluded, and questioning between peers goes on as before (Theory of the State N38). Past residue is not written off by closing off questioning (Institution Theory Z35a); exit terminates the current answering load, and does not by itself clear facts of prior performance (F6 §3.3); shared resources and procedural obligations are not closed out by departure, and can only pass to arrangement or arbitration (Conflict Treatise §25, originally of two first-person laws after they part, extended to a member's exit; own undertaking). The sample's "no longer bound after exit" speaks only of binding force going forward: binding force stops going forward, and the earlier account is not written off. Three stages (own undertaking): (1) acts during membership are still recorded; (2) procedures already begun are still concluded; (3) whether new procedures can be opened after exit on acts during membership follows the acknowledged covenant: where the covenant permits, the covenant is followed; where it does not, that road within the system is closed, but questions from peers and other channels go on as before, and the account is not written off. What stops going forward is the rights newly acquired or exercised as a member; rights and procedures that have already arisen and continue under the covenant follow the original covenant; after exit, a state can no longer initiate new procedures under the covenant as a party to it. The stretch closed when the covenant does not permit new procedures is registered as a remainder as it stands, and is not written as though a road remained.

§4 Free to Leave; Coming Back Needs Consent

A member is free to leave; can it come back? Stonegorge later wanted to come back. This time the door it knocked on was one it had closed itself.

During the notice period, in the summer of Year 18, Stonegorge and the Plateau Federation signed the Lamp Island Agreement. The Plateau Federation would withdraw its garrison from Lamp Island within sixty days, the administration post would close, and no troops would be stationed on Lamp Island thereafter; the Mirror Sea Fleet would pass through the Southern Lane "on twenty-four hours' advance notice, exempt from registration"; the Plateau Federation would lease a supply wharf at the harbor at Gorgemouth for twenty-five years; "Stonegorge shall not take part in any multilateral economic measures directed against the Plateau Federation"; and the Plateau Federation would revoke External Economic Security Order No. 7, by which it had cut off fuel to Stonegorge, raised tariffs on it and stopped its clearing. The Commonwealth Chamber ratified the agreement by 124 votes to 56. In the autumn of Year 18, the garrison on Lamp Island withdrew and the administration post closed; of the roughly one thousand seven hundred people who had moved out, about one thousand one hundred had returned to the island by Year 19.

In the spring of Year 20, Stonegorge's Commonwealth Chamber passed a motion of no confidence, early elections were held, and the new majority elected a new Chief of the Commonwealth. That autumn, Stonegorge applied to rejoin. Article 26 of the Compact provides that re-accession is a new admission under Article 7(a), and that the members may put forward conditions; Article 7(a) requires the written consent of all members. In the discussion, the UCSE's permanent representative raised three items: how the clause of the Lamp Island Agreement on "not taking part in any multilateral economic measures" was to be dealt with; the two petitions left unanswered during the period of exit; and the relation between the supply wharf at the harbor at Gorgemouth and joint defense. The NUT's permanent representative said: "Conditions are to be written out, with acts as the measure." The DRP's permanent representative said: "No further conditions should be set; leaving is a right, and so is coming back." Stonegorge's representative said: "The Lamp Island Agreement was made with the Plateau Federation, and Stonegorge cannot delete or alter it unilaterally; Stonegorge is willing to explain, item by item, for every future common measure, whether it will take part, and to reply to those two petitions." The Plateau Federation's Ministry of Foreign Affairs declared: "Stonegorge's going back to that system is a violation of the Lamp Island Agreement." By the winter of Year 20, the matter was undecided.

On whom this matter falls, §1 has already written. With re-accession, the lines to look at gain the two floors described below; and before this, there is now also Article 26 of the Compact.

Coming back is a new covenant. Setting up new binding requires the consent of every party (Paper 1, XZ1); re-accession is new binding too, and the parties may set conditions. This is a check, not force. A member that does not consent to admitting Stonegorge decides nothing on Stonegorge's behalf on its own affairs; it only declines to make this new covenant with it. Conditions may be set, but being able to set conditions does not mean that anything at all may be set. This series establishes only two floors. One: re-accession does not wash the account clean; acts during the period of exit are still recorded and still questioned. The other: a state may not come back bringing a colonization under way. Conditions above the floor are left to the political leaders of the parties.

No permanent bar is set against the name of a state. What a state did before stays on the account; whether it can come in today depends on its acts today. The Theory of the State says that whether a state's supreme rule thereafter is legitimate is checked by the present, and "is not decided by its origin" (Theory of the State N34). The original sentence speaks of the other side's rule after an occupation; here it is extended to the door of re-accession (own undertaking). A state that applies at first accession with a line-crossing under way, and an observer member's seat, were written of in Paper 1, §9 (Paper 1, XZ7), and are not written again here.

Stonegorge's application read by the two floors. The facts point to three things.

First, the two petitions fall on the first floor. Stonegorge did not answer during the notice period, nor after the opinions came out in Year 19; the account is booked under its name, and does not vanish with re-accession. It says it is willing to answer, which is its response to the account. Whether it must answer before it is admitted, or may answer after admission, is a condition above the floor, and is left to political leaders.

Second, that clause of the Lamp Island Agreement is a covenant from outside the system following a member in and out of the system. Read by the first point of Paper 4, §5: the clause requires Stonegorge to promise in advance that it will take part in no future common measure directed against the Plateau Federation; this fixes "taking part or not" in advance and promises it to the other party to the covenant, so that to take part in any measure thereafter, Stonegorge must first break the covenant or obtain the other party's consent; it has the same form as Article 14, which was deleted from the NUT's agreement, and the facts point to the side of cancelling the interrupt-right. Stonegorge's saying that it is willing to explain, measure by measure, whether it will take part takes another road, one that Article 7(c) of the Compact already provides: a member that does not take part in a given measure explains its reasons publicly, and the reasons are entered in the record. This is the road the NUT took after deleting Article 14 (Paper 1, §6), and its permanent representative has said where the two roads differ: "Article 14 would have been a promise made to the Plateau Federation; not taking part in that item is something explained to the Conference of Members." Whether this clause can become a condition of re-accession, and how it is to be dealt with, is judged by the members who vote on re-accession, and the account falls on the positions and persons that put forward conditions and vote.

Third, the relation between the supply wharf and joint defense lies above the floor, and is left to political leaders.

Those who make the judgment in this matter are the members. Whether each member consents, and what conditions it puts forward, are its own acts, and the account falls on the positions and persons that put forward conditions and vote. The Plateau Federation's declaration entered this process; the Plateau Federation is outside the system, and how its covenant and its declaration are to be read is checked under Paper 4, §5.

First accession is the same. When Stonegorge first applied, in the spring of Year 14, admission likewise required the written consent of all. The DRP delayed its consent for five months; in the meantime the Plateau Federation issued a declaration of opposition, the Federal Clearing Bureau wrote to three of Stonegorge's banks saying that it would conduct a "compliance review" of their clearing, and the Plateau Federation signed a long-term fertilizer contract with the DRP at fifteen percent below the previous year's market price (Paper 1). First accession and re-accession both rest on the consent of the members, and both times the covenants and declarations of a state outside the system came in. Both are checked under Paper 1, XZ1 and Paper 4, XZ25, and no separate doctrine is set up. The hypothetical case for the second floor is in the next section.

The DRP said: "Leaving is a right, and so is coming back." The weight of this objection lies in symmetry: since leaving needs no one's consent, coming back would seem to need none either. But the two are not symmetrical. Leaving is this state's own change of structure, questioned inside this state, and needs no one else's consent (§1); coming back is a new covenant, a matter of states with one another, and needs others' consent. They are asymmetrical precisely because the one is a state's own affair and the other a matter of states with one another. The Law Series says: "Exit is not a failure of law; it is the normal process of law receding and reconstituting when the conditions of its existence change." (Law Series P3 §6.1) Leaving and coming back are read as law's receding and reconstituting when conditions have changed. The covenant of reconstitution settles the one matter it is to settle, and does not rewrite the years of absence: "Consensus does not ratify the past. It closes out this one incompatibility now; it does not rewrite what either party did before." (Conflict Treatise §24) The Conflict Treatise originally speaks of consensus between two laws; here it is extended to the covenant of re-accession (own undertaking).

It may also be said that setting conditions is using re-accession to put pressure on others. Conditions that are written out, that go by acts and the account, and that are not aimed at forms of government or the names of states, are not pressure. Only conditions that require the other party to change an autonomous choice of its own that crosses no line must be checked under the second point of Paper 4, XZ25.

By the three layers: the two floors are at the normative layer. Whether an act counts as a colonization under way is recognized by the act, and at the boundary it is left to the judgment of those in positions. Article 26 of the Compact, which permits members to put forward conditions, is an example at the rule layer. What conditions in particular to put forward, and whether to admit, are at the judgment layer, and the account falls on the positions and persons that put forward conditions and vote.

Two things are left here: where the gauge for conditions above the floor lies, this paper does not set; Stonegorge's application was still undecided as of the winter of Year 20.

§5 First Exit, Then Colonize

A member first exits, then enters another state by armed force, then applies to come back. How does the system read this sequence? Among the events that have happened there is no such case. What follows is a hypothetical case, written apart from the events above.

The Grey River flows from south to north into the Mirror Sea; its lower course, about one hundred forty kilometers, is the boundary river between the Republic of the Sandbars and Stonegorge, with the Sandbars on the west bank and Stonegorge on the east. From the spring of Year 18, the Provisional Assembly of the Republic, convened at Sandmouth, controlled Sandmouth Province, North Strand Province and the northern half of West Shoal Province, and the Whitewall government fought on in the southern half of West Shoal Province. Suppose that, after Stonegorge's exit took effect, in the autumn of Year 19, about one thousand of Stonegorge's Coastal Guard crossed the Grey River into a ferry-crossing area along the river in the northern half of West Shoal Province, about thirty square kilometers in extent, set up a "Ferry Crossing Administration Post," collected crossing fees, and registered those passing through. The Provisional Assembly in the north protested. In the autumn of Year 20, Stonegorge applied to rejoin with this administration post still in place.

This hypothetical case falls on the people in the crossing area and on those passing through; the fees and the registration both fall on them. There are three lines to look at: a colonizing entry, that is, entering another state by force and deciding on behalf of the people there; where the colonizing comes from (Paper 5, XZ29); and the second floor of re-accession, written in the previous section. Before this, Stonegorge's Coastal Guard was on the east bank of the Grey River, and Stonegorge's exit had taken effect in the winter of Year 18.

Step one: look at where it comes from. The entry took place when Stonegorge was not a member, and does not count as coming from the system. The system's joint defense is divided by where the colonizing comes from, and can be used in three slots: outside the system against a member, member against member, and a member against outside the system (Paper 5, §3); for the system, this entry is outside the system against outside the system, not within the three slots, and joint defense does not take it up. Not taking it up is not doing nothing: putting questions, recording, sanctions, receiving those who leave: these indirect means remain open; how far they are used is left to the political leaders in their positions. Why not reach back? If one reached back, whatever a state that had once been a member did thereafter would forever "come from the system"; leaving would then never have been accomplished, and the freedom to leave spoken of in §1 would come to nothing.

Step two: look at the account. Whatever part was prepared within the system before exit, or made use of the system's arrangements, if there is any, is still booked to the account. Whether the Coastal Guard used any of the system's joint plans in this entry is not recorded. What is recorded is something that actually happened: the prepositioned logistics depot at the harbor at Gorgemouth, which Stonegorge had asked the Joint Defense Coordination Office to remove before its exit took effect, was removed in the winter of Year 18. This section only sets the direction, and does not judge whether there was any such part.

Step three: look at the floor. Applying to rejoin in the autumn of Year 20 with the administration post in place falls on the floor that a state "may not come back bringing a colonization under way." While the administration post is there, the floor blocks the way here, and the matter is not left to political leaders to weigh; only after withdrawal, and after the account has landed, do the conditions for coming back lie above the floor (§4). Whether this entry is a colonizing entry is recognized by the acts: entering by force, setting up an administration post, collecting crossing fees, registering those passing through; how it is recognized at the boundary is left to the judgment of those in positions. Article 4(1) of the Compact is a commitment by members: not to enter the territory or territorial waters of any state by armed force, and not to set up administration within another state. After its exit, Stonegorge is not bound by it; to come back, it must acknowledge it again.

Step four: look at how large the gap is. If a state first exits and then acts, the system's joint defense does not take it up, and this looks like a gap. The gap is narrowed by Paper 5, §5 (Paper 5, XZ30): whichever of the three slots it is in, each participating state that sends forces must have its own first face hold, that is, its own cultivation must be under erosion, and that erosion must come from the same source as the oppression suffered by the side being defended (War Treatise §13, Gate Four); whether a member or not, what differs is only prior coordination and preparation of the kind of joint plans and prepositioned logistics. Membership never automatically gave anyone grounds for starting a war. The matter of the Outer Sandbars, written in §6, serves as a contrast: that entry took place during membership, and so it still counts as coming from the system after expulsion. In both places it is recognized by when the colonizing took place.

It may be said that, if a state first exits and then acts, the system cannot deal with it, and that this is a gap. The weight of this objection is real: that bit of prior coordination and preparation is indeed gone. But the gap has been narrowed; that state cannot come back; the indirect means remain open; and what was borrowed before exit is still booked to the account. Conversely, some say that one should reach back, and that whoever has been in the system counts even after leaving. Step one has answered this: if one reaches back, leaving comes to nothing.

Two things are left here: how far the indirect means are used is left to the political leaders in their positions; whether preparation before exit borrowed the system's arrangements is not recorded in this hypothetical case.

XZ49 Colonizing after first exiting does not count as coming from the system; there is no exiting, colonizing and then rejoining. Joint defense recognizes whether a state was a member when the colonizing took place, and does not reach back; reaching back would make the freedom to leave come to nothing (XZ45). The part prepared within the system before exit, making use of the system's arrangements, is still booked to the account. Colonizing begun after exit is, for the system, outside the system against outside the system; joint defense does not take it up, the indirect means remain open (putting questions, recording, sanctions, receiving those who leave), and how far they are used is left to the political leaders in their positions. Applying to rejoin bringing a colonization under way is blocked at the second floor of re-accession (XZ48), and is not left to political leaders. The gap here is narrowed by Paper 5, XZ30: whichever colonizing entry forces are sent to stop, the sending state's own first face must hold; whether a member or not, the only difference is prior coordination and preparation.

§6 Expulsion

Can the system expel a member? The Theory of the State established one sentence about persons: "no expulsion: the state does not use exclusion by judgment as punishment or elimination" (Theory of the State N14); exclusion by judgment is judging a person out of membership. That was said by the state about its people. Whether a framework of cooperation can expel its member states, the upstream has not established. The matter of the Outer Sandbars put this question before the system.

The Outer Sandbars are an island group of the Republic of the Sandbars, in the Mirror Sea about sixty kilometers north of Sandmouth, with about two thousand three hundred inhabitants, who live mainly by fishing. In the summer of Year 17, the Sandbars withdrew the islands' garrison to the northern mainland, and most of the officials on the islands left with it. In the autumn of Year 17, a meeting of the UCSE's Executive Council decided on deployment by 6 to 3; two vessels and a company landed on Great Outer Sandbar, and the UCSE's Office of Defense set up the "Outer Sandbars Provisional Administration Office." The administration office registered the residents and issued an "Outer Sandbars residence card"; collected fishing fees from boats fishing in Outer Sandbars waters; selected seven residents to form an advisory council; replaced the Sandbars textbooks in the islands' primary school with "provisional textbooks" printed in Rivermouth City; and imposed a curfew at night. A group of fishermen protested on the quay under a banner reading "Island matters are for island people to decide," and three of them were held by the administration office for two days.

In the spring of Year 18, the Conference of Members made its first record: "The UCSE has entered the Outer Sandbars of a state outside the system with military force, set up a provisional administration office, issued residence cards, collected fishing fees, and replaced school textbooks." The vote was 3 to 0, the UCSE not taking part. In the summer of Year 18, the Conference of Members made a second record under clause 1 of Article 18 of the Compact: "There is a colonizing entry." The NUT and Stonegorge voted in favor, and the DRP abstained. That same summer, the DRP proposed suspending the UCSE's right to vote in the Conference of Members. The NUT opposed the proposal, its permanent representative saying: "Suspending its vote will not make the administration office withdraw. The NUT wants it to sit here and answer." Stonegorge abstained, and the proposal did not pass. This course of events has been written in Paper 4, §7, and in Paper 5.

Article 27 of the Compact governs suspension and expulsion. Suspending a member's right to vote in the Conference of Members requires the consent of three-quarters of the other members. Expulsion requires all three of the following: the Conference of Members has made at least two records on the same act of that member; within six months from the first record, the member has not stopped that act; and all the other members consent, the decision stating its reasons. Clause 4: "Suspension and expulsion do not release the member from obligations arising from its earlier acts." This article, like the other articles of the Compact, is an example, and two places where it does not agree with this series' direction must also be pointed out: its suspension suspends only the right to vote, which is narrower than this series' direction (Paper 4, XZ27); its conditions for expulsion are not limited to line-crossing, which is wider than this series' direction.

By the autumn of Year 18, there were two records on the Outer Sandbars, of the spring and of the summer of Year 18, the administration office had not withdrawn, and the first two requirements were met. The DRP proposed expelling the UCSE. The DRP's permanent representative said: "Article 4(1) of the Compact is the first commitment of this covenant. It is also the one the UCSE cited when it replied to the Republic in Year 13." The NUT's permanent representative said: "Once it is expelled, has what it did on the Outer Sandbars nothing more to do with the system? It went in while it was in the system." And further: "Expulsion pushes it outside the system; outside, no one can question it any more." Stonegorge's permanent representative said: "Clause 4 of Article 27 says that expulsion does not release the member from obligations arising from its earlier acts. What expulsion releases it from, and what remains, the Compact does not say." The UCSE's permanent representative said: "Expel a member, and the system becomes the kind of thing it says it is not." At the vote there were three members besides the UCSE: the DRP voted in favor, the NUT against, and Stonegorge abstained. Expulsion requires the consent of all, and it did not pass. The Provisional Assembly at Sandmouth wrote to the Secretary-General asking for expulsion; the Whitewall government declared: "Whether the system expels its own member has nothing to do with the Republic; the Outer Sandbars belong to the Republic." In the spring of Year 19, the UCSE announced that the administration office would "gradually hand civil affairs over to the advisory council." From then until Year 20, no member raised expulsion again, and the administration office remained.

This matter falls on the roughly two thousand three hundred people on the Outer Sandbars, the three fishermen who were held among them; and also on those reached by the UCSE's acts thereafter who can petition against the UCSE under Article 14 of the Compact. There are three lines to look at: the means the system can use toward members, written in Paper 4, XZ27; the fifth of the five oughts, that is, these people's road of petition; and where the colonizing comes from (Paper 5, XZ29). Before this, there were Article 27 of the Compact and its clause 4, and the two records of the spring and the summer of Year 18.

What expulsion is. Expulsion, as this series speaks of it, is directed at the member state as a construct, and means the termination of its membership. People do not go out with it: neither the UCSE's people nor the people on the Outer Sandbars are expelled because the UCSE is expelled. Expulsion, like suspension, is withdrawing cooperation: the system no longer works with the member in common procedures, and decides nothing on its behalf on its own affairs. The affairs of the Outer Sandbars are still decided by the UCSE, and its room to bear the cost and not comply remains. By the criterion of force, expulsion cannot substitute for the UCSE's judgment, and it is not force (the reading of Paper 4, XZ27).

The system may expel a member, and the conditions must be strict; how strict is not something philosophy can answer, and it is left to political leaders. What philosophy can give is the direction of the reasons and two floors.

The direction of the reasons is line-crossing. Line-crossing is of two kinds: colonizing another state; and crossing the line against one's own people, such as massacre, torture, detention without trial, and the driving people out on a large scale. The latter rests on covenants the member has acknowledged, and is aimed at acts, not at forms of government: a coup that is not itself accompanied by line-crossing is not a reason; of the measures taken toward that state, the humanitarian part must be cut out. For reasons other than line-crossing, such as long-standing non-performance, how they are to be handled is not decided by philosophy; it is left to political leaders, under the two floors below. In the matter of the Outer Sandbars, the reason is a colonizing entry, which is within the direction. Of the three conditions of Article 27, the first two look at acts and the last at votes: expulsion is a decision made on acts and landed through votes. This time the vote did not pass; those who made the judgment were three members, and what each said is in the record. The sample charter in Appendix A of F6 allows a member to be expelled by two-thirds of the members present, and that too is only a sample (F6, Appendix A, Article 10(b); a working demonstration; used only as an example).

Two floors. One: expulsion does not write off the account. Provisions of the kind of clause 4 of Article 27 say just this. F6, speaking of elimination, says: "Elimination can therefore only move the account between its aspects; it cannot empty the account." (F6 §2.1, Note) The original sentence speaks of the elimination of persons; here it is extended to moving a member state out of the system: the account only changes place, and the total account remains (own undertaking). The other: expulsion does not change where the colonizing comes from. Colonizing begun before expulsion still counts as coming from the system after expulsion, and joint defense can still be used (Paper 5, XZ29). The NUT's remark, "It went in while it was in the system," says exactly this. The hypothetical case of §5 is the reverse: there the entry took place after exit, and does not count as coming from the system. In both places it is recognized by when the colonizing took place.

The cost of expulsion, written in three layers. The normative layer: those who are protected do not go out with the expulsion, and this means three things. Petitions submitted before expulsion are still concluded; this is stage two of §3. Those reached by the system's acts thereafter, for instance those reached by the system's sanctions or notifications concerning the expelled state, have a road by which to ask, following the unified sentence (system level, Paper 2, §6). Questioning and recording go on as before: the system and peers can still question and record a state outside the system (Paper 2, XZ11).

The cost is written at this layer too: for those reached by that state's acts (its own people, and people outside the system who are under its acts), the road of petition against that state's acts thereafter is closed within the system. This stretch is registered as a remainder as it stands; it is also one of the reasons why the conditions for expulsion must be strict.

The rule layer: Article 14 of the Compact, under which who may petition is drawn by those affected and not by citizenship, is an example; that after expulsion this road closes for acts thereafter is a consequence of this example. The judgment layer: whether to expel must take this cost into account, and is left to political leaders.

The three fishermen of the Outer Sandbars give this cost a face. After they were held, they had a lawyer in Rivermouth City complain to the UCSE's Inspectorate about the holding and the curfew. The Inspectorate replied that the complainants did not hold UCSE citizenship and were not lawfully resident in UCSE territory, and so did not fall within what it could take up. Article 14 of the Compact became the one road left. In the autumn of Year 18, they petitioned the Petitions Committee under clause 2 of Article 14; the member of the Committee holding UCSE citizenship did not take part in the deliberations. In the spring of Year 19, the Committee decided to take up the petition, on the ground that "clause 2 speaks of persons affected by it, and is not drawn by citizenship; the Inspectorate does not take up the complaint, and the petitioners have no other available remedy within the UCSE." As of Year 20, the Committee was still hearing it. Had expulsion passed at that time, this petition, submitted before the expulsion, would still be concluded; but whatever the administration office did to the people on the islands thereafter, they would have had no road in the system.

From this, Stonegorge's question can be answered. What expulsion releases is the UCSE's place in common procedures, and the one road of petition within the system against its acts thereafter; what remains is the earlier account, the petitions already submitted, and the questioning and recording of it by the system and by the states. The NUT's "outside, no one can question it any more" goes too far: the system and peers can still question and record a state outside the system, and what is closed is the one road of Article 14. The NUT's wanting the UCSE, in the matter of suspension, to "sit here and answer" is exactly the shape of this cost: staying inside, the UCSE remains under that road.

One more case must be mentioned. Where a member splits into two parts and the system recognizes one part and "expels" the other, this is read as not recognizing that part as succeeding to the original member's membership. Among the events that have happened there is no split of a member; this case is judged in Paper 5, §11 (Paper 5, XZ34), and the two floors above apply as they stand.

By the four items of Theory of the State N36, the channel of expulsion is written as follows. Who initiates it: the member that proposes it and the positions that vote. The object: the termination of membership and its reasons. The side that proposes and votes cannot close the questioning directed at the expulsion: the party proposed for expulsion can make statements at every stage, which is how Article 7(b) of the Compact is written for a member being questioned; the decision must state its reasons; peers and those affected still ask. When it fails, the account is booked to the positions and persons that proposed and voted (Theory of the State N39).

The UCSE said: "Expel a member, and the system becomes the kind of thing it says it is not." The weight of this objection is that expulsion looks like a ruling handed down by the system from above its members. But expulsion acquires no force over the UCSE: the affairs of the Outer Sandbars are still decided by the UCSE; whether to withdraw or to stay, the system cannot decide in its place; the system only no longer works with it in common procedures. What must be guarded against is something else: expulsion becoming a tool by which a party with great influence removes dissenting members. So expulsion must be aimed at acts, its conditions must be strict, and it must itself be open to questioning. Judgment falls on acts: who the acting party is does not change this judgment. Here the one proposed for expulsion is the UCSE, and the reading above is not changed by a word.

What is left here: how strict is left to political leaders. In the matter of the Outer Sandbars, neither expulsion nor suspension passed; as of Year 20 the administration office remained, and what is left in the system's hands is questioning, recording, and each member's own cooperation. The cost is written as it is.

§7 Dissolution

The system itself may also break up. The members may all no longer want it, or it may no longer be able to do what it was meant to do. Then, to whom is the earlier account handed?

When the system dissolves, it must do its best to make follow-up arrangements. The arrangements include at least three items: where unsettled matters, petitions and procedures, go; records are handed, matter by matter, to the member states concerned for safekeeping; and the account of each act falls on the states and positions that voted for it and carried it out (Theory of the State N39). The words "do its best" must be read precisely: what cannot be arranged is registered as a remainder, and it is not declared that there is no longer any shortfall. The Theory of the State says: "Every division of membership, whether acquisition, renunciation, revocation, intake or secession, leaves its own shortfall," and further: "neither redrawing the object domain nor running a procedure to its end writes off the account." (Theory of the State N17) Dissolution is a division too, only what is divided off is the whole system; that sentence is extended from membership within a state to the dissolution of the system (own undertaking).

Step one: dissolution is an act of the members. Article 30 of the Compact requires the consent of all in the Conference of Members, and requires follow-up arrangements to be made before dissolution: where unsettled petitions go, archives handed matter by matter to the members concerned for safekeeping, and the accounts for sharing settled. This article too is an example. A party that wants dissolution while others do not consent takes the road of exit (§1). Dismantling a construct is also an act of that construct: "Dismantling is also an act of the construct: the dismantling itself may not cross the line" (Institution Theory Z38). The original sentence speaks of a construct within a state; here it is extended to the system (own undertaking).

Step two: dissolution does not write off the account. Binding force stops going forward, and the earlier account is not written off, on the same principle as exit (§3). F6's sample charter wrote: "After dissolution, this Charter no longer binds any former member civilization." (F6, Appendix A, Article 19; a working demonstration; used only as an example) This sentence too speaks only of going forward. When the system is gone, where does the account fall? Institution Theory says: "the construct is the address of attribution, not the subject of the act; the address of attribution and the actual actor do not hide each other." (Institution Theory Z7a) The system is an address. When the address is withdrawn, the account falls on the member states and positions that voted and carried out acts under that address. The Theory of Government says: "If 'a final bearer' means a position where the accounts are all gathered in and the door is shut, this series does not have it; if it means that every account lands on a position that can be named, it does" (Theory of Government G36). Shared resources and procedural obligations are not closed out by departure, and can only pass to arrangement (Conflict Treatise §25). The Conflict Treatise originally speaks of two parties parting; here it is extended to the dissolution of the system (own undertaking).

Step three: what cannot be arranged is registered as it stands. The system has never dissolved. The transition period when a member exits can be looked at as a small sample. In the six months after Stonegorge's exit took effect, the accounts for sharing were settled as the covenant required, the two petitions were heard and concluded as usual, and transfers under Sharing Decision No. 4 were carried out until the summer of Year 19. The Eastfield camp then held about two hundred ten thousand people. Sharing ended when the transition period expired; in the autumn of Year 19, the UCSE and Stonegorge concluded a separate bilateral agreement to receive four thousand more people. The gap between two hundred ten thousand and four thousand is what it looks like when "do its best" does not reach. It must be registered as a remainder as it stands, and not written as already arranged. If the system dissolves, those whom the system's acts reach, for instance the petitioners of unsettled petitions, those in the course of sharing and transfer, and those written of in the records, are all within this arrangement, following the unified sentence (system level, Paper 2, §6).

By the four items, the place of dissolution is written as follows. Who initiates it: the members, with the consent of all required. The object: dissolution and the follow-up arrangements. The party questioned cannot close the questioning directed at it: dissolution is itself an act, and how unsettled petitions, and people in the course of transfer, are to land is what it must answer. When it fails, the account falls on the member states and positions that voted and carried it out, and what cannot be arranged is registered as a remainder.

It may be said that, once the system has dissolved, there is no address, so to whom is the account booked? Step two has already answered: when the address is withdrawn, the account falls on the member states and positions that voted and carried out acts. It may also be said that dissolution is the states' freedom and needs no arrangements. The weight of this objection is that no one can force a group of states to keep up a system none of them wants. This paper acknowledges this. But dismantling is also an act, and is itself under questioning; the freedom to dissolve does not wipe out unsettled matters at a stroke.

By the three layers: the three items "at least" included, and "it is not declared that there is no longer any shortfall," are at the normative layer; Article 30 of the Compact is an example at the rule layer; how each item is arranged, and to which state it is handed, is left to the judgment of those in positions.

What is left here: whatever cannot be arranged is, without exception, registered as a remainder.

§8 With Kant

This paper asks about leaving and coming back, and asked to the bottom it is an old question: can there be, between states, a rightful condition that counts, one that does not rest on a compulsion from above, and in which the states remain free throughout? The most direct connection is with Kant.

In Appendix II of Perpetual Peace (Zum ewigen Frieden), Kant treats the agreement of politics with morality, and point 2 there treats the right of nations. He says that the right of nations presupposes some rightful condition, and that this condition must arise from some contract; this contract need not, like the one from which a state arises, be founded on laws of compulsion, but "may at most also be that of a continuously free association, like the one mentioned above, of the federality of different states" (my translation; allenfalls auch der einer fortwährend-freyen Association seyn kann, wie der oben erwähnte der Föderalität verschiedener Staaten; Zum ewigen Frieden, Appendix II, point 2, per the first edition, Nicolovius, 1795, Project Gutenberg EBook #46873; compared with the English translation by M. C. Smith, EBook #50922; tier A). In the same place he says further that the contract binds the states "only with the aim of keeping themselves at peace with one another and together toward other states, and by no means in order to make acquisitions" (my translation; daß der Vertrag die Staaten nur in der Absicht verbindet, unter einander und zusammen gegen andere Staaten sich im Frieden zu erhalten, keinesweges aber um Erwerbungen zu machen; ibid., same point). At the end of Appendix II he gathers this point into one sentence: a federative condition of states that aims only at the removal of war is the only rightful condition compatible with their freedom (ibid., final paragraph of Appendix II, paraphrased).

The common question. Kant asks where the rightful condition that the right of nations needs comes from. This paper asks how, in a system of cooperation, leaving and coming back are to be arranged, and how the earlier account is to be reckoned. They do not look like one question. The two meet at one point: can there be, between states, an association that counts, one not founded on laws of compulsion, in which the states remain free throughout? At this point, they are the same question.

Where they agree. The rightful condition that the right of nations needs can arise from a continuously free association not founded on laws of compulsion: on this the two agree. The definition this series gives of the system is precisely that it has no superior with force above it and sets none up; what the system can do is question, record and withdraw cooperation (§2).

Where they part. Kant says "continuously free" without taking it apart. This series sets it down in three things: exit can go through; coming back needs others' consent; after leaving, the earlier account is not written off. Stonegorge's case matches these one by one. It gave notice of exit, and the system made a record and did not stand in the way (§1). It applied to rejoin, which is a new covenant, still undecided as of the winter of Year 20 (§4). Its two petitions from its time in the system were heard and concluded as usual, and in the transition period sharing was settled as the covenant required (§3). The DRP's case adds one more item: for procedures the covenant does not permit to be newly opened, that road within the system has closed, and the account is not written off (§3). Kant's text does not take these three things apart.

This series reads the sentence thus: the association itself is not kept up by compulsion; "continuously" says that the association goes on in freedom, not that members' acts may be exempt from questioning. Leaving, coming back and the account are what this series adds on this reading, and are not booked under Kant's name.

A question drafted on Kant's behalf. Since a continuously free association is still joined by contract, how can existing obligations be kept from being erased by exit, while an unsettled matter is kept from becoming a reason never to allow exit? How can a common procedure with no superior with force above it write both sides in a usable form?

This series answers in three places. Exit is not vetoed by the other side: leaving is this state's own change of structure, questioned inside this state (§1). Re-accession needs the consent of all parties: the association is held with one another, and one party's freedom cannot become forcing its way through another's door; leaving needs no one's consent and coming back needs consent, and only the two together make both sides free (§4). Earlier acts are not exempt from the account: the account is earlier acts, not binding going forward; F6, speaking of continuity, says that exit "does not by itself clear facts of prior performance" (F6 §3.3). The account remains, yet unsettled matters do not keep exit from taking effect indefinitely (§1). It is not that Kant wanted freedom without reckoning old accounts, and that only this series brings the account in; in Kant, the association arises from contract to begin with. On this shared difficulty, this series separates the period of membership, procedures already begun, and procedures newly opened after exit (§3), and states which consents re-accession requires. This is an unfolding, and does not claim to refute Kant.

This section does not extend in two directions. As for the half-sentence in the same point, "only with the aim of keeping themselves at peace," this series takes anti-colonization as its main line and does not guarantee peace; this place of parting has been written in the Prequel, and is not set against Kant again here. The test of publicity has been set against Kant in Theory of the State Paper 5, §4, and is not set against him again here either.

Where This Paper Stops

This paper has proved these things. Exiting or switching an external arrangement is a change of structure in this state, questioned inside this state, and not decided alone by the position being questioned; the system can review, can record, and can require a notice period and a transition period in the covenant, but cannot veto; the notice period may not be made into a lock, and a fixed end shows only that it cannot be extended by the party being left on its own. Any arrangement that in fact acquires force over members is a superior, and locking exit uses the same criterion; when a rule hands to the outside the authority to review its own repeal, the answer comes in two steps, and the state is still at the state's end. Exit does not write off the account, in three stages: acts during membership are still recorded, procedures already begun are still concluded, and whether new procedures can be opened after exit follows the acknowledged covenant. A member is free to leave, coming back needs consent, and re-accession has two floors. Colonizing after first exiting does not count as coming from the system; there is no exiting, colonizing and then rejoining. The system may expel a member under strict conditions; expulsion does not write off the account, and does not change where the colonizing comes from. When the system dissolves, it does its best to make follow-up arrangements.

What it has not proved: how long the notice period and the transition period are; the particular conditions for re-accession, expulsion and suspension, which are left to political leaders; the detailed rules of exit procedure in treaty law.

The remainder it leaves: through which positions a member's exit must be made, inside the member, to accord with its own law, under which the DRP's exit is registered; the stretch closed when the covenant does not permit new procedures, within which fall the two in the DRP and the seven returned petitions; the road of petition that expulsion closes; and what cannot be arranged at dissolution. These are registered as remainders as they stand, and are not written as though a road already existed. Stonegorge's re-accession was still undecided as of the winter of Year 20, and the administration office on the Outer Sandbars was still there as of Year 20; the record runs to this point and stops.

Handed to Paper 8: once a member is free to leave, coming back needs consent, and the account is not written off, the question that remains is whether every arrangement still lies under questioning.

The system may review and record, and may require a notice period and a transition period in the covenant, but cannot veto; the notice period keeps exit from being used to evade questioning under way, it may not be made into a lock, and a fixed end shows only that it cannot be extended by the party being left on its own. External review of an exit is one kind of external questioning, which this state cannot cancel, and so the system can review (Paper 2, XZ11); only force can block exit, and so the system cannot veto (XZ46). A covenant may set conditions on exit (a notice period, settling); when the conditions are not met, the account remains, still recorded and still questioned, but unsettled matters do not keep exit from taking effect indefinitely (Theory of the State N16, extended from secession to a member's exit; own undertaking); where a covenant has no exit clause, the system cannot use "non-recognition" in place of a veto either. The notice period and the transition period are checked under Theory of Government G39: the party being left cannot cancel exit on its own by pricing or by approval; the actual burdens, the existing conditions, and whether the matter can pass into a reviewable procedure must still be checked separately. Toward a member in the course of exiting, a freeze serves only the account and is not made a condition of exit (Paper 4, XZ22). Exit does not stand in for questioning (Theory of Government G27). How long the notice period and the transition period are is left to the judgment of those in positions.

XZ46 Any arrangement that in fact acquires force over members is a superior; locking exit is one case. Force is substituting one's own judgment for the other party's judgment on its own affairs; here the party substituted for is the member, and the judgment is whether, at this moment, the member implements a decision, and how. A decision made by majority under a covenant agreed beforehand lands through the member's own law; the system can only question, record and withdraw cooperation, and is not a superior (Paper 1, XZ1); only what can force a decision to land over the member's own law is one. Withdrawing cooperation does not substitute for judgment (Paper 4, XZ22); withholding from disbursements is a check, and taking a member's own capital is force. An arrangement that has acquired force answers to the requirements the Theory of Government places on a level of government (the multi-branch separation of powers, channels for internal subjects, openness to questioning), and cannot evade them with "it is only cooperation." Locking exit uses the same criterion: a lock needs force, and an arrangement that locks by force is a superior (Law Series P3 §6, Power Theory P6 §I and Moral Law P9 §4.2 extended to the system toward its members; own undertaking); a "lock" that does not rest on force is only an external questioning that the member can walk away from but cannot cancel, and the state is still at the state's end. To a rule that hands to the outside the authority to review its own repeal, the answer comes in two steps: external review is one kind of external questioning; the system can review but cannot veto, and if a member hands the veto to the system, the system does not accept it; if a member, in its own law, has set an external consent on its own exit, that is its own law, and changing it is a change of structure, questioned inside this state (Theory of the State N24); the outside cannot stop it except by force, and what carries force is no longer within this series' domain (own undertaking). Whether there is an exit clause is not the criterion; the criterion is whether force is had, or needed, to hold members in. When an arrangement has in fact acquired force is, at the boundary, left to the judgment of those in positions, and in the same place it is stated which party, and which judgment, is substituted.

XZ47 Exit does not write off the account. Acts before exit are still recorded, procedures already begun are still concluded, and questioning between peers goes on as before (Theory of the State N38). Past residue is not written off by closing off questioning (Institution Theory Z35a); exit terminates the current answering load, and does not by itself clear facts of prior performance (F6 §3.3); shared resources and procedural obligations are not closed out by departure, and can only pass to arrangement or arbitration (Conflict Treatise §25, originally of two first-person laws after they part, extended to a member's exit; own undertaking). The sample's "no longer bound after exit" speaks only of binding force going forward: binding force stops going forward, and the earlier account is not written off. Three stages (own undertaking): (1) acts during membership are still recorded; (2) procedures already begun are still concluded; (3) whether new procedures can be opened after exit on acts during membership follows the acknowledged covenant: where the covenant permits, the covenant is followed; where it does not, that road within the system is closed, but questions from peers and other channels go on as before, and the account is not written off. What stops going forward is the rights newly acquired or exercised as a member; rights and procedures that have already arisen and continue under the covenant follow the original covenant; after exit, a state can no longer initiate new procedures under the covenant as a party to it. The stretch closed when the covenant does not permit new procedures is registered as a remainder as it stands, and is not written as though a road remained.

XZ48 Free to leave; coming back needs consent. Re-accession is a new covenant, and as a covenant it requires the consent of all parties (Paper 1, XZ1); the parties may set conditions; this is a check, not force: not consenting decides nothing on the other's behalf on its own affairs, but only declines to make a new covenant with it. Two floors: re-accession does not wash the account clean, and acts during the period of exit are still recorded and still questioned; a state may not come back bringing a colonization under way. No permanent bar is set against the name of a state: judgment falls on acts, and is not decided by origin (Theory of the State N34, extended from occupation to re-accession; own undertaking). Conditions above the floor are written out, go by acts and the account, are not aimed at forms of government or the names of states, and are set by the political leaders of the parties; those that require the other party to change an autonomous choice of its own that crosses no line are checked under Paper 4, XZ25. The covenant of re-accession settles the one matter it is to settle, and does not rewrite the acts of the period of exit (Conflict Treatise §24; own undertaking). Line-crossing under way at first accession, and an observer member's seat: see Paper 1, XZ7.

XZ49 Colonizing after first exiting does not count as coming from the system; there is no exiting, colonizing and then rejoining. Joint defense recognizes whether a state was a member when the colonizing took place, and does not reach back; reaching back would make the freedom to leave come to nothing (XZ45). The part prepared within the system before exit, making use of the system's arrangements, is still booked to the account. Colonizing begun after exit is, for the system, outside the system against outside the system; joint defense does not take it up, the indirect means remain open (putting questions, recording, sanctions, receiving those who leave), and how far they are used is left to the political leaders in their positions. Applying to rejoin bringing a colonization under way is blocked at the second floor of re-accession (XZ48), and is not left to political leaders. The gap here is narrowed by Paper 5, XZ30: whichever colonizing entry forces are sent to stop, the sending state's own first face must hold; whether a member or not, the only difference is prior coordination and preparation.

XZ50 Expulsion: permitted, under strict conditions; it does not write off the account and does not change where the colonizing comes from. The system may expel a member; how strict is not decided by philosophy, and is left to political leaders. Expulsion means the termination of membership and is directed at the member state as a construct; people do not go out with it; the Theory of the State established no expulsion for persons (Theory of the State N14), and established nothing on a framework's expelling a member state. Expulsion, like suspension, is withdrawing cooperation, and acquires no force over the expelled party (Paper 4, XZ27). The direction of the reasons is line-crossing: colonizing another state, or crossing the line against one's own people (massacre, torture, detention without trial, the driving people out on a large scale); the latter rests on covenants the member has acknowledged, and is aimed at acts, not at forms of government; a coup not itself accompanied by line-crossing is not a reason; the humanitarian part is cut out. Reasons other than line-crossing (such as long-standing non-performance) are left to political leaders, under the two floors. Two floors: expulsion does not write off the account, for when a member is moved out the account only changes place (F6 §2.1, Note; own undertaking); colonizing begun before expulsion still counts as coming from the system after expulsion, and joint defense can still be used (Paper 5, XZ29). Expulsion is itself an act, open to questioning: the party proposed for expulsion can make statements at every stage, the decision states its reasons, and the account falls on the positions and persons that propose and vote (Theory of the State N39); it is aimed at acts, not at the names or leanings of states, so that expulsion does not become a tool by which a party with great influence removes dissenting members. In three layers: at the normative layer, those who are protected do not go out with the expulsion, meaning three things: petitions submitted before expulsion are still concluded (XZ47, stage two); those reached by the system's acts thereafter have a road by which to ask, following the unified sentence (system level, Paper 2, §6); questioning and recording go on as before. For those reached by that state's acts (its own people, and people outside the system who are under its acts), the road of petition against that state's acts thereafter is closed within the system; this is the cost of expulsion, registered as a remainder as it stands, and one of the reasons why the conditions are strict. At the rule layer, the article on expulsion and the article under which who may petition is drawn by those affected are examples. At the judgment layer, whether to expel takes this cost into account, and is left to political leaders. When a member splits, "expelling the other part" is read as not recognizing that part as succeeding to that member's membership (Paper 5, XZ34).

XZ51 When the system dissolves, it does its best to make follow-up arrangements. The follow-up arrangements include at least: where unsettled matters (petitions, procedures) go; records handed, matter by matter, to the member states concerned for safekeeping; and the account of each act falling on the states and positions that voted for it and carried it out (Theory of the State N39). "Do its best": what cannot be arranged is registered as a remainder, and it is not declared that there is no longer any shortfall (Theory of the State N17; own undertaking). Dismantling is also an act of the construct, and the dismantling itself may not cross the line (Institution Theory Z38; own undertaking). Dissolution stops only binding force going forward, and the earlier account is not written off; shared resources and procedural obligations are not closed out by departure, and can only pass to arrangement (Conflict Treatise §25, extended to the dissolution of the system; own undertaking). What cannot be arranged is stated in the procedure, and registered as a remainder as it stands.

摘要

一个成员要退出一项外部安排，怎样审，由谁审？这一篇接国家论交来的三问：这样的退出怎样审、由谁审；一条规则能不能把改它自己的决定也交给外部；把退出交给外部锁住的国家，还在不在国家这一端。答的是：退出是本国的构造改动，在本国内部受问，不由正在被问的位置独定；体系能审查、记录，可以在约里要求通知期与过渡期，不能否决，通知期不得被用成锁。凡事实上取得对成员强力的安排就是上级，锁住退出用的是同一判据；成员给自己的退出设了外部的同意，改它仍是它自己的事，外部拦不住，除非用强力。往下四步：退出不销账，分三阶段；走得开，回来要同意，再加入不洗账，也不能带着正在进行的殖民回来；先退出再殖民不算来自体系，但不能退出、殖民、再加入；体系可以开除成员，条件从严，开除不销账，不改变殖民的来处，它的代价是那一国施为所及的人对它此后施为的申诉之路在体系里关上。体系解散，尽量做好后续安排，安排不到的登为余项。全篇以 DRP 在被问的时候退出宪约第 14 条一事开篇，用到石峡邦的退出与再申请、DRP 在自己的法里给退出设外部同意的设例、石峡邦退出以后进入别国的设例，以及开除 UCSE 的提议。随文与格劳秀斯对接，篇末与康德《永久和平论》附录二"持续自由的结合"对话。全篇七条（XZ45 至 XZ51），各节末写出，篇末合列。

这一篇接这三问，再往下走。第一节问退出怎样审、由谁审，体系有什么权限。第二节问，一个体系若事实上拦得住退出，它是什么；一条规则把审查它自己废止的权限交给外部时，又怎样。第三节问，退出以后，以前的账在哪里。第四节问走了的成员怎样回来，第五节问先退出、再进入别国的又怎样。第六节问体系能不能开除成员，第七节问体系自己解散时留下什么。第八节与康德对话。

这个系列在须判断的地方分三层说话：规范层、规则层、判断层，读法见前置篇第十一节。本篇在各节写明哪一句是规范，哪一项是建议的规则或例子，哪一步交位置上的判断。判一件事，本篇照规范读事实，写到事实指向哪一边为止，并写明在那件事里作出判断的是谁；具体一项施为在规则下怎样判，留给那些位置。

第一节　退出在内部受问；体系能审查，不能否决

一个成员要走，谁来审？最省事的两个答案都不对。一个说体系来审，审不过就不许走；那样体系手里就有了一把锁。另一个说这是成员自己的事，由它那里作出退出决定的位置说了算；那样一个正在被问的位置，可以靠退出把问关上。DRP 那一次，两种危险都摆在了眼前。

退出是本国的施为。 国家论说，「中止或退出一项条约，本身是本国的施为」（国家论 N24）。原句说的是两国之间的条约；一个成员退出多国体系的一项条款，是同一种施为，只是对面从一国换成了多国（自担）。既是本国的施为，它就是本国的一项构造改动，在本国内部受问。国家论第一篇写过的那一条路在这里照用：一项决定关系到正在被问的位置，那个位置不独定。

DRP 的退出放在这里看。声明出自外务部，它正是答委员会问的那个位置；批准它的是主席。接受第 14 条，是人民议会表决的；退出时，人民议会没有表决。六十一位议员要求表决，主席团没有排入议程。退出这一决定在 DRP 的法里该经哪些位置，这一点没有记载，本篇不替 DRP 读它自己的法。事实指向的只有一处：作出退出决定的，是正在被问的位置；要求它在内部受问的那条路，六十一位议员的联名，停在了主席团那里。这条路是 DRP 内部的事。体系不替人民议会作答，也不替主席团把它关上。在这件事里作出判断的位置有三处：外务部与主席作出了退出；主席团决定不排议程；成员会议以 3 比 0 记下退出发生在发问期间，DRP 不参加表决，它的陈述载入记录。

DRP 声明里给的理由，是第 14 条"被用来干涉共和国的司法"。这是在说，约被用歪了，所以可以不再受它约束。格劳秀斯讲条约时写过同一种道理：「当事一方若违反条约，这一违反就解除了另一方的约定。因为每一条款都有条件的约束力。」（If one of the parties violates a treaty, such a violation releases the other from its engagements. For every clause has the binding force of a condition.；《战争与和平法》第二卷第十五章第十二节，据 A. C. Campbell 英译，New York & London: M. Walter Dunne 1901 年本，Project Gutenberg 电子本 #46564，节译，拉丁原文未核，B 档）他随即留了一个限：双方若另有约定，条约不因细小的不快与差失而作废（同上，同节，转述）。

两家问的是同一件事：约是彼此的，一方说另一方背了约，它还受不受约。在这一点上两家同向。约是彼此的，没有一个在上的法庭替各方断定谁先背约，被约束的一方可以不再受约。分开在两处。格劳秀斯说违约即解除；本系列说，不论理由是什么，退出本身是本国的施为，在本国内部受问，体系可以在约里要求通知期与过渡期。再者，解除的只是往后的约束，已开始的程序照结，这是第三节的事。DRP 说的"干涉"有没有其事，委员会的意见与 DRP 的陈述都在记录里，读的人可以去比；本篇不替谁下这一判。

体系能做什么。 石峡邦的退出没有被一件正在进行的问缠住，正好看清体系的权限。

第 17 年秋，石峡邦邦议院改选，新的多数选出新的邦首席。她在就职时说："体系给了我们一份记录，灯岛还在高原联邦手里。"这句话说的是同年春天的事：高原联邦的舰队进入石峡水道，在灯岛登陆，设了"航道管理所"；成员会议记下"有一项殖民性的进入"，UCSE 出兵护航，两个九十日以后撤回，灯岛的驻军留了下来（见第五篇）。第 17 年冬，邦议院以 97 票对 83 票同意通知退出，邦首席依宪约第 25 条书面通知秘书长。

宪约第 25 条写的是：书面通知满十二个月生效，此后六个月为过渡期，清结分担的账目，已提出的申诉照常审结。第 3 款："退出不影响退出以前的施为所生的义务，也不影响已开始的程序。"第 4 款：成员会议可以审查退出的通知，作出记录，不得否决。

反对党请最高法庭解释，通知退出是否也须三分之二。第 18 年春，最高法庭答复："基本法只对批准规定了三分之二。"同季，成员会议依第 25 条第 4 款以 3 比 0 作了记录，石峡邦不参加表决。记录列出通知时正在进行的事项：申诉委员会受理的两件对石峡邦的申诉，一件关于东坪营地的外出规定，一件关于依《反分化法》被判刑的一位编辑；第 1、2 号共同措施；分担经费的结算。UCSE 常驻代表说："体系不能留住石峡邦，也不打算留。"DRP 常驻代表说："退出是成员的权利。"NUT 常驻代表问《石峡山口通行协定》在退出以后是否照旧，石峡邦答照旧。第 18 年冬退出生效；第 20 年秋，石峡邦又申请再加入，那是第四节的事。

这件事落在石峡邦的人身上，邦议院里投反对票的八十三席所代表的人也在内；落在那两件申诉的申诉人身上；也落在东坪营地约二十一万人身上，他们的分担与转送靠的是体系的共同程序。要看的线有两侧。体系一侧，是五条应当中的第一条，体系的施为不越线，它不做成员的上级，所以不否决退出；还有第五条，已提出的申诉要有人照常审结。石峡邦一侧，是退出在本国内部受问。在此之前，有宪约第 25 条，也有石峡邦加入时的那一次批准：第 14 年秋，邦议院以 121 票对 59 票批准加入，基本法要求的是一百二十票。

事实指向的方向是清楚的。在石峡邦内部，退出经了邦议院的表决，反对的一方请最高法庭解释门槛，法庭作了答复；问在内部走了一遍，答案落在石峡邦自己的法上。在体系里，成员会议审查了通知，把进行中的事项一一记下，没有阻拦。内部的问与体系的审查各在各的位置上，谁也没有替谁。

《制度如何可能》附录二的一份样例宪法，给群体退出写过另一条路：「联邦议会不得无理由拒绝退出申请，但有权就退出条件进行协商。如协商不成，由宪法法院裁定退出条件。」（F6 附录二第 6 条(c)，工作展示，只作样例）那里，退出的条件最后落在一个在上的法院手里。国家论讲一国之内的分离时没有取这条路，它要的是「双方都不单方终局」（国家论 N16）。成员与体系之间连这样一个在上的法院也没有，这里同样不取。

条件不得成为锁。 约可以给退出设条件：通知期，清结账目，过渡期。条件没有满足时怎么办？国家论在一国之内的分离上说过：「未结的事项也不使退出无限期不生效。」（国家论 N16）这一句推到成员退出体系，主词从分离的单位换成成员（自担）。条件没满足，账留着，照记、照问；退出照样到期生效。约里没有退出条款的，体系也不能拿"不承认"代替否决：不承认一个成员已经走了，接着要它照旧落实共同的决定，与不许它走是一回事。对一个正在退出的成员，冻结只为账，不作退出的条件，第四篇第二节写过这一点（XZ22）。

条件还要从反方向核。政府论讲过，退出的代价可以高到等于一把锁：「"可能"不等于"可负担"：退出成本可以高到实质上等价于锁定」。所以「要核的是，被退出的一方能不能以定价或审批独自取消它。」（政府论 G39）原句说人退出一级政府的安排，这里推到成员退出体系（自担）。宪约第 25 条与第 14 条第 5 款的十二个月，都有既定的终点，到期就生效，成员会议不能凭批与不批使通知期无限延长。这排除了被退出的一方独自延长的那一种锁；通知期里的实际负担、既有的条件，以及能不能转入可审查的程序，还须分别核，不由有终点就全部判过。

通知期在 DRP 那件事里起了什么作用，也照实写。十二个月里，委员会照常审结了二人一件，作出了意见；这一件没有因为退出而落空。可通知期让不了 DRP 作答：它在六个月内没有答复，成员会议能做的是记下来。通知期让正在进行的问走完，不能让被问的一方开口。这是这个体系的代价，第八篇写它（XZ54）。

国家论有一条要求：「每一条通道都要写清：谁发动，影响哪一对象，被问的一方能不能关上，失灵时怎样归账」（国家论 N36）。退出这一处是这样。发动的，是本国内部的位置与人；体系审查、记录。对象，是退出这一项构造改动。被问的一方关得上一条通道，取消不了问（第二篇 XZ11）。失灵时，账落在作出退出的位置与人上；一项通知期、冻结、条件或"不承认"若在事实上锁住了退出，就照下一节的判据读。

有人会说：体系不能否决，成员就能在被问的时候用退出躲开问，DRP 正是这样做的；或者在体系外的压力下被一个个拉走，石峡邦正是这样走的。这一说的分量在事实上：DRP 不再作答，七件申诉被退回；石峡邦走以前，灯岛还在别人手里，走以后它与高原联邦订了约。可是这两件事，否决都救不了。「退出不替追问。」（政府论 G27）DRP 走了，已开始的申诉照样审结，问还在（第三节）。否决要靠强力：不许一个成员走，手里又没有强力，否决就只是一句话；有了强力，体系就成了它的上级（第二节）。成员在压力下一个个疏远，是一个体系平时失灵的样子；战争论讲平时的备战，要的是往来的通道开着、互相承认不断（战争论 §10）。这一失灵要照实记下，算作代价，它不是给体系一把锁的理由。

反过来也有人会问：通知期本身，会不会成了一项人为的退出成本，把人拖住？通知期本身不等于锁；期限固定，也不独自证明实际退出走得通。照上面政府论那一句核，这两处写明了到期生效、不能由被退出的一方独自延长；其他足以决定退出实际走不走得通的条件，例子里没有逐项写出，这里不补判。

也有人会由此推出：成员既然可以退出，体系就可以薄。这一推不成立。法学第四篇写的是退出可行时的顶层，剩下的是共识加退出（法学第四篇 §2）；这里不同：成员走得开一项安排，走不开彼此。国家论说两国之间的条约，「可退出协定，退不出那条河。」（国家论第五篇第一节）石峡邦退出了体系，石峡水道与灯岛还在原处，东坪营地的人也还在。

照前置篇第十一节的三层，这一节是这样分的。不能否决，通知期不得被用成锁，条件不使退出无限期不生效，这是规范层。宪约第 25 条的十二个月与六个月，第 14 条第 5 款的十二个月，是规则层的例子，本系列不主张它们最好。一项具体的约里，通知期与过渡期定多长，交位置上的判断。

这里留下一处。退出这一决定，在 DRP 内部该由谁作出才合它自己的法，六十一位议员的要求后来怎样，这一点没有记载；体系够不着替它答，本篇登为余项。

XZ45　退出在内部受问；体系能审查，不能否决。退出或改接一项外部安排，是本国的一项构造改动（国家论 N24，两国推到多国，自担），在本国内部受问，不由正在被问的位置独定。体系可以审查、记录，可以在约里要求通知期与过渡期，不能否决；通知期让退出不被拿来躲开正在进行的问，它不得被用成锁，固定的终点只说明它不能由被退出的一方独自延长。外部审查一项退出，是外部追问的一种，本国取消不了，所以体系能审查（第二篇 XZ11）；能拦住退出的只有强力，所以体系不能否决（XZ46）。约可以给退出设条件（通知期、清结）；条件没有满足时，账留着，照记、照问，但未结的事项不使退出无限期不生效（国家论 N16，分离推到成员退出，自担）；约里没有退出条款的，体系也不能以"不承认"代替否决。通知期与过渡期照政府论 G39 核：被退出的一方不能以定价或审批独自取消退出；实际负担、既有条件与能不能转入可审查的程序，还须分别核。对退出中的成员，冻结只为账，不作退出的条件（第四篇 XZ22）。退出不替追问（政府论 G27）。通知期与过渡期多长，交位置上的判断。

第二节　取得对成员强力的，就是上级

第一节说体系不能否决退出。可是一个体系若事实上拦得住退出，或者一个成员自己把否决交给了体系，事情又怎样？要答这一问，先要说清什么是强力。

强力怎样认。 强力，是用自己的判断力替代对方就它自己的事的判断。在体系与成员之间，被替代的一方是成员；被替代的那一件判断，是成员此刻落不落实、怎样落实一项决定。照事先之约以多数作出的决定，经成员自己的法落地，落不落地仍在成员；体系能做的是问、记、收回配合，它不是上级（第一篇 XZ1）。能越过成员自己的法强行落地的，才是。收回配合不替代谁的判断：成员仍旧自己决定，只是别人不再配合它（第四篇 XZ22）。财务上也是这个分别。从体系本要拨给一个成员的款项里扣下一部分，是收回配合，是牵制；拿走成员自己的资本，是替它处置它自己的东西，是强力。一项安排若事实上这样做了，它就已经是上级。

已经是上级的安排，照政府论对一级政府的要求作答：多权分立，内部主体有够得着它的通道，它本身可被追问。它不能拿"只是协作"躲开这些要求。几个国家合成一个更大的国家，本系列不禁（前置篇第三节）；禁的是带了强力而不认。

锁住退出。 锁住退出用的是同一判据。锁要靠强力：一个成员要走，体系不许，能让它留下的，只有替它作这一件判断的力量。靠强力锁住的，就是上级，已经到了政府论讲的那一级。法学系列说：「谁控制退出权，谁就决定法的边界。」（法学第三篇 §6）原句说法与退出的一般关系；读到体系与成员之间，控制成员退出的一方，就在决定成员的法的边界，它已在成员之上（自担）。权力论讲涵育性的权力：「涵育性权力的 A 同样投入维持, 但不取消 B 的中断权: B 可以走」（权力论第六篇 §I）。道德律第九篇说：「不可强制保留的机构特征使退出权确立为权利」（道德律第九篇 §4.2）。这两句原说权力与机构对人，这里推到体系对成员：体系可以为维持协作投入，不能取消成员的走（自担）。

不靠强力的"锁"，又是什么？一个体系对要走的成员说"我们不同意"，却没有手段让它留下，这一句"不同意"只是一项问、一份记录。成员走得开，却取消不了这一问。国家仍在国家这一端。

一条规则把审查它自己废止的权限交给外部。 国家论交来的三问里，第二问最难。一条规则若可以把审查它自己废止的权限也交给外部，本国头上就像多了一层：这条规则改不改，最后由外面说了算。国家这一端的定义，说的是头上没有带强力的上级；那么一国把自己的退出交给外部审查，它还在不在这一端？这里分两步答。

第一步，外部的审查是外部追问的一种。体系能审查一项退出：记下它，记下进行中的事，问它的理由。体系不能否决。一个成员若把对自己退出的否决交给体系，体系也不收。一个只靠问、记与收回配合的协作体系，收下这一否决，就收下了一项它兑现不了的权限；真要兑现，就是强力。

第二步，成员若在自己的法里，给自己的退出设了一项外部的同意，那是它自己的法。改它，是一项构造改动，在本国内部受问（国家论 N24）。外部拦不住它，除非用强力；而带强力的安排，已不在本系列说的协作里，那是政府论的一级。这一步把国家论那一句推到成员给自己设的外部同意上（自担）。两步合起来，那一问就不必再到外面去找一个最后作数的地方：问在内部走，也在外部走；能拦住的只有强力，而强力不在这里。

拿一个设例看第二步。下面的事没有发生过，只是设若。设若第 13 年秋，DRP 人民议会接受第 14 条的那项决议里另有一句："共和国退出本条，须经成员会议同意。"这一句是 DRP 自己的决议，不是宪约的条文；宪约第 14 条第 5 款照旧，没有写成员会议的同意。

设若的经过是这样。第 18 年春，人民议会以 322 票对 251 票修改那项决议，删去这一句，27 人缺席。同月，外务部经主席批准，依第 14 条第 5 款声明退出；人民议会就退出本身仍没有表决。第 18 年夏，UCSE 常驻代表在成员会议上提议，依共和国自己写下的那一句，就"同意与否"表决。他说："共和国当年自己写下要经成员会议同意。"DRP 外务部长答："那一句是人民议会写的，人民议会已经删了；退出依宪约第 14 条第 5 款。"NUT 常驻代表说："体系不能拿一国自己的法去锁这一国。"秘书长持 DRP 籍，回避；代行的副秘书长写了书面意见："宪约第 14 条第 5 款没有写成员会议的同意。"是否付了表决、表决的结果如何，本篇不设。

这一设例落在 DRP 这一构与它内部的几个位置上：人民议会、外务部、主席；也落在第 14 条退出所及的申诉人身上，他们就是 DRP 那件真事里的人。要看的线，是锁住退出与做上级用的那同一个判据。在此之前，那一句写在 DRP 自己的决议里，宪约没有它；人民议会删它，走的是共和国自己的程序；退出本身，人民议会仍没有表决。

照第二步读，事实指向的是这样。那一句在 DRP 的法里，删它是 DRP 的一项构造改动，人民议会以 322 票对 251 票作了，这一改动在 DRP 内部受过问。退出本身没有经人民议会，这一处照第一节，仍是 DRP 内部的余项。成员会议若就"同意与否"表决，表决本身是一项问，记下 DRP 当年写过什么、后来删了什么，这在体系的权限里。体系若借这一句挡住退出，不认 DRP 已经走了，还要它照旧受第 14 条约束、落实其下的决定，它就是在用自己的判断替代 DRP 就它自己的事的判断：DRP 留不留在第 14 条里，正是它自己的事。体系若没有手段让 DRP 照办，这一"挡"只剩一份记录；若有，体系就取得了对成员的强力，成了它说自己不是的上级。在设例里作出判断的位置有三处：提议的 UCSE 常驻代表，写书面意见的副秘书长，要表决的各成员。本篇不设表决，不替它们下终判。

有没有退出条款不是判据。 同一个判据，也读得清宪约与一个联邦宪章的分别。宪约第 2 条第 2 款写着："本宪约不设凌驾于成员之上的机关。体系的任何机关不得对成员、对成员境内的人执行强力。"第 25 条许退出。UCSE 第 13 年夏批准宪约时，南湾邦选派的一位成员院议员在辩论中说："一个国家可以在十二个月以后退出三界体系，一个邦退出 UCSE，宪章里却没有一个字。"他说的是事实：UCSE 宪章第 66 条只写新成员单位怎样加入，对退出没有规定。

这句话容易读成：一处有退出条款，一处没有，分别就在这里。可是有没有退出条款不是判据；第一节说过，约里没有退出条款，体系也不能以"不承认"代替否决。判据是一项安排有没有、要不要靠强力留人。UCSE 是它的成员单位头上带强力的那一级：宪章第 52 条设防卫军，第 53 条许防卫军在三种情形下在成员单位境内执行任务。一个邦退出 UCSE，是分离，照国家论第三篇的路走（国家论 N16），不在本系列。南湾邦第 11 年春的那次公投与此后的联合委员会，国家论第三篇第六节已判过，这里只作对照。体系与 UCSE 分开的地方，在宪约第 2 条第 2 款，不在条文写没写退出。

有人会说：连退出都拦不住的体系，约束不了谁；要约束，就得有一把锁。这一说的分量在于，一个谁都可以走的安排，看起来什么也担保不了。可是约束不靠锁。它靠问，被问的一方关不上它；靠账，退出不销账；靠收回配合；也靠回来要别人同意。账与回来，下面第三、四节写。要一把锁，就是要一个上级；要上级的，照政府论作答，并且认它是上级。

这里留下一处。一项安排什么时候"事实上"取得了强力，边界上会有难判的情形。核的办法照前置篇第二节：它是否使对方不照它的意思做就活不下去，或者实际取消了对方承担代价而不从的余地；同处写明替代的是哪一方、哪一件判断。到了一件具体的事怎样判，交位置上的判断。

XZ46　凡事实上取得对成员强力的安排，就是上级；锁住退出是其一。强力，是用自己的判断力替代对方就它自己的事的判断；在这里被替代的是成员，那一件判断是成员此刻落不落实、怎样落实一项决定。照事先之约以多数作出的决定，经成员自己的法落地，体系只能问、记、收回配合，不是上级（第一篇 XZ1）；能越过成员自己的法强行落地的，才是。收回配合不替代判断（第四篇 XZ22）；从拨款里扣除是牵制，拿走成员自己的资本是强力。取得了强力的安排，照政府论对一级政府的要求作答（多权分立、内部主体的通道、可被追问），不能以"只是协作"躲开。锁住退出用的是同一判据：锁要靠强力，靠强力锁住的就是上级（法学第三篇 §6、权力论第六篇 §I、道德律第九篇 §4.2 推到体系对成员，自担）；不靠强力的"锁"，只是一项走得开、取消不了的外部追问，国家仍在国家这一端。一条规则把审查它自己废止的权限交给外部，分两步答：外部的审查是外部追问的一种，体系能审查、不能否决，成员把否决交给体系，体系也不收；成员若在自己的法里给自己的退出设了外部的同意，那是它自己的法，改它是一项构造改动，在本国内部受问（国家论 N24），外部拦不住，除非用强力，而带强力的已不在本系列的定义域里（自担）。有没有退出条款不是判据，判据是有没有、要不要靠强力留人。一项安排什么时候事实上取得了强力，边界交位置上的判断，同处写明替代的是哪一方、哪一件判断。

第三节　退出不销账

DRP 常驻代表说"退出一项条款是成员的权利"。这句话没有错，DRP 也确实走了：第 19 年春以后，委员会不再受理对它的新申诉。可是二人还在关押，委员会第 18 年冬的意见还在等答复。退出以后，以前的账在哪里？

走与账要分开。走掉的，是往后的约束力；账，是以前的施为。制度论说：「过去的未尽不因关闭追问而销账」（制度论 Z35a）。《制度如何可能》讲成员退出时的连续性：「退出终止的是该位置所支撑的现行应答负荷，不自动清掉此前的履行事实，也不取消有独立根据的其他处理位置。」（F6 §3.3）冲突论讲两方分开以后：「若仍有共同资源，第三方责任或程序义务，离开也不结清它们，只能转入安排或仲裁。」（冲突论 §25）这三句原说制度、成员位置与两条第一人称的法；推到成员退出体系，换的是主词，推的那一步是从"关闭追问、离开一个位置"到"退出一项多国安排"（自担）。同一本《制度如何可能》附录一的样例宪章写过「退出后，该文明不再受本宪章约束」（F6 附录一第 6 条(b)，工作展示，只作样例）。与上面三句合读，它说的只是向后的约束力。约束力往后止，以前的账不销。

退出不销账，按时间分三个阶段看。

第一阶段，成员期间的施为照记。 二人的关押，发生在 DRP 是成员、第 14 条对它有效的那几年里，记在 DRP 名下，退出不把它划掉。石峡邦在通知期里还是成员，那一年的施为也照记。第 18 年春，它依宪约第 7 条（丙）公开说明不再参加第 1 号共同措施，理由是"石峡邦正在与高原联邦就灯岛谈判"；冻结在石峡邦银行里的联邦结算局资产随之解冻，镜海舰队在峡口港的港口服务恢复。第 7 条（丙）许一个成员不参加某项共同措施，只要它向成员会议公开说明理由；石峡邦走了这条路，理由载入了记录。同期，申诉委员会就东坪营地那一件向它提了两次问，它都没有作答，这也记下了。账记在石峡邦名下，也记在作出这些决定的位置上。

第二阶段，已开始的程序照结。 DRP 退出生效以前，委员会继续审理二人一件与另外三件；第 18 年冬就二人一件作出意见，请 DRP 公开复审、许探视。DRP 没有答复，第 19 年夏成员会议记下了没有答复。石峡邦的两件申诉照第 25 条审结，第 19 年作出意见，石峡邦没有答复。程序走完了，答复没有来。程序照结，不保证被问的一方开口。

第三阶段，对成员期间的施为，退出以后能不能开启新的程序，照已认的约。 约许的，照约；约不许的，体系内那一条路关上，但平级者的问与别的通道照旧，账不销。宪约第 14 条第 5 款"生效以后提出的，不受理"，是约不许的明例。第 19 年春以后，七件新的申诉被退回，体系里的那条路关上了。可 UCSE 外事署经联络处两次就二人向 DRP 外务部提过，平级者的问照旧在走。

第 5 款要与第 6 款合读。入口那一头，第 6 款许委员会受理生效以前的施为、效果延续到生效以后的，二人的申诉就是这样受理的。出口这一头，第 5 款对生效以后提出的一律不受理，不问施为发生在什么时候。入口宽，出口窄。这一不对称是这个例子的写法，本系列不主张它最好；它关上的那一段，照实登为余项，不写成还有路。

第三阶段也会落在门口。第六篇第三节写过一个设例：第 18 年春，沙洲安定队的一位指挥官带两名部下渡过灰河，向石峡邦守备队交出武器，申请庇护；营地里的人认出他，说第 16 年春烧村时带队的是他；沙洲的白垣政府与北部的临时议会都来索要。设若到第 18 年冬石峡邦退出生效时，他的庇护申请仍未决。照本节读，宪约第 25 条第 3 款说，退出不影响退出以前的施为所生的义务，也不影响已开始的程序；宪约第 4 条第（二）项不推回的承诺，此后不再以宪约约束石峡邦。可门口不推回本来不靠宪约，靠的是统一句：本国的拒入、遣返所及、又无从以退出避开的人，就这些施为而言在本国内部（国家论 N15）。第六篇第三节由此立的方向一，是越过了线的人到了门口也不推回（第六篇 XZ38）。退出不改变这一点。石峡邦此后怎样处理他，本篇不设。

向后止的是什么。 向后止的，是以成员身份新增或行使的权利；既已发生、依约存续的权利与程序，照原约。退出以后，不能再以参与方的身份启动约里新的程序。石峡邦的过渡期是一例。成员会议照约付清东坪营地第 19 年上半年的分担经费，二点四亿记账单位；第 4 号分担决定下的转送，做到第 19 年夏为止。这些是依约存续的，照原约走完。从第 18 年冬起，石峡邦不能再以成员身份提请发问、开启共同程序。

有人会说：退出就是不再受约，还要它为以前的事作答，等于不许它走。DRP 的陈述正是这个意思。这一说的分量在于，退出若带着无穷的旧账，走就只剩一个名义。可是走与账是两件事。DRP 的退出照期生效，体系没有拦它。二人一件照结；这一件提在生效以前，照第 14 条第 4、5 款，DRP 对它仍须在六个月内书面答复。它不答，体系记下来。体系强制不了它答，这是这个体系的代价，不是销账（第八篇 XZ54）。

还有人会说：约不许新开程序，就等于销了账。也不是。关上的是体系内的那一条路；账还在记录里，在平级者的问里，也在它将来要回来的时候（第四节）。

这里留下一处。二人此后的问经什么路？七件被退回的申诉的申诉人也一样。在体系里，那条路已经关上；剩下的是 DRP 内部的通道与平级者的问。本篇照实登为余项，不写成已经有路。

XZ47　退出不销账。退出以前的施为照记，已开始的程序照结，平级者之间的追问照旧（国家论 N38）。过去的未尽不因关闭追问而销账（制度论 Z35a）；退出终止的是现行的应答负荷，不自动清掉此前的履行事实（F6 §3.3）；共同的资源与程序义务，离开也不结清，只能转入安排或仲裁（冲突论 §25，原说两条第一人称的法分开以后，推到成员退出，自担）。样例里"退出后不再受约束"只说向后的约束力：约束力往后止，以前的账不销。分三阶段（自担）：（一）成员期间的施为照记；（二）已开始的程序照结；（三）对成员期间的施为，退出以后能不能开启新的程序，照已认的约：约许的照约；约不许的，体系内那一条路关上，但平级者的问与别的通道照旧，账不销。向后止的，是以成员身份新增或行使的权利；既已发生、依约存续的权利与程序，照原约；退出以后，不能再以参与方的身份启动约里新的程序。约不许新开程序时关上的那一段，照实登为余项，不写成还有路。

第四节　走得开，回来要同意

走得开，还回得来吗？石峡邦后来要回来。这一次它来敲的，是一扇它自己关上的门。

在通知期里，第 18 年夏，石峡邦与高原联邦签了《灯岛协定》。高原联邦六十日内撤出灯岛驻军，管理所关闭，灯岛此后不驻军；镜海舰队经南航道"提前二十四小时通知，免于登记"；高原联邦租用峡口港的一处补给码头，二十五年；"石峡邦不参加针对高原联邦的任何多边经济措施"；高原联邦撤销它对石峡邦断燃料、加关税、停结算的那道第 7 号令。邦议院以 124 票对 56 票批准。第 18 年秋，灯岛的驻军撤出，管理所关闭；迁出的约一千七百人，到第 19 年约一千一百人回了岛。

第 20 年春，石峡邦邦议院通过不信任案，提前选举，新的多数选出新的邦首席。同年秋，石峡邦申请再加入。宪约第 26 条写的是：再加入照第 7 条（甲）作新的接纳，各成员可以提出条件；第 7 条（甲）要的是全体成员书面同意。讨论中，UCSE 常驻代表提出三项：《灯岛协定》里"不参加任何多边经济措施"一条怎样处理；退出期间没有作答的两件申诉；峡口港的补给码头与联合防御的关系。NUT 常驻代表说："条件要写明，以施为为准。"DRP 常驻代表说："不应另设条件；走是权利，回来也是。"石峡邦的代表说："《灯岛协定》是与高原联邦订的，石峡邦不能单方删改；石峡邦愿意对今后每一项共同措施逐项说明是否参加，并答复那两件申诉。"高原联邦外交部声明："石峡邦回到那个体系，就是违反《灯岛协定》。"到第 20 年冬，事情未决。

这件事落在谁身上，第一节已经写过。到了再加入，要看的线多了下面说的两条底；在此之前，也多了一条宪约第 26 条。

回来是一项新约。 立新的约束，要每一方同意（第一篇 XZ1）；再加入也是新的约束，各方可以设条件。这是制约，不是强力。一个成员不同意接纳石峡邦，并不替石峡邦就它自己的事作决定，只是不与它订这一份新约。条件可以设，可设条件不等于什么都能设。本系列只立两条底。一条，再加入不洗账：退出期间的施为照记、照问。另一条，不能带着正在进行的殖民回来。底线以上的条件，交各方的政治家。

不对一国名设永久禁入。一国从前做过什么，账照记；今天能不能进来，看它今天的施为。国家论说一国此后的最高规则正当与否，照当下核，「不由来历决定」（国家论 N34）。原句说占领以后对方的规则，这里推到再加入的门口（自担）。初次加入时带着正在进行的越线来申请的一国，以及观察成员的席位，第一篇第九节写过（XZ7），这里不另写。

照两条底读石峡邦的申请。 事实指向三处。

其一，两件申诉落在第一条底上。石峡邦在通知期里没有作答，第 19 年的意见出来以后也没有答复；账记在它名下，不因再加入而消失。它说愿意答复，这是它对账的回应。要它先答复才接纳，还是接纳以后再答，是底线以上的条件，交政治家。

其二，《灯岛协定》里的那一条，是一份体系外的约跟着成员进出体系。照第四篇第五节的第一处读：这一条要石峡邦事先许下，对今后任何针对高原联邦的共同措施一概不参加，是把"参不参加"事先定死，许给了约的另一方，此后要参加哪一项，先得违约或得对方同意；它与 NUT 那份协定里被删去的第 14 条同形，事实指向取消中断权的一边。石峡邦说愿意对每一项共同措施逐项说明是否参加，走的是另一条路，这条路宪约第 7 条（丙）本来就有：不参加某项措施的成员，公开说明理由，理由载入记录。NUT 删去第 14 条以后走的就是这一条（第一篇第六节），它的常驻代表说过两条路差在哪里："第 14 条是对高原联邦许下的；不参加那一项，是对成员会议说明的。"这一条能不能成为再加入的条件、怎样处理，由再加入时表决的各成员判断，账落在提条件与表决的位置与人上。

其三，补给码头与联合防御的关系，在底线以上，交政治家。

在这件事里作判断的，是各成员。每一成员同不同意、提什么条件，都是它自己的施为，账落在提条件与表决的位置与人上。高原联邦的声明进到了这一过程里，它在体系之外，它的约与声明怎样读，照第四篇第五节核。

初次加入也是这样。 第 14 年春石峡邦第一次申请时，接纳同样要全体书面同意。DRP 推迟同意五个月；其间高原联邦发声明反对，联邦结算局致函石峡邦的三家银行说要对它们的结算作"合规审查"，高原联邦又与 DRP 签了一份比上一年市价低一成五的化肥长约（第一篇）。初次加入与再加入，都落在各成员的同意上，体系外一国的约与声明两次都进来了。两处都照第一篇 XZ1 与第四篇 XZ25 核，不另立一说。第二条底的设例，在下一节。

DRP 说："走是权利，回来也是。"这一说的分量在于对称：走既不要人同意，回来似乎也不该要。可是两件事不对称。走是本国自己的构造改动，在本国内部受问，不要别人同意（第一节）；回来是一项新约，是彼此的事，要别人同意。不对称，正因为一件是自己的事，一件是彼此的事。法学系列说：「退出不是法的失败，是法的存在条件变化时法自然退场和重建的正常过程。」（法学第三篇 §6.1）走与回来，读作法在条件变了时的退场与重建。重建的那一份约，了结它要了结的那一件，不改写走了的那几年：「共识不追认既往。它了结的是此刻这一件不相容，不改写此前任何一方做过什么。」（冲突论 §24）冲突论原说两条法之间的共识，这里推到再加入的约（自担）。

还有人会说：设条件，就是拿再加入压人。条件写明、以施为与账为准、不针对政体与国名的，不是压人。要对方改它不越线的自主选择的，才要照第四篇 XZ25 的第二处核。

照三层分：两条底是规范层。一项施为算不算正在进行的殖民，照施为认，边界上交位置上的判断。宪约第 26 条许各成员提条件，是规则层的例子。具体提什么、接不接纳，在判断层，账落在提条件与表决的位置与人。

这里留下两处：底线以上的条件，刻度在哪里，本篇不定；石峡邦的申请到第 20 年冬未决。

XZ48　走得开，回来要同意。再加入是一项新约，照约须各方同意（第一篇 XZ1），各方可以设条件；这是制约，不是强力：不同意不替对方就它自己的事作决定，只是不与它订新约。两条底：再加入不洗账，退出期间的施为照记、照问；不能带着正在进行的殖民回来。不对一国名设永久禁入：判在施为，不由来历决定（国家论 N34，占领推到再加入，自担）。底线以上的条件写明、以施为与账为准，不针对政体与国名，由各方的政治家定；要对方改它不越线的自主选择的，照第四篇 XZ25 核。再加入的约了结它要了结的那一件，不改写退出期间的施为（冲突论 §24，自担）。初次加入时正在进行的越线与观察成员席位，见第一篇 XZ7。

第五节　先退出，再殖民

一个成员先退出，再以武力进入别国，再回来申请。这一串，体系怎样读？已发生的事里没有这样一件。下面是一个设例，与上文的事分开写。

灰河自南向北流入镜海，下游约一百四十公里是沙洲共和国与石峡邦的界河，沙洲在西岸，石峡邦在东岸。第 18 年春起，在沙口召开的共和国临时议会控制了沙口省、北滩省与西洲省北半，白垣政府在西洲省南半继续作战。设若石峡邦退出生效以后，第 19 年秋，石峡邦守备队约一千人渡过灰河，进入西洲省北半沿河的一处渡口地带，约三十平方公里，设"渡口管理所"，收渡口费，登记过往的人。北部的临时议会抗议。第 20 年秋，石峡邦带着这处管理所申请再加入。

这一设例落在渡口地带里的人与过往的人身上，收费与登记都落在他们身上。要看的线有三条：殖民性的进入，即以强力进入别国、替那里的人做主；殖民的来处（第五篇 XZ29）；再加入的第二条底，上一节已写。在此之前，石峡邦的守备队在灰河东岸，它的退出第 18 年冬已经生效。

第一步，看来处。 进入发生在石峡邦不是成员的时候，不算来自体系。体系的联合防御按殖民的来处分，可用于体系外对成员、成员对成员、成员对体系外三格（第五篇第三节）；这一进入对体系是体系外对体系外，不在三格之内，联合防御不管。不管，不等于什么都不做：发问、记录、制裁、接住出走的人，这些间接的方式照开着；用到哪里，交位置上的政治家。为什么不往回追？往回追，凡曾是成员的国家，此后做的事都永远"来自体系"；那样走就没有走成，第一节说的走得开就落了空。

第二步，看账。 退出以前在体系里预备的、借用过体系安排的部分，若有，账照记。守备队这一次进入用没用过体系的联合计划，没有记载。有记载的是一件已经发生的事：峡口港的预置后勤仓库，石峡邦请联合防御的协调处在退出生效以前移走，第 18 年冬已经移走。本节只立方向，不判有无。

第三步，看底线。 第 20 年秋带着管理所申请再加入，落在"不能带着正在进行的殖民回来"这一条底上。管理所在，底线就挡在这里，不交政治家去衡量；撤出、账落了地以后，回来的条件才在底线以上（第四节）。这一进入是不是殖民性的进入，照施为认：以强力进入，设管理所，收渡口费，登记过往的人；边界上怎样认，交位置上的判断。宪约第 4 条第（一）项是成员的承诺：不以武力进入任何国家的领土、领海，不在别国境内设立管理。石峡邦退出以后不受它约束；要回来，就要重认它。

第四步，看漏洞有多大。 一国先退出、再动手，体系的联合防御就不管它，这看起来是个漏洞。它由第五篇第五节收小（XZ30）：不论在三格中的哪一格，每一参与国出兵，都要它自己的第一面成立，即它自身的涵育正在被侵蚀，而这侵蚀与被守一方所受的压迫同出一源（战争论 §13 的闸门四）；成员在与不在，差的只是联合计划、预置后勤一类事先的协调与预备。成员身份本来就不自动给谁开战的理由。外沙那件事可以对照，第六节写：那一进入发生在成员期间，所以开除以后仍算来自体系。两处都按殖民发生时认。

有人会说：一国先退出再动手，体系就管不了，这是漏洞。这一说的分量是真的，那一点事先的协调与预备确实没有了。可是漏洞已被收小；那一国回不来；间接的方式照开着；退出以前借用的账照记。反过来也有人说，应当往回追，凡在体系里待过的，走了也算。第一步已答：往回追，走就落了空。

这里留下两处：间接的方式用到哪里，交位置上的政治家；退出以前的准备借没借体系的安排，在这一设例里没有记载。

XZ49　先退出再殖民，不算来自体系；不能退出、殖民、再加入。联合防御按殖民发生时是否成员认，不往回追；往回追，走得开就落了空（XZ45）。退出以前在体系里准备、借用体系安排的部分，账照记。退出以后开始的殖民，对体系是体系外对体系外，联合防御不管，间接的方式照开着（发问、记录、制裁、接住出走的人），用到哪里交位置上的政治家。带着正在进行的殖民申请再加入，挡在再加入的第二条底上（XZ48），不交政治家。这一处的漏洞由第五篇 XZ30 收小：不论出兵去挡哪一处的殖民性进入，都要出兵国自己的第一面成立，成员在与不在，只差事先的协调与预备。

第六节　开除

体系能不能把一个成员开除出去？国家论对人立过一句：「不开除：国家不以判外作惩罚或清除」（国家论 N14）；判外，是把人判到成员资格之外。那是国家对它的人说的。一个协作的框架能不能开除它的成员国，上游没有立过。外沙的事，把这一问摆到了体系面前。

外沙群岛是沙洲共和国的岛群，在沙口以北约六十公里的镜海中，岛上约二千三百人，以渔业为主。第 17 年夏，沙洲把群岛的守备撤到北部陆上，岛上的官员多随守备离开。第 17 年秋，UCSE 执政院会议以 6 比 3 决定部署，两艘舰艇与一个连在外沙大岛登岸，UCSE 的防卫署设"外沙临时管理处"。管理处给居民登记，发"外沙居住证"；向在外沙海域捕鱼的船收渔业费；挑选七位居民组成咨询会；把岛上小学的沙洲课本换成在河口城印的"临时课本"；夜里宵禁。一群渔民在码头抗议，标语写"岛上的事岛上的人定"，三人被管理处留置两日。

成员会议第 18 年春作第一次记录："UCSE 以军力进入体系外一国的外沙群岛，设立临时管理处，发放居住证，收取渔业费，更换学校课本。"表决 3 比 0，UCSE 不参加表决。第 18 年夏，成员会议依宪约第 18 条第 1 款作第二次记录："有一项殖民性的进入。"NUT、石峡邦赞成，DRP 弃权。同年夏，DRP 提议暂停 UCSE 在成员会议的表决权。NUT 反对，常驻代表说："暂停它的票，管理处不会因此撤走。NUT 要它坐在这里答。"石峡邦弃权，提议没有通过。这一段经过，第四篇第七节与第五篇已写过。

宪约第 27 条管暂停与开除。暂停一个成员在成员会议的表决权，要其他成员四分之三同意。开除要三项都具备：成员会议已就该成员的同一施为作出至少两次记录；自第一次记录起六个月内，它没有停止那一施为；其他成员全体同意，决定写明理由。第 4 款："暂停与开除不免除该成员此前施为所生的义务。"这一条与宪约的其他条文一样是例子，还要点明它与本系列的方向不一致的两处：它的暂停只停表决权，比本系列给暂停的方向窄（第四篇 XZ27）；它的开除条件不限于越线，比本系列给开除理由的方向宽。

到第 18 年秋，外沙已有第 18 年春、夏两次记录，管理处没有撤，前两项已经具备。DRP 提议开除 UCSE。DRP 常驻代表说："宪约第 4 条第（一）项是这份约的第一条承诺。第 13 年 UCSE 复函共和国时提到的，也是这一项。"NUT 常驻代表说："开除以后，它在外沙做的事就与体系无关了吗？它是在体系里的时候进去的。"又说："开除是把它推到体系外面；外面没有人能问它了。"石峡邦常驻代表说："第 27 条第 4 款写着开除不免除此前施为所生的义务。开除免掉的是什么，留下的是什么，宪约没有写。"UCSE 常驻代表说："开除一个成员，体系就成了它说自己不是的那种东西。"表决时，UCSE 以外的成员有三个：DRP 赞成，NUT 反对，石峡邦弃权。开除要全体同意，没有通过。沙口的临时议会致函秘书长，请求开除；白垣政府声明："体系开不开除它自己的成员，与共和国无关；外沙是共和国的。"第 19 年春，UCSE 宣布管理处"逐步把民政交给咨询会"。此后到第 20 年，没有成员再提开除，管理处仍在。

这件事落在外沙岛上的约二千三百人身上，被留置的三位渔民在内；也落在此后 UCSE 施为所及、可以依宪约第 14 条申诉 UCSE 的人身上。要看的线有三条：体系对成员能用的手段，第四篇 XZ27 写过；五条应当中的第五条，即这些人的申诉之路；殖民的来处（第五篇 XZ29）。在此之前，有宪约第 27 条与它的第 4 款，有第 18 年春、夏的两次记录。

开除是什么。 本系列说的开除，是对成员国这一构的，指它的成员资格终止。人不随它出局：UCSE 的人、外沙岛上的人，都不因 UCSE 被开除而被开除。开除与暂停一样，是收回配合：体系不再与它在共同程序里共事，不替它就它自己的事作决定。外沙的事仍由 UCSE 决定，它承担代价而不从的余地仍在。照强力的判据，开除替代不了 UCSE 的判断，它不是强力（第四篇 XZ27 的读法）。

体系可以开除成员，条件要严；严到哪里，不是哲学答得了的，交政治家。哲学能给的，是理由的方向与两条底。

理由的方向是越线。 越线有两种：殖民别国；对本国的人越线，如屠杀、酷刑、不经审判的拘押、大规模驱逐。后一种以成员已认的约为据，针对施为，不针对政体：政变本身不伴越线的，不是理由；对那一国采取的措施，人道的部分要切出来。越线以外的理由，例如长期不履约，怎样处理不由哲学定，交政治家，照下面两条底。外沙这一件，理由是殖民性的进入，在方向之内。第 27 条的三项条件里，前两项看施为，后一项看票：开除是对施为作出、经票落地的决定。这一次票没有过，作出判断的是三个成员，各自的话都在记录里。《制度如何可能》附录一的样例宪章，以出席成员三分之二可以开除成员，那也只是样例（F6 附录一第 10 条(b)，工作展示，只作样例）。

两条底。 一条，开除不销账。第 27 条第 4 款一类的规定，说的就是这个。《制度如何可能》讲清除时说：「所以清除只能在各相之间挪账，清不空总账。」（F6 §2.1 注）原句说对人的清除，这里推到把一个成员国移出体系：账只是换了地方，总账还在（自担）。另一条，开除不改变殖民的来处。开除以前开始的殖民，开除以后仍算来自体系，联合防御照可用（第五篇 XZ29）。NUT 那句"它是在体系里的时候进去的"，说的就是这一条。第五节的设例正好相反：那里的进入发生在退出以后，不算来自体系。两处都按殖民发生时认。

开除的代价，分三层写。 规范层：被保障的人不随开除出局，指三件。开除以前已提出的申诉照结，这是第三节的第二阶段。体系此后的施为所及的人，例如体系对被开除一国的制裁、通报所及的人，照统一句（体系一级，第二篇第六节）有路可问。问与记照旧：体系与平级者对体系外的一国，照样可以问、可以记（第二篇 XZ11）。

代价也写在这一层：那一国施为所及的人（它自己的人，以及在它施为之下的体系外的人）对那一国此后施为的申诉之路，在体系里关上。这一段照实登为余项；它也是开除的条件要严的理由之一。

规则层：宪约第 14 条按受其影响的人划申诉资格，不以籍划，是一个例子；开除以后这条路对此后的施为关上，是这个例子的后果。判断层：开不开除，要把这笔代价算进去，交政治家。

外沙的三位渔民，让这笔代价有了名字。他们被留置以后，托河口城的一位律师向 UCSE 的监察署投诉留置与宵禁。监察署答复：投诉人不是 UCSE 籍，也不在 UCSE 境内合法居留，不在受理之列。宪约第 14 条成了剩下的一条路。第 18 年秋，他们依第 14 条第 2 款向申诉委员会申诉；持 UCSE 籍的委员不参加审议。第 19 年春，委员会决定受理，理由是："第 2 款说的是受其影响的人，不以籍划；监察署不受理，申诉人在 UCSE 国内没有别的可用的救济。"到第 20 年，委员会仍在审理。开除若在那时通过，这件提在开除以前的申诉照结；可此后管理处再对岛上的人做什么，岛上的人在体系里就没有路了。

由此可以答石峡邦那一问。开除免掉的，是 UCSE 在共同程序里的席位，以及它此后的施为在体系里的那一条申诉之路；留下的，是此前的账，是已提出的申诉，是体系与各国对它的问与记。NUT 说"外面没有人能问它了"，说过了头：体系与平级者对体系外的一国照样可以问、可以记，关上的是第 14 条那一条路。NUT 在暂停一事上要 UCSE"坐在这里答"，正是这笔代价的样子：留在里面，它就还在那条路之下。

还有一种情形要说一句。成员分裂成两部分，体系认可其中一部分，"开除"另一部分；这读作不认那一部分承接原成员的成员资格。已发生的事里没有成员分裂，这一种情形判在第五篇第十一节（XZ34），上面两条底照用。

照国家论 N36 的四件写开除这一条通道。发动的，是提议的成员与表决的各位置。对象，是成员资格的终止与它的理由。提议与表决的一方关不上指向开除的问：被提议开除的一方可以在每一阶段陈述，宪约第 7 条（乙）对被问的成员就是这样写的；决定要写明理由；平级者与受影响的人照问。失灵时，账记在提议与表决的位置与人上（国家论 N39）。

UCSE 说："开除一个成员，体系就成了它说自己不是的那种东西。"这一说的分量在于，开除看起来像是体系在成员之上下了一道判决。可是开除不取得对 UCSE 的强力：外沙的事仍由 UCSE 决定，撤还是留，体系替不了它；体系只是不再与它在共同程序里共事。要防的是另一件事：开除成为影响力大的一方清除异议成员的工具。所以开除要针对施为，条件从严，本身可被追问。判在施为：施为的一方是谁，不改变这一判。这里被提议开除的是 UCSE，上面的读法一字不改。

这里留下的：严到哪里，交政治家。外沙这一件，开除与暂停都没有通过；到第 20 年管理处仍在，体系手里剩下的是问、记与各成员各自的配合。代价照写。

XZ50　开除：可以，条件从严，不销账，不改变殖民的来处。体系可以开除成员；严到哪里，不由哲学定，交政治家。开除指成员资格的终止，对的是成员国这一构，人不随它出局；国家论对人立了不开除（国家论 N14），对一个框架开除成员国没有立。开除与暂停一样是收回配合，不取得对被开除一方的强力（第四篇 XZ27）。理由的方向是越线：殖民别国，或对本国的人越线（屠杀、酷刑、不经审判的拘押、大规模驱逐）；后一种以成员已认的约为据，针对施为，不针对政体，政变本身不伴越线的不是理由，人道部分切出。越线以外的理由（如长期不履约），交政治家，照两条底。两条底：开除不销账，把成员移出去，账只是换了地方（F6 §2.1 注，自担）；开除前开始的殖民，开除后仍算来自体系，联合防御照可用（第五篇 XZ29）。开除本身是施为，可被追问：被提议开除的一方在每一阶段可以陈述，决定写明理由，账落在提议与表决的位置与人（国家论 N39）；它针对施为，不针对国名或偏向，免得开除成为影响力大的一方清除异议成员的工具。分三层：规范层，被保障的人不随开除出局，指三件：开除前已提出的申诉照结（XZ47 第二阶段）；体系此后的施为所及的人，照统一句（体系一级，第二篇第六节）有路可问；问与记照旧。那一国施为所及的人（它自己的人，以及在它施为之下的体系外的人）对那一国此后施为的申诉之路，在体系里关上；这是开除的代价，照实登余项，也是条件从严的理由之一。规则层，开除的条款与按受其影响的人划申诉资格的条款，是例子。判断层，开不开除，把这一代价算进去，交政治家。成员分裂时"开除另一部分"，读作不认它承接那一成员的成员资格（第五篇 XZ34）。

第七节　解散

体系自己也可能散。各成员都不想要它了，或者它已经做不成它要做的事。那时，以前的账交给谁？

体系解散，要尽量做好后续安排。安排至少包括三项：未结的事项，申诉与程序，去哪里；记录按事项交相关的成员国保管；每一项施为的账，落到投票与执行它的国家与位置上（国家论 N39）。"尽量"两字要读准：安排不到的，登为余项，不宣布已无亏欠。国家论说：「成员资格的每一次划分，取得、放弃、撤销、收纳与分离，都留下它自己的亏欠」，又说「重划对象域、走完程序，都不销账。」（国家论 N17）解散也是一次划分，只是划掉的是整个体系；那一句由一国之内的成员资格推到体系的解散（自担）。

第一步，解散是各成员的施为。 宪约第 30 条要成员会议全体同意，并要在解散以前订后续安排：未结申诉的去处，档案按事项交相关成员保管，分担的账目清结。这一条也是例子。要解散而别人不同意的一方，走的路是退出（第一节）。拆除一个构，也是这个构的施为：「拆除也是构的施为：拆除本身不得越线」（制度论 Z38）。原句说一国之内的构，这里推到体系（自担）。

第二步，解散不销账。 约束力往后止，以前的账不销，与退出同理（第三节）。《制度如何可能》的样例宪章写过：「解散后，本宪章对所有前成员文明不再具有约束力。」（F6 附录一第 19 条，工作展示，只作样例）这一句说的也只是向后。体系没了，账落到哪里？制度论说：「构是归责地址，不是施为的主体；归责地址与实际实施者不互相遮蔽。」（制度论 Z7a）体系是一个地址。地址撤了，账就落到在这个地址下投票与执行的成员国与位置上。政府论说：「"最终的承担者"若指一处把账收齐、把门关上的位置，这个系列没有它；若指每一笔账都落得到一个说得出的位置，有」（政府论 G36）。共同的资源与程序义务，离开也不结清，只能转入安排（冲突论 §25）。冲突论原说两方分开，这里推到体系的解散（自担）。

第三步，安排不到的照实登。 体系解散的事没有发生过。一个成员退出时的过渡期，可以当一个小样来看。石峡邦退出生效以后的六个月里，分担的账照约清结，两件申诉照常审结，第 4 号分担决定下的转送做到第 19 年夏为止。东坪营地当时约有二十一万人。分担在过渡期满时结束；第 19 年秋，UCSE 与石峡邦另订双边协定，再接收四千人。二十一万与四千之间，就是"尽量"落不到的样子。它要照实登为余项，不写成已经安排好。体系若解散，体系施为所及的人，例如未结申诉的申诉人、分担与转送中的人、记录里写到的人，照统一句（体系一级，第二篇第六节）都在这一安排之内。

照四件写解散这一处。发动的，是各成员，要全体同意。对象，是解散与后续安排。被问的一方关不上指向它的问：解散本身是施为，未结的申诉、转送中的人怎样落，是它要答的。失灵时，账落在投票与执行的成员国与位置上，安排不到的登为余项。

有人会说：体系解散了，就没有地址，账记给谁？第二步已经答了：地址撤了，账落到投票与执行的成员国与位置上。也有人会说：解散是各国的自由，不必安排。这一说的分量在于，没有谁能逼一群国家把一个它们都不要的体系维持下去。这一点本篇认下。可是拆除也是施为，本身在问之下；解散的自由，不使未结的事一笔勾销。

照三层分："至少包括"的三项与"不宣布已无亏欠"，是规范层；宪约第 30 条是规则层的例子；每一项怎样安排、交给哪一国，交位置上的判断。

这里留下的：安排不到的，一律登为余项。

XZ51　体系解散，尽量做好后续安排。后续安排至少包括：未结事项（申诉、程序）的去处；记录按事项交相关成员国保管；每一项施为的账落到投票与执行它的国家与位置（国家论 N39）。"尽量"：安排不到的登为余项，不宣布已无亏欠（国家论 N17，自担）。拆除也是构的施为，拆除本身不得越线（制度论 Z38，自担）。解散只止向后的约束力，以前的账不销；共同的资源与程序义务，离开也不结清，只能转入安排（冲突论 §25，推到体系的解散，自担）。安排不到的，程序里写明，照实登为余项。

第八节　与康德

这一篇问的是走与回来，问到底是一个老问题：各国之间能不能有一种作数的法的状态，它不靠一个在上的强制，各国在其中又始终是自由的。接得最直接的是康德。

康德在《永久和平论》附录二讲政治与道德的一致，第 2 点讲国际法。他说，国际法要以某种法的状态为前提，这一状态必出自某种契约；这契约不必像建立国家的那一种那样以强制法为基础，「至多也可以是一种持续自由的结合，如上文提到的各国的联邦」（allenfalls auch der einer fortwährend-freyen Association seyn kann, wie der oben erwähnte der Föderalität verschiedener Staaten；《永久和平论》附录二第 2 点，据 1795 年 Nicolovius 初版，Project Gutenberg 电子本 #46873；对照 M. C. Smith 英译，电子本 #50922，A 档）。同一处他又说，这契约结合各国，「只为让它们彼此之间、并共同对别国保持和平，决不是为了有所取得」（daß der Vertrag die Staaten nur in der Absicht verbindet, unter einander und zusammen gegen andere Staaten sich im Frieden zu erhalten, keinesweges aber um Erwerbungen zu machen；同上，同点）。附录二末了，他把这一点收成一句：只以消除战争为目的的各国联邦状态，是唯一与各国的自由相容的法的状态（同上，附录二末段，转述）。

共同的问题。 康德问的是，国际法所需的法的状态从哪里来。这一篇问的是，一个协作体系里，走与回来怎样安排，以前的账怎样算。看起来不是一题。两题在一处相交：各国之间能不能有一种作数的结合，它不以强制法为基础，各国在其中始终是自由的。在这一处，是同题。

同在哪里。 国际法所需的法的状态，可以出自一种不以强制法为基础、持续自由的结合：这一点两家相同。这个系列给体系下的定义，就是没有、也不立带强力的上级；体系能做的是问、记、收回配合（第二节）。

分在哪里。 康德说"持续自由"，没有把它拆开说。本系列把它落在三件事上：退出走得开；回来要别人同意；走了以后，以前的账不销。石峡邦的事一一对得上。它通知退出，体系作了记录，没有阻拦（第一节）。它申请再加入，那是一份新约，到第 20 年冬未决（第四节）。它在体系里时的两件申诉照常审结，过渡期里分担照约清结（第三节）。DRP 那件事再添上一项：约不许新开的程序，体系里那一条路关上了，账不销（第三节）。康德的原文没有把这三件分开说。

本系列将这一句读作：结合本身不靠强制维持；"持续"说的是结合在自由中延续，不是说成员的施为可以免问。走、回、账三处，是本系列在这一读法上加的，不算在康德名下。

替康德拟一个问题。 既然持续自由的结合仍由契约联结，怎样使既有的义务不随退出被抹掉，同时又不让一项未结的事变成永远不许退出的理由？一个头上没有带强力上级的共同程序，怎样把这两面都写得可用？

本系列的答法分三处。退出不由对方否决：走是本国自己的构造改动，在本国内部受问（第一节）。再加入要各方同意：结合是彼此的，一方的自由，不能变成强进另一方的门；走不要人同意，回来要人同意，两样合起来才是两边都自由（第四节）。以前的施为不免账：账是以前的施为，不是今后的约束；《制度如何可能》讲连续性，说退出「不自动清掉此前的履行事实」（F6 §3.3）。账留着，未结的事项却不使退出无限期不生效（第一节）。这里不是康德要自由而不算旧账，本系列才把账算进去；康德那里，结合本来就出自契约。本系列在这一共同的困难上，把成员期间、已开始的程序、退出以后新开的程序分开（第三节），并写明再加入要哪些同意。这是展开，不宣称驳倒康德。

这一节不往两处扩。同一点里"只为保持和平"那半句，本系列以反殖民为主线，不担保和平，这一分处前置篇已写，这里不再对。公开性的检验，国家论第五篇第四节已对过，这里也不再对。

这一篇停在哪里

这一篇证了这些。退出或改接一项外部安排，是本国的构造改动，在本国内部受问，不由正在被问的位置独定；体系能审查、能记录、能在约里要求通知期与过渡期，不能否决，通知期不得被用成锁，固定的终点只说明它不能由被退出的一方独自延长。凡事实上取得对成员强力的安排，就是上级，锁住退出用的是同一判据；一条规则把审查它自己废止的权限交给外部时，分两步答，国家仍在国家这一端。退出不销账，分三阶段：成员期间的施为照记，已开始的程序照结，退出以后能不能开启新的程序照已认的约。走得开，回来要同意，再加入有两条底。先退出再殖民，不算来自体系；不能退出、殖民、再加入。体系可以开除成员，条件从严，开除不销账，不改变殖民的来处。体系解散，尽量做好后续安排。

它没有证明的：通知期与过渡期多长；再加入、开除、暂停的具体条件，这些交政治家；条约法上退出程序的细则。

它留下的余项：一个成员内部的退出由哪些位置作出才合它自己的法，DRP 那一次登在这里；约不许新开程序时关上的那一段，DRP 那二人与七件被退回的申诉都在其中；开除关上的那一条申诉之路；解散时安排不到的。这些照实登为余项，不写成已经有路。石峡邦的再加入到第 20 年冬未决，外沙的管理处到第 20 年仍在，事情记到这里为止。

交给第八篇：走得开、回来要同意、账不销之后，剩下的问是：每一项安排是否都还在问之下。

本篇命题（合列）

XZ45　退出在内部受问；体系能审查，不能否决。退出或改接一项外部安排，是本国的一项构造改动（国家论 N24，两国推到多国，自担），在本国内部受问，不由正在被问的位置独定。体系可以审查、记录，可以在约里要求通知期与过渡期，不能否决；通知期让退出不被拿来躲开正在进行的问，它不得被用成锁，固定的终点只说明它不能由被退出的一方独自延长。外部审查一项退出，是外部追问的一种，本国取消不了，所以体系能审查（第二篇 XZ11）；能拦住退出的只有强力，所以体系不能否决（XZ46）。约可以给退出设条件（通知期、清结）；条件没有满足时，账留着，照记、照问，但未结的事项不使退出无限期不生效（国家论 N16，分离推到成员退出，自担）；约里没有退出条款的，体系也不能以"不承认"代替否决。通知期与过渡期照政府论 G39 核：被退出的一方不能以定价或审批独自取消退出；实际负担、既有条件与能不能转入可审查的程序，还须分别核。对退出中的成员，冻结只为账，不作退出的条件（第四篇 XZ22）。退出不替追问（政府论 G27）。通知期与过渡期多长，交位置上的判断。

XZ46　凡事实上取得对成员强力的安排，就是上级；锁住退出是其一。强力，是用自己的判断力替代对方就它自己的事的判断；在这里被替代的是成员，那一件判断是成员此刻落不落实、怎样落实一项决定。照事先之约以多数作出的决定，经成员自己的法落地，体系只能问、记、收回配合，不是上级（第一篇 XZ1）；能越过成员自己的法强行落地的，才是。收回配合不替代判断（第四篇 XZ22）；从拨款里扣除是牵制，拿走成员自己的资本是强力。取得了强力的安排，照政府论对一级政府的要求作答（多权分立、内部主体的通道、可被追问），不能以"只是协作"躲开。锁住退出用的是同一判据：锁要靠强力，靠强力锁住的就是上级（法学第三篇 §6、权力论第六篇 §I、道德律第九篇 §4.2 推到体系对成员，自担）；不靠强力的"锁"，只是一项走得开、取消不了的外部追问，国家仍在国家这一端。一条规则把审查它自己废止的权限交给外部，分两步答：外部的审查是外部追问的一种，体系能审查、不能否决，成员把否决交给体系，体系也不收；成员若在自己的法里给自己的退出设了外部的同意，那是它自己的法，改它是一项构造改动，在本国内部受问（国家论 N24），外部拦不住，除非用强力，而带强力的已不在本系列的定义域里（自担）。有没有退出条款不是判据，判据是有没有、要不要靠强力留人。一项安排什么时候事实上取得了强力，边界交位置上的判断，同处写明替代的是哪一方、哪一件判断。

XZ47　退出不销账。退出以前的施为照记，已开始的程序照结，平级者之间的追问照旧（国家论 N38）。过去的未尽不因关闭追问而销账（制度论 Z35a）；退出终止的是现行的应答负荷，不自动清掉此前的履行事实（F6 §3.3）；共同的资源与程序义务，离开也不结清，只能转入安排或仲裁（冲突论 §25，原说两条第一人称的法分开以后，推到成员退出，自担）。样例里"退出后不再受约束"只说向后的约束力：约束力往后止，以前的账不销。分三阶段（自担）：（一）成员期间的施为照记；（二）已开始的程序照结；（三）对成员期间的施为，退出以后能不能开启新的程序，照已认的约：约许的照约；约不许的，体系内那一条路关上，但平级者的问与别的通道照旧，账不销。向后止的，是以成员身份新增或行使的权利；既已发生、依约存续的权利与程序，照原约；退出以后，不能再以参与方的身份启动约里新的程序。约不许新开程序时关上的那一段，照实登为余项，不写成还有路。

XZ48　走得开，回来要同意。再加入是一项新约，照约须各方同意（第一篇 XZ1），各方可以设条件；这是制约，不是强力：不同意不替对方就它自己的事作决定，只是不与它订新约。两条底：再加入不洗账，退出期间的施为照记、照问；不能带着正在进行的殖民回来。不对一国名设永久禁入：判在施为，不由来历决定（国家论 N34，占领推到再加入，自担）。底线以上的条件写明、以施为与账为准，不针对政体与国名，由各方的政治家定；要对方改它不越线的自主选择的，照第四篇 XZ25 核。再加入的约了结它要了结的那一件，不改写退出期间的施为（冲突论 §24，自担）。初次加入时正在进行的越线与观察成员席位，见第一篇 XZ7。

XZ49　先退出再殖民，不算来自体系；不能退出、殖民、再加入。联合防御按殖民发生时是否成员认，不往回追；往回追，走得开就落了空（XZ45）。退出以前在体系里准备、借用体系安排的部分，账照记。退出以后开始的殖民，对体系是体系外对体系外，联合防御不管，间接的方式照开着（发问、记录、制裁、接住出走的人），用到哪里交位置上的政治家。带着正在进行的殖民申请再加入，挡在再加入的第二条底上（XZ48），不交政治家。这一处的漏洞由第五篇 XZ30 收小：不论出兵去挡哪一处的殖民性进入，都要出兵国自己的第一面成立，成员在与不在，只差事先的协调与预备。

XZ50　开除：可以，条件从严，不销账，不改变殖民的来处。体系可以开除成员；严到哪里，不由哲学定，交政治家。开除指成员资格的终止，对的是成员国这一构，人不随它出局；国家论对人立了不开除（国家论 N14），对一个框架开除成员国没有立。开除与暂停一样是收回配合，不取得对被开除一方的强力（第四篇 XZ27）。理由的方向是越线：殖民别国，或对本国的人越线（屠杀、酷刑、不经审判的拘押、大规模驱逐）；后一种以成员已认的约为据，针对施为，不针对政体，政变本身不伴越线的不是理由，人道部分切出。越线以外的理由（如长期不履约），交政治家，照两条底。两条底：开除不销账，把成员移出去，账只是换了地方（F6 §2.1 注，自担）；开除前开始的殖民，开除后仍算来自体系，联合防御照可用（第五篇 XZ29）。开除本身是施为，可被追问：被提议开除的一方在每一阶段可以陈述，决定写明理由，账落在提议与表决的位置与人（国家论 N39）；它针对施为，不针对国名或偏向，免得开除成为影响力大的一方清除异议成员的工具。分三层：规范层，被保障的人不随开除出局，指三件：开除前已提出的申诉照结（XZ47 第二阶段）；体系此后的施为所及的人，照统一句（体系一级，第二篇第六节）有路可问；问与记照旧。那一国施为所及的人（它自己的人，以及在它施为之下的体系外的人）对那一国此后施为的申诉之路，在体系里关上；这是开除的代价，照实登余项，也是条件从严的理由之一。规则层，开除的条款与按受其影响的人划申诉资格的条款，是例子。判断层，开不开除，把这一代价算进去，交政治家。成员分裂时"开除另一部分"，读作不认它承接那一成员的成员资格（第五篇 XZ34）。

XZ51　体系解散，尽量做好后续安排。后续安排至少包括：未结事项（申诉、程序）的去处；记录按事项交相关成员国保管；每一项施为的账落到投票与执行它的国家与位置（国家论 N39）。"尽量"：安排不到的登为余项，不宣布已无亏欠（国家论 N17，自担）。拆除也是构的施为，拆除本身不得越线（制度论 Z38，自担）。解散只止向后的约束力，以前的账不销；共同的资源与程序义务，离开也不结清，只能转入安排（冲突论 §25，推到体系的解散，自担）。安排不到的，程序里写明，照实登为余项。
